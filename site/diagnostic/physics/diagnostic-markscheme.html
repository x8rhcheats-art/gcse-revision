Section A — Forces and Motion (8)

A1 (1) weight = mass × gravitational field strength, or W = m × g. Accept W = mg.

A2 (2)

  • a = (v − u) / t or (20 − 8) / 4.0 — (1)
  • 3.0 m/s² — (1)

A3 (2) Trap: distance given in cm, answer required in N m.

  • Converts 25 cm to 0.25 m — (1)
  • 40 × 0.25 = 10 N m — (1)
  • If he answers 1000 (didn't convert), code as U. This is the single most common error in his department's mark schemes.

A4 (3) Any three:

  • Initially weight is greater than air resistance, so there is a resultant force downwards and he accelerates — (1)
  • As speed increases, air resistance increases — (1)
  • When air resistance equals weight, the resultant force is zero — (1)
  • So he travels at constant (terminal) velocity — (1) max 3

Watch for: "gravity" instead of weight — his examiners do not accept it. "Wind resistance" is also rejected. Code as W. Watch for: any answer written in terms of energy rather than forces. Code as W and flag it — this cost marks on the 2025 paper.

Section B — Energy (8)

B1 (1) Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred/stored/dissipated. Accept "transferred from one store to another".

B2 (2)

  • efficiency = useful energy output / total energy input — (1)
  • 240/800 = 0.30 → 30% — (1)

Watch for: "useful energy / total energy" without the words output and input. His markers explicitly refused this. Code W.

B3 (2)

  • GPE = m × g × h or 3.0 × 10 × 2.5 — (1)
  • 75 J — (1)

B4 (3)

  • Gravitational potential (energy) store decreases / transfers to the kinetic store — (1)
  • Transfer is mechanical (by working) — (1)
  • Some energy is transferred to the thermal store of the surroundings, by air resistance / friction — (1)

Watch for: "energy is lost". Technically accepted by some markers but discourage it — dissipated or transferred to the surroundings is safer.

Section C — Waves and EM Spectrum (8)

C1 (1) wave speed = frequency × wavelength, or v = f × λ.

C2 (2)

  • λ = v / f or 340 / 425 — (1)
  • 0.80 m — (1)

C3 (2) below: infrared — (1); above: ultraviolet — (1)

C4 (a) (2)

  • Transverse: vibrations/oscillations are perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of energy transfer — (1)
  • Longitudinal: vibrations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer — (1)
  • Diagram showing both correctly can earn both marks.

C4 (b) (1) Damage to surface cells / skin cancer / blindness or eye damage. Any one.

Section D — Static Electricity (8)

D1 (a) (2)

  • Friction causes electrons to be transferred — (1)
  • Electrons move from the cloth to the rod / rod gains electrons — (1)

Watch for: any mention of protons moving. Protons do not move. Code K — this is a real misconception, not a wording slip.

D1 (b) (1) Positive.

D2 (1) Gold-leaf electroscope.

This is named in the spec and his department deducted a mark from anyone who couldn't name it. If he writes "electroscope" alone, give the mark but flag it.

D3 (3)

  • Fuel flowing through the pipe / hose causes charge to build up by friction — (1)
  • A spark may jump / discharge occurs, which could ignite the fuel or fuel vapour — (1)
  • Earthing the aircraft (bonding wire to the ground) allows the charge/electrons to flow to earth — (1)

Watch for: answers that stop at "it's earthed" without saying where the electrons go. His markers withheld the mark for this exact omission.

Section E — Electrical Circuits (8)

E1 (1) voltage = current × resistance, or V = I × R.

E2 (2)

  • V = I × R or 0.25 × 48 — (1)
  • 12 V — (1)

E3 (2)

  • Q = I × t or 0.40 × 90 — (1)
  • 36 C — (1)

E4 (3) This is the discriminator question in this section.

  • Recognises that voltage across each resistor is 6.0 V (parallel) — (1)
  • I₁ = 6/20 = 0.30 A and I₂ = 6/30 = 0.20 A — (1)
  • Total current = 0.50 A — (1)

Watch for: adding the resistances to get 50 Ω and dividing (giving 0.12 A). That's treating parallel as series — a genuine knowledge gap. Code K.

Section F — Solids, Liquids and Gases (8)

F1 (2)

  • density = mass / volume or 240 / 30 — (1)
  • 8.0 g/cm³ with the unit — (1)

If the number is right but the unit missing, code P.

F2 (1) 300 K.

F3 (2)

  • ΔQ = m × c × ΔT or 0.50 × 4200 × 20 — (1)
  • 42 000 J — (1)

Standard form (4.2 × 10⁴ J) is worth encouraging. His markers noted "very few used standard form — please use it".

F4 (3)

  • Arrangement: particles are close together / touching — (1)
  • Arrangement: randomly arranged / irregular / no fixed pattern — (1)
  • Motion: particles slide / flow / move past one another — (1)

Critical wording. "The particles move" alone does not score. His markers wanted something implying contact and movement past each other. If he writes only "they move around", code W — this is one to drill.

Section G — Radioactivity (8)

G1 (a) (1) 2 protons and 2 neutrons. Accept "a helium nucleus".

G1 (b) (1) +2. Accept "positive, +2".

G2 (2)

  • The time taken for the activity of a sample to halve — (1)
  • or the time taken for half the (unstable) nuclei in a sample to decay — (1)
  • Both marks for a complete, correct statement.

Watch for: "the time for a nucleus to halve". Wrong — it's the sample, not a single nucleus. His markers picked this up specifically. Code W.

G3 (2)

  • Identifies 4 half-lives (24 ÷ 6) — (1)
  • 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 Bq — (1)

G4 (2)

  • Alpha is relatively large/massive and has a charge of +2, so it interacts strongly with atoms it passes — (1)
  • It therefore loses its energy quickly over a short distance, so it is easily absorbed (by paper or a few cm of air) — (1)

Section H — Practical and Data Skills (8)

H1 (a) (1) Temperature (of the water).

H1 (b) (1) Resistance (of the thermistor).

H2 (2)

  • Validity: whether the investigation actually tests what it claims to — i.e. only the independent variable is changed, all other variables controlled (a fair test) — (1)
  • Reliability: whether repeating the measurements gives consistent results / repeats and a mean — (1)

These are examined as separate questions and students routinely conflate them. If he can't split them, this is a priority — it appears in every paper.

H3 (2)

  • Both variables are continuous / numerical — (1)
  • A line graph shows the trend or relationship between them, and allows values in between to be read off — (1)

H4 (2) Any two:

  • Using a different spring each time means the spring is not a controlled variable, so the test is not valid — (1)
  • Measuring each extension only once means there are no repeats, so anomalies can't be identified and the results are not reliable — (1)

"Do it again" or "take more readings" alone is too vague — his markers said so explicitly. Push for what is repeated and what is done with the repeats.

How to read the result

By section. Anything at 4/8 or below is a module that needs full treatment. Anything at 7/8 or 8/8 can be handled with a cheat sheet and a short drill rather than a full day.

By error code. This is the more useful axis.

  • Mostly K — genuine content gaps. Needs relearning, and the three weeks get tight. We'd prioritise ruthlessly by exam weighting.
  • Mostly W — he knows the physics but can't express it in the language the mark scheme wants. This is the best possible result: it's the fastest thing to fix and it's worth a lot of marks, because it's exactly what his department's examiners penalised across the whole year group.
  • Mostly M / U / P — technique and carelessness. Fixable in days, not weeks, with drilling. Also the most frustrating kind of lost mark, and worth showing him explicitly so he can see how many marks are sitting there.

A score of, say, 38/64 that is mostly W, U and P is a far better starting position than 44/64 that is mostly K.
