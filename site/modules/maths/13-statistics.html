02How it actually works

Statistics on 4MA1 is about three things: a typical value (an average), how spread out the data are, and pictures that show both. The Higher tier adds cumulative frequency graphs, quartiles and histograms with unequal class widths. Box plots are not on the 4MA1 specification, so you will not be asked to draw one.

Mean, median, mode and range from a list

Spec 6.2A–B (Foundation) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q7

The mean shares the total out equally, the median is the middle value once the data are in order, the mode is the most common value, and the range is largest minus smallest.

  1. Order the data first. Count how many values there are, n.
  2. Median: the value in position (n + 1)/2. If that is a half, take the mean of the two middle values.
  3. Mean: add them all, divide by n.
  4. Range: largest − smallest. It is a single number, not "3 to 12".
Meanmean = total of the values ÷ number of valuesso total = mean × number of values, which is the key to every reverse-mean question

Worked example: 4, 9, 2, 7, 7, 5, 10, 3, 8, 6, 11

1. In order: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11. There are 11 values.

2. Median: position (11 + 1)/2 = 6th, so the median is 7.

3. Mode 7. Range 11 − 2 = 9. Mean 72 ÷ 11 = 6.55 (3 s.f.).

Working backwards from a mean, and combining groups

Spec 6.2B (Foundation) · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q7 · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q7

Means cannot be added or averaged directly, but totals can: convert every mean to a total first.

  1. Total for each group = mean × number in the group.
  2. Add or subtract totals, and numbers of people, as the question needs.
  3. Divide the new total by the new number of people.

Worked example: 24 students have a mean of 68; 16 other students have a mean of 73. Find the mean of all 40.

1. Totals: 24 × 68 = 1632 and 16 × 73 = 1168 M1

2. Combined total 1632 + 1168 = 2800 M1

3. Mean = 2800 ÷ 40 = 70 A1

Backwards: a class of 30 has mean 71.5. The 18 boys have mean 69. Girls' total = 30 × 71.5 − 18 × 69 = 2145 − 1242 = 903, so the 12 girls' mean is 903 ÷ 12 = 75.25.

✗ (68 + 73) ÷ 2 = 70.5. That only works when the groups are the same size.
✓ Weight each mean by its group size: total ÷ total number.

Frequency tables for ungrouped data

Spec 6.2A–B (Foundation) · 2014 Edexcel Jan 4H Q4

In a frequency table each value appears "frequency" times, so the total of the data is the sum of value × frequency.

  1. Add a column for x × f.
  2. Mean = (total of the x × f column) ÷ (total of the f column).
  3. Median: find the position (n + 1)/2, then run down a cumulative total of f until you pass it.
  4. Mode: the value with the highest frequency, not the highest frequency itself.

Worked example: number of pets x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 with frequencies 3, 7, 9, 4, 2

1. x × f: 3, 14, 27, 16, 10. Total = 70 M1

2. Total frequency = 25, so mean = 70 ÷ 25 = 2.8 A1

3. Median position (25 + 1)/2 = 13th. Running totals 3, 10, 19: the 13th value is in the "3" row, so the median is 3. The mode is 3 (frequency 9).

✗ Mean = 70 ÷ 5, dividing by the number of rows.
✗ "Mode = 9", giving the frequency.
✓ Divide by the total frequency, 25. The mode is the value, 3.

Grouped data: modal class and the estimated mean

Spec 6.2C–D (Foundation) · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q1 · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q3 · 2016 Edexcel Jan 4HR Q7

With grouped data you do not know the exact values, so you use each class midpoint as a stand-in, which is why the mean is only an estimate.

  1. Find the midpoint of each class: (lower bound + upper bound) ÷ 2.
  2. Multiply each midpoint by its frequency.
  3. Add those products, and divide by the total frequency.
  4. Modal class: the class with the highest frequency. Write it as the class, e.g. 20 < m ≤ 30.

Worked example: masses 0–10 (5), 10–20 (12), 20–30 (18), 30–50 (10), 50–80 (5)

1. Midpoints 5, 15, 25, 40, 65. Products 25, 180, 450, 400, 325 M1 M1

2. Total 1380; total frequency 50 M1

3. Estimated mean = 1380 ÷ 50 = 27.6 A1

This is how the 4-mark estimated mean is scored: M2 for frequency × midpoint (M1 if you use a consistent value in each class that is not the midpoint), M1 for dividing by the total frequency, A1.

From the SAM 1H scheme: "M1 for frequency × consistent value within interval". Using the class ends instead of midpoints loses a method mark straight away.

✗ Dividing by 5, the number of classes, or by the sum of the midpoints.
✓ Σ(midpoint × frequency) ÷ Σ frequency.

Quartiles and interquartile range from a list

Spec 6.2B–C (Higher) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q12 · 2015 Edexcel Jan 3HR Q12

The quartiles cut ordered data into four equal parts, and the interquartile range (upper quartile − lower quartile) is the spread of the middle half.

  1. Put the data in order.
  2. Lower quartile: position (n + 1)/4. Median: (n + 1)/2. Upper quartile: 3(n + 1)/4.
  3. IQR = UQ − LQ.

Worked example: the 11 values 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11

1. In order already; (11 + 1)/4 = 3, so the LQ is the 3rd value, 4 M1

2. UQ is the 3 × 3 = 9th value, 9 M1

3. IQR = 9 − 4 = 5 A1

The IQR ignores the top and bottom quarters, so a single very large or very small value (an outlier) does not affect it. The range uses exactly those extreme values.

Cumulative frequency tables and graphs

Spec 6.1B–C (Higher) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q12 · 2014 Edexcel Jan 3H Q8

Cumulative frequency is a running total, and each running total is plotted at the upper end of its class.

  1. Running totals: add each frequency on to the one before. The last one must equal the total.
  2. Plot each total at the upper class boundary: 0 < m ≤ 10 is plotted at 10, not 5.
  3. Join the points with a smooth curve or straight line segments. You may start at (lowest boundary, 0).

Worked example: frequencies 8, 17, 25, 40, 22, 8 for 0–10, 10–20, …, 50–60

1. Cumulative frequencies: 8, 25, 50, 90, 112, 120 B1

2. Points: (10, 8), (20, 25), (30, 50), (40, 90), (50, 112), (60, 120) M1

3. Join with a curve or line segments A1

SAM 2H scheme: "NB A bar chart, unless it has a curve going consistently through a point in each bar, scores no points."

✗ Plotting at the midpoints (5, 15, 25…).
✓ Plotting at the upper bounds (10, 20, 30…).

Reading a cumulative frequency graph

Spec 6.2A, 6.2D (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q10

Go across from a cumulative frequency to find a value, or up from a value to find how many are below it.

  1. Median: go across from n/2 on the cumulative frequency axis to the curve, then down.
  2. Quartiles: go across from n/4 (LQ) and 3n/4 (UQ). IQR = UQ − LQ.
  3. "How many more than x?": go up from x, read the cumulative frequency, and subtract it from the total.
  4. Show the reading lines on the graph: they carry the method mark.

Worked example (based on 2018 1H Q10): 80 films

1. IQR: read across at 80 ÷ 4 = 20 and 3 × 80 ÷ 4 = 60. The 2018 readings were 103 and 123 minutes M1

2. IQR = 123 − 103 = 20 minutes (the scheme accepted 19 to 21) A1

3. "More than 35% are over 120 minutes." Up from 120: cf = 55. Over 120 = 80 − 55 = 25 M1

4. 25 ÷ 80 × 100 = 31.25% M1

5. 31.25% is less than 35%, so no, she is not correct A1

✗ Reading 55 and saying "55 films are over 120 minutes". The graph gives how many are up to 120.
✓ Subtract from the total: 80 − 55 = 25 are over 120.

Histograms: frequency density

Spec 6.1A (Higher) · 2016 Edexcel Jan 4HR Q18

In a histogram the area of each bar shows the frequency, so the height is frequency density, not frequency.

Frequency densityfrequency density = frequency ÷ class widthso frequency = frequency density × class width = area of the bar
  1. Work out each class width from the table (upper bound − lower bound).
  2. Frequency density = frequency ÷ width, for every class.
  3. Draw bars with no gaps, width = class width, height = frequency density. Label the vertical axis "Frequency density".

Worked example: 0–10 (10), 10–30 (24), 30–40 (30), 40–80 (18)

1. Widths 10, 20, 10, 40 M1

2. Frequency densities 10 ÷ 10 = 1, 24 ÷ 20 = 1.2, 30 ÷ 10 = 3, 18 ÷ 40 = 0.45 A1

The tallest bar (30–40) is not just the biggest frequency: it is the most densely packed class.

✗ Bar heights equal to the frequencies. With unequal widths that makes wide classes look far too big.
✓ Height = frequency ÷ class width.

Reading a histogram when there is no scale

Spec 6.1A (Higher) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q14 · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q20

If the axis has no numbers, use the one frequency you are given to find what one unit of area is worth.

  1. Count the area of the bar(s) you know the frequency of, in small squares or as width × height.
  2. Work out how many people one unit of area stands for.
  3. Find the area of the bars you need and convert with the same rate.

Worked example (based on SAM 1H Q14): 26 plants are under 20 cm

1. The 0–20 bar gives frequency density 26 ÷ 20 = 1.3, which fixes the scale for the others M1

2. The other bars then give 3.6 × 10, 3.3 × 10 and 1 × 30, so the total = 26 + 36 + 33 + 30 M1

3. = 125 plants A1

The 2018 version gave the frequency of the two end bars together (6 babies) and asked for the middle ones. Same idea: area of end bars ↔ 6, then scale up.

Estimating from part of a bar

Spec 6.1A (Higher) · 2012 Edexcel Jan 3H (old spec)

To estimate how many are in part of a class, take the same fraction of that bar's area, assuming the values are spread evenly across the class.

  1. Find the frequency density of each bar involved.
  2. Multiply by the width of the part you need, not the whole class.
  3. Add the pieces. Say it is an estimate.

Worked example: the 20–40 bar has frequency 24. Estimate how many are between 25 and 35.

1. Frequency density = 24 ÷ 20 = 1.2 M1

2. 25 to 35 is a width of 10, so 1.2 × 10 = 12 (an estimate) A1

Comparing two distributions

Spec 6.2B (Higher) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q12(b) · 2015 Edexcel Jan 3HR Q12

A full comparison makes two points: one about an average and one about the spread, each in the context of the question.

  1. Average: compare medians (or means). "Group A's median is higher, so on average A took longer."
  2. Spread: compare IQRs (or ranges). "Group A's IQR is smaller, so A's times were more consistent."
  3. Quote the numbers you are comparing.

✗ "A is higher and more spread." Higher what? More spread how?
✓ "A has a higher median (34 compared with 29), so on average A scored more. A has a lower IQR (8 compared with 15), so A's scores were more consistent."

Pie charts and two-way tables

Spec 6.1A–C (Foundation)

A pie chart shares 360° in proportion to the frequencies; a two-way table splits the same people two ways at once.

  1. Angle = frequency ÷ total × 360°.
  2. Frequency from an angle = angle ÷ 360 × total.
  3. Two-way table: every row and every column must add up to its total. Fill in the cells you can, then use the totals to find the rest.

Worked example: 72 students, 110° for football

1. 110 ÷ 360 × 72 = 22 students M1 A1

2. 18 students would need 18 ÷ 72 × 360 = 90°.

03Cheat sheet

The facts behind most of the marks

Total = mean × number. Combine groups with totals, never by averaging the means.

Frequency table mean: Σxf ÷ Σf. Grouped: use midpoints, and call it an estimate.

Median at (n + 1)/2 for a list. LQ at (n + 1)/4, UQ at 3(n + 1)/4. IQR = UQ − LQ.

Cumulative frequency graph: plot at upper bounds; read at n/4, n/2, 3n/4; "more than" means subtract from the total.

Histogram: frequency density = frequency ÷ class width; frequency = area.

Compare: an average and a spread, with numbers, in context.

04Understanding it

U1

Why is the mean from a grouped frequency table only an estimate?

Show answer

The table only says how many values are in each class, not what they are. Using the midpoint assumes the values in each class average out to the middle of it.

If most values in a class were near the top, the true mean would be higher than the estimate. The raw data would give the exact mean.

U2

Class A (20 students) has a mean of 60. Class B (30 students) has a mean of 70. Why is the overall mean not 65?

Show answer

65 treats the two classes as equally important, but B has more students. The totals are 1200 and 2100, so the overall mean is 3300 ÷ 50 = 66.

The combined mean is pulled towards the bigger group. It only equals the midpoint of the two means when the groups are the same size.

U3

Why are cumulative frequencies plotted at the upper class boundary and not the midpoint?

Show answer

A cumulative frequency of 25 for 10 < m ≤ 20 means 25 values are 20 or less. You only know that all of them are in by the end of the class, at 20.

At the midpoint, 15, some of the class's values would not have been reached yet, so plotting 25 there would overstate how many are below 15.

U4

One player's scores include a single very high score. Why is the IQR a better measure of consistency than the range here?

Show answer

The range uses only the largest and smallest values, so one unusual score makes it large even if every other score is close together.

The IQR uses the middle 50% of the data, so it ignores the extremes. It reflects how the player usually performs.

U5

A histogram has classes 0–10 and 10–40. Both have a frequency of 30. Why are the bars different heights?

Show answer

The area shows the frequency. The 10–40 bar is three times as wide, so it only needs a third of the height to have the same area.

Frequency densities: 30 ÷ 10 = 3 and 30 ÷ 30 = 1. If the heights were equal, the wider class would look as if it had three times as many values.

U6

A cumulative frequency graph for 60 people shows a reading of 42 at 30 minutes. What does 42 mean, and how many took more than 30 minutes?

Show answer

42 people took 30 minutes or less. The graph always counts from the bottom up.

So 60 − 42 = 18 people took more than 30 minutes. The most common error is to give 42 as the answer.

U7

For 11 values, why is the lower quartile the 3rd value, while for 80 values on a cumulative frequency graph you read across at 20, not 20.25?

Show answer

With a short list you need an exact position: (11 + 1)/4 = 3, so the 3rd value.

A cumulative frequency graph is a smooth estimate for grouped data, so the quarter-of-the-way point is simply 80 ÷ 4 = 20. The 2018 scheme used readings at cf 20 and cf 60. With large n the difference is too small to see on the graph anyway.

U8

Why does a comparison need both an average and a measure of spread?

Show answer

They answer different questions. The average says which group is typically higher. The spread says which group is more consistent.

Two groups can have the same median but very different spreads, like a steady player and an unreliable one with the same typical score. Exam comparisons usually give a mark for each, and each needs context: "on average the girls scored higher", not just "the median is higher".
