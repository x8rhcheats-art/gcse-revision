Accuracy and Bounds

Every measurement is rounded, so every calculation with it has a range. The Higher bounds question is worth 2 to 4 marks and always tests the same thing: which end of each range makes the answer biggest or smallest. For subtraction and division it is the opposite end from what you expect. This module also covers rounding, significant figures, estimation and choosing a sensible accuracy, which cost marks on nearly every question in the paper.

02How it actually works

A value "correct to" some accuracy could really be anywhere in a range half a unit either side. Bounds questions ask how far that range stretches after a calculation. Everything else here is about writing numbers to the right accuracy and not losing accuracy along the way.

Rounding to decimal places

Spec 1.8B · every paper

Look at the digit after the last one you are keeping: 5 or more rounds up, 4 or less leaves it.

  1. Count the decimal places you need and draw a line after that digit.
  2. Look at the next digit only.
  3. Round, and keep trailing zeros that are part of the accuracy: 7.2968 to 2 dp is 7.30, not 7.3.

Worked example — round 7.2968 and 0.0060749 to 2 decimal places

7.29|68: the next digit is 6, so round up. 7.29 → 7.30 → 7.30

0.00|60749: the next digit is 6, so round up → 0.01

Significant figures

Spec 1.8B · every paper ("Give your answer correct to 3 significant figures")

Significant figures start at the first non-zero digit; zeros before it are placeholders and don't count.

  1. Find the first non-zero digit and count from there.
  2. Round at the required figure as usual.
  3. For big numbers, fill with zeros to keep the size: 38 472 to 3 sf is 38 500, not 385.
  4. Zeros between non-zero digits count: 4.07 has 3 sf.

Worked example — 0.004076 to 2 sf, 38 472 to 3 sf

0.004076: the first significant digit is the 4. Keep 4, 0; the next digit is 7, so round up → 0.0041

38 472: keep 3, 8, 4; the next digit is 7, so round up → 38 500

✗ 38 472 to 3 sf = 385. The size of the number has changed.
✗ 0.004076 to 2 sf = 0.0. The leading zeros were counted as significant.
✓ Only the first non-zero digit starts the count, and zeros fill back to the right size.

Estimation

Spec 1.8D

Estimate by rounding every number to 1 significant figure, then calculating in your head.

  1. Round each number to 1 sf (unless the question says otherwise).
  2. Show the rounded numbers. That is the method mark.
  3. Work out the simpler calculation. Dividing by a small decimal makes the answer bigger.

Worked example — estimate (48.7 × 3.12) ÷ 0.487

(50 × 3) ÷ 0.5 (M1 for at least two numbers correctly rounded)

= 150 ÷ 0.5 = 300 (A1). The exact value is 312, so the estimate is sensible.

Upper and lower bounds of a measurement

Spec 1.8C · 2016 Edexcel Jan 3H Q9(b) · 2017 Edexcel Jan 4HR Q9(c)

The bounds are half a unit of the rounding either side of the stated value.

Bounds of a rounded valuebounds = value ± ½ × (the unit it was rounded to)"to 1 dp" → ± 0.05 · "to the nearest 10" → ± 5 · "to 2 sf" for 3400 → ± 50
  1. Identify the unit of rounding: nearest cm (1), 1 dp (0.1), 2 sf of 3400 (100).
  2. Halve it.
  3. Subtract for the lower bound and add for the upper bound.
  4. Error interval: LB ≤ x < UB.

Worked example — bounds of 6.4 cm (1 dp), 3400 g (2 sf) and 70 kg (nearest 5 kg)

6.4 cm: unit 0.1, half 0.05 → 6.35 ≤ l < 6.45

3400 g to 2 sf: the second sf is the hundreds, so the unit is 100 and half is 50 → 3350 and 3450

70 kg to the nearest 5: half of 5 is 2.5 → 67.5 and 72.5

✗ Upper bound of 6.4 = 6.49 or 6.449… The upper bound is written as 6.45, even though 6.45 itself would round up.
✗ 3400 to 2 sf: bounds 3399.5 and 3400.5. That treats it as the nearest whole number.
✓ Work out the unit from the accuracy stated, then halve it.

Bounds for counted quantities

Spec 1.8C · 2014 Edexcel Jan 4H Q21

A number of items "to the nearest 100" has bounds 50 either side, and in bounds calculations you use those values just as you would for a measurement.

  1. 1300 sheets to the nearest 100 → 1250 ≤ n < 1350.
  2. In a calculation, use 1250 and 1350 as the bounds (the mark schemes accept them).
  3. Decide which bound you need using the rules in the next sections.

Bounds in addition and multiplication

Spec 1.8A (Higher)

For adding and multiplying positive values, the biggest answer comes from the upper bounds and the smallest from the lower bounds.

  1. Write both bounds of every value.
  2. Max: UB + UB, UB × UB. Min: LB + LB, LB × LB.
  3. Round only at the end, and only if asked.

Worked example — a rectangle measures 8.3 cm by 5.6 cm, each to 1 dp

Bounds: 8.25 ≤ l < 8.35 and 5.55 ≤ w < 5.65 (B1 for any correct bound)

Upper bound of perimeter = 2(8.35 + 5.65) = 28 cm

Upper bound of area = 8.35 × 5.65 = 47.1775 cm² (M1 A1); lower bound of area = 8.25 × 5.55 = 45.7875 cm²

Bounds in subtraction

Spec 1.8A (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q15

To make a difference as big as possible, take the biggest first number and subtract the smallest second number, and the other way round for the minimum.

Bounds of a difference — memoriseUB(a − b) = UB(a) − LB(b)  ·  LB(a − b) = LB(a) − UB(b)the bounds cross over
  1. Write the four bounds.
  2. Upper bound of a − b: UB of a, minus the LB of b.
  3. Lower bound of a − b: LB of a, minus the UB of b.

Worked example — a = 12.4 and b = 5.7, both to 1 dp. Find the bounds of a − b.

a: 12.35 to 12.45; b: 5.65 to 5.75 (B1)

UB = 12.45 − 5.65 = 6.8 (M1 for the correct combination, A1)

LB = 12.35 − 5.75 = 6.6

✗ UB(a − b) = 12.45 − 5.75 = 6.7. Upper minus upper is not the maximum, because you are taking away more than you need to.
✓ To maximise a − b, make a big and b small.

Bounds in division

Spec 1.8A (Higher) · 2014 Edexcel Jan 4H Q21

A fraction is biggest when the top is biggest and the bottom is smallest.

Bounds of a quotient — memoriseUB(a ÷ b) = UB(a) ÷ LB(b)  ·  LB(a ÷ b) = LB(a) ÷ UB(b)speed = distance ÷ time · density = mass ÷ volume · thickness = height ÷ number
  1. Write the bounds of the numerator and the denominator.
  2. For the maximum, divide the upper bound of the top by the lower bound of the bottom.
  3. For the minimum, divide the lower bound of the top by the upper bound of the bottom.

Worked example — a runner covers 200 m (nearest 5 m) in 24.6 s (nearest 0.1 s). Find the upper bound of the average speed.

Distance 197.5 to 202.5; time 24.55 to 24.65 (B1)

UB speed = 202.5 ÷ 24.55 (M1) = 8.2484… = 8.25 m/s (3 sf) (A1)

(LB speed = 197.5 ÷ 24.65 = 8.01 m/s)

Bounds in a formula

Spec 1.8A (Higher) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q17

Work from the inside out: decide for each part whether it should be as big or as small as possible to push the whole answer the way you want.

  1. Write the bounds of every letter.
  2. Decide what the final answer needs, e.g. maximum.
  3. For each numerator: make it big. For each denominator: make it small. Inside a subtraction, cross over as before.
  4. Substitute in one line, then evaluate. Show the line you substituted. The scheme marks that line.

Worked example — y = (a + b)/c with a = 5.3, b = 2.1, c = 1.8, all to 1 dp

UB of y: top biggest, bottom smallest → (5.35 + 2.15) ÷ 1.75 (M1) = 4.29 (3 sf) (A1)

LB of y: (5.25 + 2.05) ÷ 1.85 = 3.95 (3 sf)

Bounds as a percentage or ratio

Spec 1.8A (Higher), spec example

"The smallest possible X as a percentage of the largest possible X" means work out both bounds, then LB ÷ UB × 100.

  1. Find the lower bound of the quantity.
  2. Find the upper bound of the quantity.
  3. LB ÷ UB × 100, rounded as asked.

Worked example — a rectangle is 20 cm by 11 cm, each to the nearest cm. Smallest area as a percentage of largest area, to 3 sf.

Smallest area = 19.5 × 10.5 = 204.75 (M1)

Largest area = 20.5 × 11.5 = 235.75 (M1)

204.75 ÷ 235.75 × 100 (M1) = 86.85… = 86.9% (A1)

Choosing a suitable degree of accuracy

Spec 1.8A (Higher)

A value is known to a given accuracy only if its upper and lower bounds both round to the same number at that accuracy.

  1. Work out the upper and lower bounds of the answer.
  2. Round both to 1 sf, 2 sf, 3 sf… and stop at the last level where they agree.
  3. Give that value and say why: "both bounds round to … to 2 sf".

Worked example — P = 23.7 × 8.46, with 23.7 to 1 dp and 8.46 to 2 dp. Give P to a suitable degree of accuracy.

UB = 23.75 × 8.465 = 201.04…; LB = 23.65 × 8.455 = 199.96… (M1 for both)

To 3 sf: 201 and 200, which disagree. To 2 sf: 200 and 200, which agree.

P = 200 (2 sf), because both bounds round to 200 to 2 sf (A1)

Accuracy in the final answer

Spec 1.11A · every paper

Keep full calculator values all the way through and round once, at the end, to the accuracy the question asks for.

  1. No instruction? Give an exact answer, or 3 sf for a non-exact decimal (angles to 1 dp).
  2. Money: 2 dp, e.g. $28 688.08, or "to the nearest HK$" if asked.
  3. Context: people and objects are whole numbers. "How many boxes are needed" rounds up; "how many fit" rounds down.
  4. Write the unrounded value first (e.g. 54.361…), then the rounded answer. If the rounding goes wrong, the method mark is still there.

Worked example — volume of a sphere of radius 2.35 cm

V = 4/3 × π × 2.353 = 54.3615… = 54.4 cm³ (3 sf)

Rounding r to 2.4 first gives 57.9. That is wrong by more than 3, and the A1 is lost.

✗ Rounding a middle value, then carrying on with the rounded number.
✓ Use the calculator's ANS or memory, and round once.

03Cheat sheet

The bounds rules

Bounds of a value: ± half the unit of rounding. Error interval LB ≤ x < UB

a + b, a × b: max = UB with UB · min = LB with LB

a − b: max = UB(a) − LB(b) · min = LB(a) − UB(b)

a ÷ b: max = UB(a) ÷ LB(b) · min = LB(a) ÷ UB(b)

Suitable accuracy: the level at which UB and LB round to the same value

Definitions marked to the word

Lower boundThe smallest value that would round to the given number
Error intervalThe range of possible values, written LB ≤ x < UB
EstimateAn approximate answer found by rounding each number (usually to 1 sf) first
Degree of accuracyHow precisely a value is given, e.g. to 3 sf, 2 dp or the nearest 10

04Understanding it

U1

A length is 6.4 cm to 1 dp. Why is the upper bound written as 6.45 when 6.45 itself would round to 6.5?

Show answer

The true length can be anything up to, but not including, 6.45: 6.449, 6.4499, 6.44999… There is no "largest number below 6.45", so we use the boundary 6.45 and write 6.35 ≤ l < 6.45.

In calculations, using 6.45 gives the limit the answer can approach, which is what the question wants.

U2

Why does the upper bound of a − b use the lower bound of b?

Show answer

Subtracting b takes something away. To keep as much as possible, take away as little as possible, which means the smallest b.

So the biggest a minus the smallest b gives the biggest difference. With a = 12.4 and b = 5.7 (1 dp): 12.45 − 5.65 = 6.8, but 12.45 − 5.75 is only 6.7.

U3

Why does the maximum speed use the minimum time?

Show answer

Speed = distance ÷ time. Dividing by a smaller number gives a bigger answer: covering the same distance in less time means going faster.

So for the maximum speed use the maximum distance and the minimum time: UB ÷ LB.

U4

3400 is given "correct to 2 significant figures". Why are its bounds 3350 and 3450, not 3399.5 and 3400.5?

Show answer

To 2 sf, the second significant figure is the 4, which is in the hundreds column. So the number was rounded to the nearest 100.

Half of 100 is 50, so the bounds are 3400 ± 50. 3399.5 and 3400.5 would only be right if it had been rounded to the nearest whole number.

U5

P = 23.7 × 8.46 has bounds 199.96 and 201.04. Why can P only be given as 200 to 2 sf, and not as 201?

Show answer

To 3 sf the bounds round to 200 and 201, so we cannot tell which is correct. The true value might be either.

To 2 sf both round to 200. That is the most accurate value we can be certain of, so 200 (2 sf) is the suitable degree of accuracy.

U6

Why does rounding the radius to 2.4 before finding the volume of a sphere of radius 2.35 cm give a much worse answer?

Show answer

The volume depends on r3, so a small error in r is magnified. 2.4 is about 2% too big, and cubing makes that about 6%.

4/3π(2.35)3 = 54.4 but 4/3π(2.4)3 = 57.9. Rounding early loses the A1, even with a perfect method.

U7

For y = 3a/(b − c), explain which bound of b and which bound of c give the largest possible y.

Show answer

For y to be big, the numerator 3a must be big (UB of a) and the denominator b − c must be small.

b − c is smallest when b is small and c is large: LB of b minus UB of c. So UB(y) = 3 × UB(a) ÷ (LB(b) − UB(c)). The subtraction inside the denominator flips the rule twice.

U8

Why do you round up to find the number of 12-egg boxes needed for 100 eggs, but round down to find how many 12-egg boxes you can fill?

Show answer

100 ÷ 12 = 8.33… 8 boxes hold only 96 eggs, so to hold every egg you need 9 boxes. Round up.

But you can only fill 8 boxes completely. The ninth would have just 4 eggs. Round down. The context decides, not the "5 or more" rule.
