Differentiation

The calculus at the end of the course, and a reliable 4–8 marks near the back of a paper. The exam uses one rule, for axn, and applies it in four ways: a gradient, a stationary point, a maximum or minimum in a practical problem, and velocity and acceleration from displacement. Once the rule is secure, every question is a routine.

02How it actually works

A straight line has one gradient everywhere. A curve's gradient changes as you move along it. Differentiating gives a formula, dy/dx, for the gradient at any x, which is exact where a drawn tangent is only an estimate. Where the gradient is zero the curve is momentarily flat: a peak or a trough. That single fact is behind every maximum, minimum and "at rest" question.

Rates of change and what dy/dx means

Spec 3.4A

dy/dx is the gradient of the curve, the rate at which y changes as x changes, and it has its own value at every point.

  • On y = x2 the tangent at x = 1 has gradient 2, at x = 2 gradient 4 and at x = 3 gradient 6. The gradient is 2x, so dy/dx = 2x.
  • dy/dx is read "dee y by dee x". If the letters change, so does the notation: dA/dx, dV/dr, ds/dt.
  • Positive dy/dx means y is increasing, negative means decreasing, and zero means the curve is momentarily flat.

Differentiating axn

Spec 3.4B · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q17(a) · 2016 Edexcel Jan 4H Q16(a) (old spec)

Multiply by the power, then take one off the power. Do it to each term separately. A number on its own disappears.

Differentiating a power of x — memorisey = axn   →   dy/dx = naxn−1n is any integer, including negative · kx → k · a constant → 0 · NOT on the formula sheet
  1. Write every term as a number × a power of x.
  2. For each term: bring the power down and multiply, then reduce the power by 1.
  3. A term in x (x1) becomes just its number. A constant (x0) becomes 0.

Worked example: y = 4x3 − 5x2 + 7x − 2

4x3 → 3 × 4x2 = 12x2 · −5x2 → −10x · 7x → 7 · −2 → 0

dy/dx = 12x2 − 10x + 7   (B2: B1 for any two terms correct)

2018 2H Q17(a): y = x3 − 2x2 − 15x + 5 gives dy/dx = 3x2 − 4x − 15. The scheme's note was "Award B1 for any 2 or 3 of the 4 terms differentiated correctly", so the constant disappearing counts as one of the four.

✗ 4x3 → 12x3. The power must come down by 1: 12x2.
✗ 7x → 7x0 = 0, or −2 → −2. x becomes 1, so 7x → 7. A constant → 0.
✗ 4x3 → 4 × 3 = 12x. Keep the x with its new power.

Rewrite before you differentiate

Spec 3.4B · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q21 · 2013 Edexcel Jan 3H Q18(a) (old spec)

The rule only works on separate terms of the form axn, so fractions, brackets and quotients must be turned into powers of x first.

  1. x in the denominator: use a negative power. 2/x = 2x−1, and 3/x2 = 3x−2.
  2. Brackets: expand first. (2x + 1)(x − 3) = 2x2 − 5x − 3.
  3. Divided by x: split into separate fractions. (x3 + 4x)/x = x2 + 4.
  4. Differentiate. With a negative power, "take one off" makes it more negative: −1 → −2.

Worked examples

y = 2/x = 2x−1 → dy/dx = −1 × 2x−2 = −2x−2 = −2/x2

y = 3/x2 = 3x−2 → dy/dx = −6x−3 = −6/x3

y = 8x2 + 2/x (SAM 1H Q21) → dy/dx = 16x − 2x−2

y = (2x + 1)(x − 3) = 2x2 − 5x − 3 → dy/dx = 4x − 5

✗ 2/x → 2/1 = 2, by "differentiating the bottom". Rewrite as 2x−1 first.
✗ 2x−1 → −2x0. The power goes down by 1: −1 − 1 = −2.
✗ Differentiating each bracket separately and multiplying. Expand first.

The gradient at a point

Spec 3.4C

Differentiate first, then substitute the x-value into dy/dx, not into y.

  1. Find dy/dx.
  2. Substitute the given x into dy/dx. The result is the gradient of the curve (and of the tangent) at that point.

Worked example: the gradient of y = x3 − 4x + 1 at the point where x = 2

dy/dx = 3x2 − 4.   At x = 2: 3(4) − 4 = 8

Compare module 7: drawing a tangent would only have given an estimate close to 8.

✗ Putting x = 2 into y: 8 − 8 + 1 = 1. That is the y-coordinate, not the gradient.

Where does the gradient take a given value?

Spec 3.4C · 2016 Edexcel Jan 4H Q16(b) · 2017 Edexcel Jan 3HR Q20(b) (old spec)

Set dy/dx equal to the value, solve for x, then put each x into the original equation to get y.

  1. dy/dx = the given gradient.
  2. Solve: linear for a quadratic curve, quadratic for a cubic curve.
  3. Substitute each x into y (the original equation) for the coordinates.

Worked example: the point on y = 2x2 − 8x + 3 where the gradient is 12

dy/dx = 4x − 8 = 12, so x = 5   (M1 · A1)

y = 2(25) − 40 + 3 = 13, so the point is (5, 13)   (A1)

For a cubic you get a quadratic. In 2017 3HR Q20, y = x3 − 4x2 + 5x + 4 with gradient 1 gave 3x2 − 8x + 4 = 0, so (3x − 2)(x − 2) = 0 and x = 2/3 or x = 2: two points.

Stationary points

Spec 3.4C · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q21 · 2013 Edexcel Jan 3H Q18(b) (old spec)

At a stationary (turning) point the gradient is zero, so solve dy/dx = 0.

  1. Differentiate.
  2. Set dy/dx = 0 and solve for x.
  3. Substitute each x into the original equation to get y.
  4. Give the answer as coordinates (x, y).

Worked example: SAM 1H Q21, the stationary point of y = 8x2 + 2/x

y = 8x2 + 2x−1, so dy/dx = 16x − 2x−2   (M2: M1 for one term correct)

16x − 2/x2 = 0   (M1). Multiply by x2: 16x3 = 2, so x3 = 1/8, so x = 0.5   (M1)

y = 8(0.25) + 2/0.5 = 2 + 4 = 6, so the point is (0.5, 6)   (A1)

Worked example: y = x3 − 3x2 − 9x + 5

dy/dx = 3x2 − 6x − 9 = 3(x2 − 2x − 3) = 3(x − 3)(x + 1) = 0, so x = 3 or x = −1

x = −1: y = −1 − 3 + 9 + 5 = 10.   x = 3: y = 27 − 27 − 27 + 5 = −22

Stationary points: (−1, 10) and (3, −22)

✗ Stopping at "x = 3 or x = −1". The question asks for the coordinates, so find each y.
✗ Substituting x back into dy/dx to get y. That always gives 0. Use the original equation.

Maximum or minimum? Use the shape

Spec 3.4D: "by considering the general shape of the graph only"

You decide whether a stationary point is a maximum or a minimum from the shape of the graph, which the equation tells you.

  1. Quadratic, x2 term positive: U-shape, so its one stationary point is a minimum. x2 term negative: ∩-shape, so a maximum.
  2. Cubic, x3 term positive: it rises, dips, then rises. The left-hand (smaller x) stationary point is the maximum and the right-hand one is the minimum. For a negative x3 term, it is the other way round.
  3. In a practical problem, such as A = 120x − 2x2, the leading term is negative, so ∩-shaped, so the stationary point is the maximum the question wants.
  4. To confirm, you can check the sign of dy/dx just either side: + then − is a maximum, − then + is a minimum.

Worked example: y = x3 − 3x2 − 9x + 5, continued

A positive x3 term means the curve rises, falls, then rises again. So (−1, 10) is the maximum and (3, −22) is the minimum.

Check: dy/dx at x = −2 is 15 (going up), at x = 0 is −9 (going down), at x = 4 is 15 (going up). ✓

Increasing and decreasing: where is the gradient negative?

Spec 3.4C, with 2.8A · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q17(b)

"Negative gradient" means dy/dx < 0. That is a quadratic inequality, solved with critical values and a sketch.

  1. Write dy/dx < 0 (or > 0 for a positive gradient).
  2. Solve dy/dx = 0 for the critical values.
  3. dy/dx is a U-shaped quadratic, so < 0 is between the critical values.
  4. Write the answer with inequality signs.

Worked example: 2018 2H Q17(b), y = x3 − 2x2 − 15x + 5

3x2 − 4x − 15 < 0   (M1)

(3x + 5)(x − 3) = 0, so x = −5/3 or x = 3   (M1 · A1)

−5/3 < x < 3   (A1): the curve goes downhill between its maximum and its minimum.

Maximum and minimum problems

Spec 3.4C, 3.4E · 2014 Edexcel Jan 4H Q15 · 2015 Edexcel Jan 3HR Q17 · 2016 Edexcel Jan 3HR Q15(c) (old spec)

Write the quantity to be maximised as a formula in one variable, differentiate, set the derivative to zero, then substitute back to get the maximum value.

  1. "Show that": use the constraint (fixed perimeter, fixed volume) to write the other length in terms of x, then substitute it into the area or volume formula.
  2. Differentiate with respect to the variable (dA/dx, dV/dr).
  3. Set it = 0 and solve. Reject values that make a length zero or negative.
  4. Substitute into the original formula for the maximum value, with units.
  5. Justify max or min from the shape: a negative leading term gives a maximum.

Worked example: 120 m of fencing makes three sides of a rectangular pen against a wall. Find the largest possible area.

Two sides are x m, so the side parallel to the wall is (120 − 2x) m.   A = x(120 − 2x) = 120x − 2x2

dA/dx = 120 − 4x = 0, so x = 30   (M1 · A1)

A = 120(30) − 2(900) = 3600 − 1800 = 1800 m²   (A1). The −2x2 term makes it ∩-shaped, so this is a maximum.

✗ Giving x = 30 as "the maximum area". x is a length. Substitute it back into A.
✗ Substituting x = 30 into dA/dx. That gives 0 every time.

Kinematics: displacement, velocity and acceleration

Spec 3.4E · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q25

Velocity is the rate of change of displacement, and acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. Differentiate s to get v, then differentiate v to get a.

Velocity from displacement — memorisev = ds/dts = displacement from O (m) · t = time (s) · v = velocity (m/s)
Acceleration from velocity — memorisea = dv/dta = acceleration (m/s²) · differentiate s twice to reach a
Question saysMeans
"initially", "at the start"t = 0
"instantaneously at rest", "changes direction"v = 0

"returns to O"s = 0

Worked example: SAM 2H Q25, s = t3 − 5t2 − 8t + 3. When is the particle instantaneously at rest?

v = 3t2 − 10t − 8   (M1: 2 of 3 terms correct)

3t2 − 10t − 8 = 0   (A1)

(3t + 2)(t − 4) = 0   (M1)

t = 4 only: t = −2/3 is rejected because time cannot be negative   (A1)

✗ Setting s = 0 for "at rest". At rest means not moving, so the velocity is zero. s = 0 means "back at O".
✗ Keeping t = −2/3. The scheme said "t = 4 only".

Kinematics: harder questions

Spec 3.4E (the specification's own example)

Keep s, v and a separate and labelled, and substitute each time into the right one.

  1. Write out s, v and a as three separate lines before answering anything.
  2. Translate the words into a condition (the table above) and solve.
  3. Put the time into whichever of s, v or a the question asks for, with units.

Worked example: s = 24t2 − t3, 0 ≤ t ≤ 20 (the spec example)

v = ds/dt = 48t − 3t2  ·  a = dv/dt = 48 − 6t

Maximum velocity: a = 0, so 48 − 6t = 0, so t = 8. v = 48(8) − 3(64) = 384 − 192 = 192 m/s

Worked example: s = t3 − 6t2 + 9t + 2

v = 3t2 − 12t + 9 = 3(t − 1)(t − 3), so the particle is at rest at t = 1 and t = 3.

Initial velocity: v(0) = 9 m/s.   a = 6t − 12, so a = 0 when t = 2.

Displacement at t = 3: 27 − 54 + 27 + 2 = 2 m from O.

✗ Using v = s/t, "distance over time". That only works at constant velocity. Here velocity changes, so differentiate.

03Cheat sheet

One rule, four uses

Rule: axn → naxn−1. kx → k. A constant → 0. Rewrite 1/x as x−1 and expand brackets first.

Gradient at a point: put x into dy/dx.

Stationary point: dy/dx = 0, solve for x, then put x into y for the coordinates.

Max or min: from the shape. Positive cubic: left point max, right point min. −x2 gives a max.

Negative gradient: dy/dx < 0, a quadratic inequality, answered with inequality signs.

Optimisation: formula in one variable → derivative = 0 → substitute back into the formula.

Kinematics: v = ds/dt, a = dv/dt. At rest: v = 0. Maximum velocity: a = 0. Initially: t = 0.

04Understanding it

U1

Why does a constant term disappear when you differentiate, and why does 7x become 7?

Show answer

dy/dx measures how steep the graph is. Adding a constant (+5) slides the whole graph up without changing its steepness anywhere, so it contributes 0 to the gradient.

y = 7x is a straight line with gradient 7 everywhere, so its derivative is 7. The rule agrees: 7x1 → 1 × 7x0 = 7.

U2

Why must you rewrite 4/x as 4x−1 before differentiating, and what is its derivative?

Show answer

The rule works only on terms of the form axn. In 4/x the x is in the denominator, so it is not yet in that form. 4/x = 4x−1 is.

Then −1 × 4x−2 = −4/x2. The derivative is always negative, which matches the graph of y = 4/x: it slopes downhill on both branches.

U3

Why is dy/dx = 0 at a maximum or minimum point?

Show answer

Just before a maximum the curve goes up (positive gradient). Just after, it goes down (negative gradient). To change from positive to negative smoothly, the gradient must pass through zero, and it does so exactly at the peak, where the tangent is horizontal.

A minimum is the same in reverse. So solving dy/dx = 0 finds every turning point.

U4

A cubic with a positive x3 term has stationary points at x = −1 and x = 3. Without any more calculation, which is the maximum? Why?

Show answer

A positive cubic comes up from the bottom-left and ends at the top-right. To have two turning points, it must rise, turn down, then turn back up.

So the first turning point you meet (smaller x, x = −1) is the maximum, and x = 3 is the minimum. The spec says this "general shape" reasoning is all you need.

U5

In an optimisation question, why do you substitute the x from dA/dx = 0 back into A, and not into dA/dx?

Show answer

dA/dx = 0 only tells you where the maximum is: the value of x. It is zero there by definition, so substituting into dA/dx just gives 0.

The maximum value is the area itself, so x goes into the formula for A.

U6

Why is "instantaneously at rest" v = 0, and not s = 0?

Show answer

s is where the particle is; v is how fast it is moving. At rest means not moving, so v = 0, even if it is far from O.

"Instantaneously" means it is only stopped for an instant: typically it is turning round, like a ball at the top of its flight. s = 0 would mean it is passing through O, possibly at full speed.

U7

Why does the maximum velocity happen when the acceleration is zero?

Show answer

Acceleration is the gradient of the velocity: a = dv/dt. Velocity is at a maximum when its graph is at a peak, which is where its gradient is zero.

It is the same idea as dy/dx = 0 at a stationary point, one level up: v is to a what y is to dy/dx.

U8

Why does a question like 2018 2H Q17(b), "the range of values of x for which C has a negative gradient", lead to a quadratic inequality?

Show answer

"Negative gradient" means dy/dx < 0. Differentiating a cubic gives a quadratic, so the condition is a quadratic inequality, solved exactly as in module 5.

dy/dx is a U-shaped quadratic, so it is negative between its roots. Those roots are the x-coordinates of the maximum and minimum. The curve goes downhill between them.
