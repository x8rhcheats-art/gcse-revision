Module 4 of 14 · Edexcel IGCSE Mathematics A 4MA1 (Higher)

Algebraic Manipulation

Expanding, factorising, algebraic fractions, rearranging formulae and proof. These are the skills that every later algebra question depends on. On their own they are worth 2 to 4 marks each and appear on every paper. The Higher-only versions to master are triple brackets, quadratics with a ≠ 1, the difference of two squares, fractions over quadratic denominators, and formulae where the subject appears twice.

From the 2018 May 2H mark scheme, on "Make a the subject of M = ac − bd":

"[must have been seen with a = to award accuracy mark]"

An answer of (M + bd)/c on its own loses the A1. The subject has to be stated: a = (M + bd)/c. The 2018 May 1H scheme, for the triple expansion, required that "the correct number of terms must be present". That means 8 terms before collecting, or 6 after the first two brackets are multiplied by the third.

02How it actually works

Expanding and factorising are the same process run in opposite directions, and every section below comes back to that. Algebraic fractions are ordinary fraction rules applied after factorising. Rearranging is solving an equation with letters left in. Proof is expanding until the answer can be seen.

Expanding single and double brackets

Spec 2.2C, 2.2E · 2015 Edexcel Jan 4HR Q11(c)

Every term in one bracket multiplies every term in the other; then collect like terms.

  1. Single bracket: multiply the outside term by each inside term, and watch the signs: −2(x − 4) = −2x + 8.
  2. Double bracket: four products (a grid or FOIL), then collect the two middle terms.
  3. A squared bracket is two brackets: (3x − 2)2 = (3x − 2)(3x − 2).

Worked example — expand (2x − 3)(x + 5) and (3x − 2)2

2x × x = 2x2; 2x × 5 = 10x; −3 × x = −3x; −3 × 5 = −15 (M1 for 4 terms, 3 correct)

= 2x2 + 7x − 15 (A1)

(3x − 2)(3x − 2) = 9x2 − 6x − 6x + 4 = 9x2 − 12x + 4

✗ (3x − 2)2 = 9x2 + 4 or 9x2 − 4. The middle terms are missing.
✗ −2(x − 4) = −2x − 8. The sign on the second term is wrong.
✓ Write the squared bracket out twice, and multiply the sign with the number.

Expanding triple brackets

Spec 2.2A (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q11(a) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q11

Multiply two of the brackets first and simplify, then multiply that quadratic by the third bracket.

  1. Choose two brackets and expand them to a simplified quadratic (3 terms).
  2. Multiply each of those 3 terms by each term of the third bracket, giving 6 terms.
  3. Collect like terms: x3, then x2, then x, then the number.
  4. Check: substitute x = 1 into the question and into your answer. They must agree.

Worked example — expand and simplify (x + 2)(x − 3)(x + 4)

(x + 2)(x − 3) = x2 − 3x + 2x − 6 = x2 − x − 6 (M1 for expanding any two brackets, at least 3 of 4 terms correct)

(x2 − x − 6)(x + 4) = x3 + 4x2 − x2 − 4x − 6x − 24 (M1 for 6 terms, at least half correct)

= x3 + 3x2 − 10x − 24 (A1)

Check with x = 1: 3 × (−2) × 5 = −30, and 1 + 3 − 10 − 24 = −30 ✓

Factorising — common factors

Spec 2.2D · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q1(c) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q6(a)

"Factorise fully" means take out the highest common factor of the numbers and the lowest power of every shared letter.

  1. HCF of the numbers.
  2. For each letter in every term, take the lowest power.
  3. Divide each term by that factor to fill the bracket.
  4. Check the bracket has no common factor left, then expand to check.

Worked example — factorise fully 15a3b − 25a2b2

HCF of 15 and 25 is 5; lowest powers a2 and b → 5a2b

15a3b ÷ 5a2b = 3a; 25a2b2 ÷ 5a2b = 5b

5a2b(3a − 5b) (M1 A1; the 2018 2H scheme gave the M1 for "any correct partially factorised expression with at least 2 terms in the common factor", such as 5a(3a2b − 5ab2) here)

✗ 5a(3a2b − 5ab2). Correct, but not fully factorised, so 1 mark out of 2.
✓ Check the bracket: if every term inside still shares a factor, take it out.

Factorising quadratics x2 + bx + c

Spec 2.2F · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q3(d)

Find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b.

  1. List factor pairs of c, with signs.
  2. Pick the pair that adds to b.
  3. Write (x + p)(x + q) and expand to check.

Worked example — factorise x2 + x − 30

Multiply to −30, add to +1: 6 and −5 (M1 for (x ± 6)(x ± 5))

(x + 6)(x − 5) (A1)

Factorising quadratics with a ≠ 1

Spec 2.2B (Higher) · 2015 Edexcel Jan 4HR Q11(d)

Multiply a × c, find two numbers with that product and sum b, split the middle term, then factorise in pairs.

  1. Work out a × c.
  2. Find two numbers that multiply to ac and add to b.
  3. Split bx into those two terms.
  4. Factorise the first pair and the second pair; the same bracket appears in both.
  5. Take out that bracket. Expand to check.

Worked example — factorise 6x2 + x − 12

ac = 6 × −12 = −72; two numbers with product −72 and sum +1: 9 and −8

6x2 + 9x − 8x − 12 = 3x(2x + 3) − 4(2x + 3) (M1 for splitting correctly or for (3x ± 4)(2x ± 3))

= (3x − 4)(2x + 3) (A1)

✗ (6x − 4)(x + 3). This expands to 6x2 + 14x − 12. Always expand to check the middle term.
✓ With a ≠ 1, use the ac method rather than guessing.

Difference of two squares

Spec 2.2B (Higher) · 2014 Edexcel Jan 3H Q19 · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q15(b)

Anything squared minus anything squared factorises into (first − second)(first + second).

Difference of two squares — memorisea2 − b2 = (a − b)(a + b)a and b can be numbers, terms or whole brackets · a2 + b2 does not factorise
  1. Take out any common factor first (2x2 − 50 = 2(x2 − 25)).
  2. Identify what is being squared: 25x2 = (5x)2, 36y2 = (6y)2.
  3. Write (a − b)(a + b). If a and b are brackets, simplify inside each factor.

Worked example — factorise 25x2 − 36y2, 2x2 − 50 and (x + 5)2 − (x − 1)2

25x2 − 36y2 = (5x − 6y)(5x + 6y)

2x2 − 50 = 2(x2 − 25) = 2(x − 5)(x + 5)

[(x + 5) − (x − 1)][(x + 5) + (x − 1)] (M1) = 6(2x + 4) = 12(x + 2) (A1)

✗ (x + 5) − (x − 1) = 4. The bracket sign flips −1 to +1, so it is 6.
✓ Put every bracket in its own bracket before simplifying: (x + 5) − (x − 1) = x + 5 − x + 1 = 6.

Simplifying algebraic fractions

Spec 2.2C (Higher)

You can only cancel factors, never individual terms, so factorise the top and the bottom completely first.

  1. Factorise the numerator fully.
  2. Factorise the denominator fully.
  3. Cancel any bracket (or factor) that appears in both.
  4. Leave the answer factorised.

Worked example — simplify fully (3x2 − 6x)/(x2 − 4)

Top: 3x(x − 2) (B1). Bottom: (x − 2)(x + 2) (B1)

Cancel (x − 2): 3x/(x + 2) (B1)

✗ (x2 + 3)/(x2 + 5) = 3/5, "cancelling the x2". Terms joined by + or − cannot be cancelled.
✓ Cancel only whole factors, after factorising.

Adding and subtracting algebraic fractions

Spec 2.2C (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q15(b)

Put both fractions over a common denominator, the product of the denominators or, better, their LCM, then combine the numerators.

  1. Factorise each denominator; the common denominator is every factor that appears, used once.
  2. Multiply each numerator by whatever its denominator is missing.
  3. Combine the numerators in a bracket, watching the minus sign, and simplify.
  4. Leave the denominator factorised.

Worked example — write 2/(x + 3) + 5/(x − 1) as a single fraction

Common denominator (x + 3)(x − 1) (M1)

[2(x − 1) + 5(x + 3)] / [(x + 3)(x − 1)] (M1)

= (7x + 13)/((x + 3)(x − 1)) (A1)

With a quadratic denominator, as in 2018: 1/(9x2 − 25) = 1/((3x − 5)(3x + 5)) and 1/(6x + 10) = 1/(2(3x + 5)), so the common denominator is 2(3x − 5)(3x + 5), not the product of the two.

✗ [2(x − 1) − 5(x + 3)] written as 2x − 2 − 5x + 15. The minus must go through the whole bracket, so it is −5x − 15.
✓ Bracket each new numerator before combining.

Multiplying and dividing algebraic fractions

Spec 2.2C (Higher) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q18

Multiply tops and bottoms, or, to divide, flip the second fraction and multiply, factorising everything so you can cancel.

  1. Dividing? Turn the second fraction upside down and change ÷ to ×.
  2. Factorise every numerator and denominator.
  3. Cancel common factors across the whole product.
  4. Multiply what is left.

Worked example — simplify (x2 − 16)/(3x + 12) ÷ (x2 − 3x − 4)/(6x)

= (x2 − 16)/(3x + 12) × 6x/(x2 − 3x − 4) (M1)

= (x − 4)(x + 4)/[3(x + 4)] × 6x/[(x − 4)(x + 1)] (M1 for correct factorising)

Cancel (x − 4), (x + 4) and 3: 2x/(x + 1) (A1)

Changing the subject — subject appears once

Spec 2.3F, 2.3A (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q1(a) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q13

Undo what has been done to the subject, in reverse order, doing the same to both sides.

  1. Clear fractions by multiplying both sides by the denominator.
  2. Get the term containing the subject on its own (add or subtract).
  3. Divide by its coefficient.
  4. Undo any power or root last: square root to undo a square (± or "positive" as stated), square to undo a root, cube root to undo a cube.
  5. Write "subject = …". The A1 depends on it.

Worked example — make r the subject of V = πr2h/3

3V = πr2h (M1 for a correct first step)

r2 = 3V/(πh)

r = √(3V/(πh)) (A1)

✗ Stopping at r2 = 3V/(πh). The subject is r, not r2.
✗ Writing only "√(3V/(πh))". The 2018 scheme required the answer "seen with a =".

Changing the subject — subject appears twice

Spec 2.3A (Higher) · 2016 Edexcel Jan 4HR Q19 · 2014 Edexcel Jan 3H Q16

Gather every term containing the subject on one side, then factorise the subject out.

  1. Clear any fraction or root first (multiply up, or square both sides).
  2. Expand brackets.
  3. Collect all terms with the subject on one side and everything else on the other.
  4. Factorise: take the subject out as a common factor.
  5. Divide by the bracket.

Worked example — make q the subject of p = (2q + 1)/(q − 3)

p(q − 3) = 2q + 1 (M1 for clearing the fraction)

pq − 3p = 2q + 1

pq − 2q = 1 + 3p (M1 for collecting q terms on one side)

q(p − 2) = 1 + 3p (M1 for factorising)

q = (1 + 3p)/(p − 2) (A1)

✗ q = (1 + 3p + 2q)/p. q is still on both sides, so it is not the subject.
✓ If the subject appears twice, you must factorise it out. That step is the method mark.

Algebraic proof

Spec 2.2E (Higher)

Write the general numbers in algebra, expand and simplify, then show the result has the required form and say so in words.

  1. Choose the representation: any integer n; consecutive n, n + 1; even 2n; odd 2n + 1; consecutive odd 2n + 1, 2n + 3.
  2. Write the expression the question describes and expand it.
  3. Simplify and factorise to show the form: a multiple of 8 is 8 × (an integer); odd is 2 × (integer) + 1.
  4. Finish with a sentence: "8(n + 1) is a multiple of 8 because n + 1 is an integer."

Worked example — prove that (2n + 3)2 − (2n − 3)2 is a multiple of 12 for all integers n

(4n2 + 12n + 9) − (4n2 − 12n + 9) (M1 for expanding both)

= 24n (A1)

= 12 × 2n, and 2n is an integer, so it is a multiple of 12 (A1 for the conclusion)

✗ "n = 1 gives 24, n = 2 gives 48, so it is true." Examples are not a proof.
✗ Using n and n + 1 for two odd numbers. They are not both odd.
✓ Use algebra for the general case, and finish with a sentence.

04Understanding it

U1

Why does (x + 3)2 not equal x2 + 9? Use a number to show it.

Show answer

(x + 3)2 = (x + 3)(x + 3) = x2 + 3x + 3x + 9 = x2 + 6x + 9. The two middle terms come from the "cross" products.

With x = 1: (1 + 3)2 = 16, but 12 + 9 = 10. The missing 6 is the 6x term.

U2

Why does a triple expansion like (x + 2)(x − 3)(x + 4) have to give an x3 term and a constant of −24 before you do any working?

Show answer

The x3 term comes only from x × x × x, and the constant comes only from 2 × (−3) × 4 = −24.

So you can check your final answer at a glance: leading term x3, constant −24. If either is wrong, there is a slip in the working.

U3

In the ac method for 6x2 + x − 12, why do you look for two numbers that multiply to −72?

Show answer

If 6x2 + x − 12 = (px + q)(rx + s), then expanding gives pr x2 + (ps + qr)x + qs. The two middle products ps and qr multiply to pr × qs = 6 × (−12) = −72, and they add to the middle coefficient, 1.

So the pair with product ac and sum b is exactly the two parts of the middle term, and splitting there lets the grouping work.

U4

Why does x2 − 9 factorise, but x2 + 9 does not?

Show answer

(x − 3)(x + 3) = x2 + 3x − 3x − 9 = x2 − 9. The middle terms cancel because the signs differ.

For x2 + 9 you would need two numbers that multiply to +9 and add to 0, which is impossible for real numbers. There is no difference of squares, because it is a sum.

U5

Why can you cancel (x − 2) in 3x(x − 2)/((x − 2)(x + 2)), but not the x in (x + 3)/(x + 5)?

Show answer

(x − 2) is a factor of both top and bottom, since everything is multiplied by it. Dividing top and bottom by the same factor leaves the value unchanged.

In (x + 3)/(x + 5), x is a term, added to something else. Check with x = 1: 4/6 = 2/3, but "cancelling x" gives 3/5. Cancelling terms changes the value.

U6

For 1/(9x2 − 25) − 1/(6x + 10), why is the best common denominator 2(3x − 5)(3x + 5) and not (9x2 − 25)(6x + 10)?

Show answer

Factorised, the denominators are (3x − 5)(3x + 5) and 2(3x + 5). They share (3x + 5). The lowest common multiple needs each factor only once: 2(3x − 5)(3x + 5).

The product works too, but it contains (3x + 5) twice, so you would have to cancel at the end. That is extra algebra where slips happen, and the "simplest form" mark depends on it.

U7

When the subject appears twice, why must you factorise it out rather than just divide?

Show answer

After collecting, you have something like pq − 2q = 1 + 3p. You cannot divide by "p" or by "−2" alone, because each only removes q from one term.

Factorising gives q(p − 2) = 1 + 3p. Now q is multiplied by one thing, (p − 2), so dividing by it leaves q on its own. Without factorising, the subject never ends up alone on one side.

U8

Why is "2n + 1" a good way to write any odd number, and why don't examples count as a proof?

Show answer

2n is a multiple of 2, so it is always even when n is an integer, and one more than an even number is odd. Every odd number can be written this way, so working with 2n + 1 covers them all at once.

Examples only check the cases you tried. A statement can hold for the first few numbers and fail later. The algebra shows it holds for every integer n.
