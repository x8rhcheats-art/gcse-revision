02How it actually works

Every question here is a triangle with some sides and angles known. If it has a right angle, use Pythagoras (three sides) or SOHCAHTOA (sides and an angle). If it does not, use the sine rule or the cosine rule. In 3D, find a flat right-angled triangle inside the solid and do the same. Keep every value in your calculator until the final answer.

Pythagoras' theorem

Spec 4.8A · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q6 · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q10

In a right-angled triangle, the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides.

Pythagoras' theorem — memorisea2 + b2 = c2c = the hypotenuse, the longest side, opposite the right angle · not on the formula sheet
  1. Find the hypotenuse: the side opposite the right angle.
  2. Finding the hypotenuse: square both sides, add, square root.
  3. Finding a shorter side: square both, subtract the smaller from the hypotenuse, square root.
  4. Check: the hypotenuse must be the longest side.

Worked examples

Shorter sides 6.2 cm and 8.5 cm: c = √(6.22 + 8.52) = √110.69 = 10.5 cm (3 s.f.)   (M1 squares added, M1 square root, A1)

Hypotenuse 14 cm, one side 9.6 cm: a = √(142 − 9.62) = √103.84 = 10.2 cm (3 s.f.)

Pythagoras hides in other shapes: the diagonal of a rectangle, the height of an isosceles triangle (split it in half), the distance between two points, the diameter opposite a right angle in a semicircle.

✗ Adding the squares when you want a shorter side: the answer comes out longer than the hypotenuse.
✗ √(6.2 + 8.5). Square first, add, then root.

SOHCAHTOA: sides and angles in right-angled triangles

Spec 4.8B–C · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q8 · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q7 · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q9

In a right-angled triangle, each trig ratio is one side divided by another, and it depends only on the angle.

Trigonometric ratios — memorisesin θ = O/H  ·  cos θ = A/H  ·  tan θ = O/AO = opposite the angle · A = adjacent (next to the angle, not the hypotenuse) · H = hypotenuse · "SOH CAH TOA"
  1. Label the sides O, A and H from the angle you are using.
  2. Tick the two sides involved: the one you know and the one you want (or both, for an angle).
  3. Pick the ratio with those two letters.
  4. Write the equation, e.g. cos 52 = 12.6/x, then rearrange.
  5. Angle: use the inverse: θ = sin−1(O/H), and so on.

Worked examples

Opposite from the hypotenuse: H = 18 cm, angle 40°. O = 18 × sin 40 = 11.6 cm   (M1 for sin 40 = O/18, M1 for 18 × sin 40, A1)

Hypotenuse from the adjacent: A = 7.5 cm, angle 28°. cos 28 = 7.5/H, so H = 7.5 ÷ cos 28 = 8.49 cm.

Angle: O = 5 cm, A = 8 cm. tan θ = 5/8, so θ = tan−1(0.625) = 32.0°.

2018 2H Q8 (x = 12.6 ÷ cos 52) accepted "20.4 – 20.5", so full accuracy mattered.

✗ x = 12.6 × cos 52. When the unknown is on the bottom, divide.
✗ Calculator in radians. Check that sin 30 = 0.5 before the exam starts.
✗ Rounding the first side to 4.2 in 2018 1H Q9, then using it for the second side. Keep 4.1589… in the calculator.

Angles of elevation and depression

Spec 4.8B (Higher) · 2013 Edexcel Jan 4H Q25 (old spec)

Angles of elevation and depression are both measured from the horizontal: up for elevation, down for depression.

  1. Draw the horizontal line from the observer's eye.
  2. Elevation is the angle up from it to the object. Depression is the angle down from it.
  3. The angle of depression from A to B equals the angle of elevation from B to A (alternate angles, since horizontals are parallel).
  4. Complete the right-angled triangle with the vertical and use SOHCAHTOA.

Worked example: from the top of a 30 m cliff, the angle of depression of a boat is 24°. How far is the boat from the foot of the cliff?

The angle of elevation of the cliff top from the boat is also 24°. The cliff is opposite that angle and the distance is adjacent.

tan 24 = 30/d, so d = 30 ÷ tan 24 = 67.4 m (3 s.f.)

Jan 13 4H Q25 had two angles from the same point: set up tan for each (both with the same horizontal distance), and use the difference of the two heights. Drill D6 is one.

✗ Measuring the angle of depression from the vertical cliff face. It is always from the horizontal.

Bearings with trigonometry

Spec 4.4D, 4.8C · 2017 Edexcel Jan 4HR Q9 · 2015 Edexcel Jan 3HR Q11 (old spec)

A bearing problem is a triangle problem with a north line drawn at each point: find the angle in the triangle, then convert it to a bearing.

  1. Draw a north line at every point named in the question.
  2. Put the distances on the diagram. East–west and north–south distances make a right angle.
  3. Find the angle inside the triangle with trig.
  4. Convert: the bearing is measured clockwise from north at the "from" point. Use 180° and 360° and alternate angles between parallel north lines.

Worked example: B is 30 km east and 50 km north of A. Work out the bearing of B from A, and of A from B.

At A, the angle between north and AB: tan θ = 30/50, so θ = tan−1(0.6) = 30.96…°   (M1, A1)

Bearing of B from A = 031° (nearest degree)   (A1)

Bearing of A from B = 031 + 180 = 211° (to the nearest degree, 30.96 + 180 = 210.96)

✗ Working out the angle from the east line and calling it the bearing. tan−1(50/30) = 59° is measured from east, not north.
✓ Always check which angle you found against the north line on your sketch.

Sine, cosine and tangent of obtuse angles

Spec 4.8A (Higher) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q21

For an obtuse angle, sin is positive and equals sin of its supplement, cos is negative, and tan is negative.

Supplementary angles — memorisesin(180° − x) = sin x  ·  cos(180° − x) = −cos xsin 150° = sin 30° = 0.5 · cos 120° = −cos 60° = −0.5

This matters in two places:

  1. Cosine rule for an angle: a negative cosine means the angle is obtuse, and cos−1 gives it correctly. Nothing extra to do.
  2. Sine rule for an angle: sin−1 only ever gives an acute angle. If the angle could be obtuse, the other answer is 180° minus the calculator's.

Worked example: in triangle ABC, a = 11 cm, b = 8 cm, angle B = 40°. Find angle A.

sin A / 11 = sin 40 / 8, so sin A = 11 × sin 40 ÷ 8 = 0.8838…

A = sin−1(0.8838…) = 62.1° or 180 − 62.1 = 117.9°. Both give a valid triangle, since 40 + 117.9 < 180.

The question will tell you which: "angle A is obtuse", or a diagram. In SAM 2H Q21 the question said angle LMN is obtuse, so the angle found by the sine rule (at L) had to be acute.

The sine rule

Spec 4.8C (Higher) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q21

In any triangle, each side divided by the sine of its opposite angle gives the same number.

The sine rule — on the formula sheeta/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin Cside a is opposite angle A · for an angle, flip it: sin A/a = sin B/b
  1. Use it when you have a side and its opposite angle, plus one more side or angle.
  2. Label: each side takes the letter of the angle opposite it.
  3. Side: a/sin A = b/sin B, so b = a × sin B ÷ sin A.
  4. Angle: sin B = b × sin A ÷ a, then sin−1. Think about the obtuse alternative.

Worked example: angle A = 42°, angle B = 71°, a = 8 cm. Find b.

b/sin 71 = 8/sin 42   (M1)

b = 8 × sin 71 ÷ sin 42 = 11.3 cm (3 s.f.)   (A1)

The cosine rule

Spec 4.8C (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q18 · 2016 Edexcel Jan 4HR Q21 (old spec)

The cosine rule is Pythagoras with a correction term for angles that are not 90°.

The cosine rule — on the formula sheeta2 = b2 + c2 − 2bc cos AA is the angle BETWEEN sides b and c, opposite side a · if A = 90°, cos A = 0 and it is Pythagoras
Cosine rule for an angle — memorisecos A = (b2 + c2 − a2) ÷ 2bca is the side OPPOSITE the angle you want · it is the one subtracted
  1. Side: use it when you know two sides and the angle between them.
  2. Angle: use it when you know all three sides.
  3. Type the whole of b2 + c2 − 2bc cos A in one go, then square root.

Worked example 1: b = 7 cm, c = 10 cm, angle A = 52°. Find a.

a2 = 72 + 102 − 2 × 7 × 10 × cos 52 = 62.81…   (M1)

a = 7.93 cm (3 s.f.)   (A1)

Worked example 2: a triangle has sides 5 cm, 7 cm and 9 cm. Find its largest angle.

The largest angle is opposite the longest side, 9 cm.

cos θ = (52 + 72 − 92) ÷ (2 × 5 × 7) = −7/70 = −0.1   (M1)

θ = cos−1(−0.1)   (M1) = 95.7°   (A1). The negative cosine is why it is obtuse.

✗ Working 49 + 100 − 140 = 9, then × cos 52. The calculator must do 2bc cos A before subtracting: type it all in one line.
✗ Putting the longest side in the b2 + c2 part when you want the largest angle. It is the one you subtract.

Area of a triangle: ½ab sin C

Spec 4.8E (Higher) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q21 · 2016 Edexcel Jan 3H Q20 (old spec)

The area of any triangle is half the product of two sides times the sine of the angle between them.

Area of a triangle — on the formula sheetArea = ½ab sin CC is the angle BETWEEN sides a and b · ½ × base × height with height = b sin C

Worked example: two sides 9 cm and 13 cm with an angle of 58° between them

Area = ½ × 9 × 13 × sin 58 = 49.6 cm2 (3 s.f.)

If the angle you have is not between the two sides (as in SAM 2H Q21 and Jan 16 3H Q20), use the sine rule to find another angle, then 180° minus the two angles gives the included one. That is a 6-mark question: M1 sine rule, M1 sin−1, A1 the angle, M1 the included angle, M1 ½ab sin C, A1.

Pythagoras in three dimensions

Spec 4.8D (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q19 · 2013 Edexcel Jan 3H Q21 (old spec)

A length in 3D is found by using Pythagoras twice, once across a flat face and once up to the point.

Diagonal of a cuboid — memorised = √(a2 + b2 + c2)a, b, c = length, width, height · the space diagonal from one corner to the opposite corner
  1. Find a right-angled triangle that contains the length you want. Sketch it flat.
  2. One of its sides is usually a diagonal across the base. Find that first with Pythagoras.
  3. Use Pythagoras again in the upright triangle.

Worked example: cuboid 4 cm by 3 cm by 12 cm. Find the space diagonal.

Base diagonal = √(42 + 32) = 5 cm. Space diagonal = √(52 + 122) = 13 cm.

In one step: √(16 + 9 + 144) = √169 = 13.

The angle between a line and a plane

Spec 4.8F (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q19 · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q19 · 2013 Edexcel Jan 3H Q21 (old spec)

The angle between a line and a plane is the angle between the line and its shadow on the plane, directly underneath it.

  1. From the top end of the line, drop a perpendicular to the plane. Where it lands is the foot.
  2. Join the foot to the point where the line meets the plane. That is the shadow (the projection).
  3. The line, its shadow and the perpendicular make a right-angled triangle, with the right angle at the foot.
  4. Find two of its sides (usually with Pythagoras), then use SOHCAHTOA for the angle at the point where the line meets the plane.

Worked example (2018 2H Q19, numbers changed): triangular prism with AF = 6 cm vertical, AB = 15 cm, BC = 8 cm. E is vertically above D. Angle FAB = angle ADC = angle BCD = 90°. Find the angle between BE and the plane ABCD.

E is above D, so the shadow of BE on the base is BD, and DE = AF = 6 cm.

BD2 = 152 + 82 = 289, so BD = 17 cm   (M1 for a complete method for BD or BE)

tan θ = DE/BD = 6/17   (M1)

θ = 19.4° (1 d.p.)   (A1)

SAM 1H Q19 is the same idea on a cone: the slant height from the curved surface area (πrl = 130), then sin(AVO) = 4.5 ÷ l, and angle AVB is double AVO. The spec says the angle between two planes is not required.

✗ Using the angle at the wrong end: the angle is at the point where the line meets the plane, not at the top.
✗ Using a side edge (BC or AB) as the shadow. The shadow goes to the point directly below the top of the line.

Problems with more than one triangle

Spec 4.8C, 4.4D · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q21 · 2017 Edexcel Jan 4HR Q9 (old spec)

In a long problem, each triangle gives you one new length or angle that unlocks the next triangle.

  1. Draw the diagram. For journeys, draw north lines at every turning point.
  2. Find the angle inside the triangle at each turn, using bearings and 180°.
  3. Pick the rule for that triangle (see the table above). Keep full calculator values.
  4. Convert back to a bearing at the end if asked.

Worked example: a ship sails 8 km from S on a bearing of 040° to T, then 11 km on a bearing of 115° to F. Find the distance SF and the bearing of F from S.

At T, the bearing back to S is 040 + 180 = 220°. The angle STF = 220 − 115 = 105°.

SF2 = 82 + 112 − 2 × 8 × 11 × cos 105 = 230.55…   (M1)

SF = 15.2 km (3 s.f.)   (A1)

Sine rule: sin(TSF) = 11 × sin 105 ÷ 15.18… so angle TSF = 44.4°   (M1)

Bearing of F from S = 040 + 44.4 = 084° (nearest degree)   (A1)

03Cheat sheet

The rules and when to use them

Pythagoras (memorise): a2 + b2 = c2, c the hypotenuse. Add for the hypotenuse, subtract for a shorter side.

SOHCAHTOA (memorise): sin = O/H · cos = A/H · tan = O/A. Unknown on the bottom: divide.

Sine rule (formula sheet): a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C. Needs a side with its opposite angle.

Cosine rule (formula sheet): a2 = b2 + c2 − 2bc cos A. Angle version cos A = (b2 + c2 − a2)/2bc is not on the sheet.

Area (formula sheet): ½ab sin C, C between a and b.

Obtuse: sin(180 − x) = sin x · a negative cos means an obtuse angle.

3D: d = √(a2 + b2 + c2) · line and plane: the angle between the line and its shadow.

Bearings: north lines at every point · clockwise from north · three figures · back bearing ± 180.

04Understanding it

U1

Why does sin 40° have the same value in every right-angled triangle with a 40° angle, however big the triangle?

Show answer

All right-angled triangles with a 40° angle have the same three angles (90°, 40°, 50°), so they are similar. Every side is multiplied by the same scale factor.

A ratio of two sides, like opposite ÷ hypotenuse, has the scale factor on the top and the bottom, so it cancels. The ratio depends only on the angle. That is what makes trigonometry possible.

U2

Show that the cosine rule turns into Pythagoras' theorem when the angle is 90°. What does that tell you about the sign of the correction term?

Show answer

a2 = b2 + c2 − 2bc cos A. When A = 90°, cos 90° = 0, so a2 = b2 + c2: Pythagoras.

When A is acute, cos A is positive, so a2 is less than b2 + c2: the opposite side is shorter than for a right angle. When A is obtuse, cos A is negative, so the term is added and the side is longer. Both match what you would expect from opening or closing the angle.

U3

Why does the sine rule sometimes give two possible angles, while the cosine rule never does?

Show answer

sin x = sin(180° − x), so an acute angle and its obtuse partner have the same sine. Knowing sin B does not tell you which. The calculator's sin−1 always gives the acute one.

Cosines of acute angles are positive and of obtuse angles negative, so every value of cos between −1 and 1 belongs to exactly one angle between 0° and 180°. cos−1 picks the right one.

U4

Where does Area = ½ab sin C come from?

Show answer

Take side a as the base. Drop a perpendicular from the opposite vertex to the base. It makes a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse b and angle C, so the height is b sin C (SOHCAHTOA: opposite = hypotenuse × sin).

Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × a × b sin C. That is also why C must be the angle between a and b.

U5

A student rounds the first side in a two-step trig problem to 3 s.f. and gets a final answer that is 0.1 out. Why, and how is it avoided?

Show answer

The rounding error in the first value is carried into the second calculation and can grow, especially after multiplying by a large number or dividing by a small sine. The final answer lands outside the mark scheme's range, and the A1 is lost.

Store the unrounded value in the calculator's memory (or use the ANS key), write the rounded value in your working with "…" after it, and round only the final answer.

U6

In the angle between a line and a plane, why must you use the line's shadow, and not any other line in the plane?

Show answer

A line meets a plane at one point, and makes different angles with the different lines through that point in the plane. The angle "between the line and the plane" means the smallest of these angles.

The smallest is with the line directly under it: its projection. That is the only choice that makes a right-angled triangle with the perpendicular, so SOHCAHTOA applies.

U7

Why is the bearing of A from B always 180° different from the bearing of B from A?

Show answer

The north lines at A and B are parallel. The line AB crosses them both. The direction from B back to A points exactly the opposite way to the direction from A to B.

Turning to face the opposite way is a turn of 180°, so the back bearing is the bearing ± 180°. Use + if the first bearing is under 180°, − if it is over.

U8

For the largest angle of a triangle with sides 8, 10 and 14, why is it enough to find one angle, and which one?

Show answer

In any triangle, the largest angle is opposite the longest side. So the angle opposite 14 is the only one needed.

cos θ = (82 + 102 − 142)/(2 × 8 × 10) = −32/160 = −0.2, giving θ = 101.5°. The negative cosine confirms it is obtuse, and a triangle can have only one obtuse angle.
