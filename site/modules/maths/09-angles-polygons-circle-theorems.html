02How it actually works

Every question in this module is angle chasing: find one angle, write down why, use it to find the next. The facts come in three layers. Lines and triangles come first, then polygons, then circles. The exam wants a reason at each step, so learn each fact with its sentence.

Angle facts and parallel lines

Spec 4.1A–D · 2016 Edexcel Jan 4HR Q2 (old spec)

Angles on a straight line add up to 180°, angles at a point add up to 360°, and parallel lines make pairs of equal or supplementary angles.

FactReason to write
Two angles making a straight lineAngles on a straight line add up to 180°
Angles all the way round a pointAngles at a point add up to 360°
The X shape: opposite anglesVertically opposite angles are equal
The Z shape between parallel linesAlternate angles are equal
The F shape: same position at each crossingCorresponding angles are equal
The C (or U) shape between parallel linesCo-interior angles add up to 180° (also called allied angles)
  1. Mark every angle you find on the diagram as you go.
  2. Write each step as a line: angle = value (reason).
  3. Use the full reason. The letter shapes (Z, F, C) are for you, not the examiner.

Worked example: parallel lines

AB is parallel to CD. A straight line crosses AB at P and CD at Q. Angle BPQ = 118°. Find angle PQD and angle CQP.

Angle PQD = 180° − 118° = 62° (co-interior angles add up to 180°)   (B1 for the angle, B1 for the reason)

Angle CQP = 118° (alternate angles are equal), or 180° − 62° (angles on a straight line add up to 180°).

✗ "Z angles", "F angles", "C angles". These are not accepted as reasons.
✗ "Alternate angles add up to 180°". Alternate angles are equal. Co-interior angles add up to 180°.
✓ "Alternate angles are equal" and "co-interior angles add up to 180°".

Triangles and quadrilaterals

Spec 4.1C–D, 4.2A–C

The angles in a triangle add up to 180°, the angles in a quadrilateral add up to 360°, and an exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior opposite angles.

An isosceles triangle has two equal sides and two equal base angles. An equilateral triangle has all sides equal and all angles 60°. The exterior angle fact saves a step: the angle outside the triangle at one corner equals the two inside angles at the other corners added together.

Worked example: the four angles of a quadrilateral are 90°, (x + 15)°, (x + 25)° and (x + 35)°. Find x.

90 + x + 15 + x + 25 + x + 35 = 360   (M1: angles in a quadrilateral add up to 360°)

3x + 165 = 360, so 3x = 195 and x = 65   (A1)

Check: 90 + 80 + 90 + 100 = 360. This example is from the spec's own notes.

Polygons: interior and exterior angles

Spec 4.2D–E · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q12 · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q11 · 2015 Edexcel Jan 3HR Q14 (old spec)

A polygon with n sides splits into n − 2 triangles, so its interior angles add up to (n − 2) × 180°, and its exterior angles always add up to 360°.

Sum of interior angles — memoriseS = (n − 2) × 180°n = number of sides · pentagon 540° · hexagon 720° · octagon 1080° · the spec writes it as (2n − 4) right angles
Exterior angle of a regular polygon — memoriseexterior angle = 360° ÷ n  ·  interior angle = 180° − exterior angleinterior + exterior = 180° at every vertex, because together they make a straight line
  1. Irregular polygon: work out the angle sum, add the known angles, subtract.
  2. Regular polygon, given n: exterior = 360 ÷ n, then interior = 180 − exterior.
  3. Regular polygon, given an angle: go through the exterior angle. n = 360 ÷ exterior.

Worked example 1: a hexagon has angles 118°, 132°, 95°, 127°, y° and y°. Find y.

Sum = (6 − 2) × 180 = 720°   (M1)

720 − (118 + 132 + 95 + 127) = 720 − 472 = 248   (M1)

y = 248 ÷ 2 = 124   (M1, A1)

Worked example 2: each interior angle of a regular polygon with n sides is 144°. Find the interior angle of a regular polygon with 2n sides.

Exterior angle = 180 − 144 = 36°   (M1)

n = 360 ÷ 36 = 10, so 2n = 20   (M1)

Exterior angle of the 20-sided polygon = 360 ÷ 20 = 18°   (M1)

Interior angle = 180 − 18 = 162°   (A1)

The 2018 2H Q12 scheme warned: "splitting the shape incorrectly (FDC and DEA are not straight lines) gains no marks for angles calculated from false information."

✗ Assuming lines meet in a straight line because they look as if they do.
✗ Using 360 ÷ n for the interior angle. That is the exterior angle.
✓ Only use a straight line, a parallel pair or an equal length if the question or the diagram's markings say so.

Bearings

Spec 4.4D · 2015 Edexcel Jan 3HR Q11 (old spec)

A bearing is an angle measured clockwise from north, written with three figures.

  1. "The bearing of B from A": stand at A, face north, turn clockwise until you face B.
  2. Draw a north line at the point you are measuring from.
  3. Always write three figures: 064°, not 64°.
  4. Back bearing (A from B): add 180° to the bearing of B from A. If that goes over 360°, subtract 180° instead.

Worked example: the bearing of B from A is 064°. The bearing of C from A is 150°.

Bearing of A from B = 064 + 180 = 244°.

Angle BAC = 150 − 64 = 86°, the angle between the two directions at A.

Bearing of A from C = 150 + 180 = 330°.

North lines are parallel, so alternate and co-interior angles appear at every bearing question. Bearings that need trigonometry or the sine and cosine rules are in module 11.

Constructions with ruler and compasses

Spec 4.5A–D · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q5 · 2016 Edexcel Jan 3H Q6 (old spec)

Every construction works by drawing arcs of equal radius, because all the points that are the same distance from two fixed points lie on one straight line: the perpendicular bisector.

Perpendicular bisector of AB:

  1. Open the compasses to more than half of AB.
  2. With the point on A, draw an arc above and below the line.
  3. Keep the same radius. With the point on B, draw arcs crossing the first two.
  4. Join the two crossing points with a straight line.

Bisector of angle ABC:

  1. With the point on B, draw an arc crossing both arms BA and BC.
  2. From each of those two crossing points, draw arcs of equal radius that cross each other.
  3. Draw a straight line from B through that crossing point.

Triangle with three given sides: draw one side with a ruler, then arcs of the other two lengths from each end. Where the arcs cross is the third vertex.

SAM 1H Q5 scheme: M1 for "Circular arc, centre B, to intersect both lines AB and BC", then A1 "dep on M1. Full construction shown."

✗ Rubbing out the arcs, or using a protractor. Without the arcs there is no evidence, so no marks.
✓ Leave every arc visible and draw the final line with a ruler.

Loci are not in the 4MA1 content list, so they are not taught here. Scale drawings (spec 4.5C) are measuring with a ruler and a scale such as 1 cm to 5 km.

Congruent shapes

Spec 4.2F–G

Congruent shapes are exactly the same shape and size, so every corresponding side and every corresponding angle is equal.

The spec only asks you to understand the word. The four ways to tell that two triangles are congruent are still worth knowing, because they are how you justify that two lengths or angles in a diagram must be equal:

ConditionWhat must match
SSSAll three sides
SASTwo sides and the angle between them
ASA (or AAS)Two angles and a corresponding side
RHSA right angle, the hypotenuse and one other side

Worked example: ABC is isosceles with AB = AC. M is the midpoint of BC. Show that triangles ABM and ACM are congruent.

AB = AC (given). BM = CM (M is the midpoint). AM is common to both triangles.

So the triangles are congruent (SSS). It follows that angle AMB = angle AMC, and since they make a straight line, each is 90°.

This is beyond what 4MA1 papers have asked, which is why there is no drill question on it. A full congruence proof is a UK GCSE question. On 4MA1 you only need to recognise congruent shapes, for example "two congruent regular pentagons" in a polygon question.

✗ "AAA" as a condition. Three equal angles prove the triangles are similar, not congruent, because one can be an enlargement of the other.
✗ Two sides and an angle that is not between them. Except for RHS, this does not fix the triangle.

Circle vocabulary, chords and tangents

Spec 4.6A–B (Foundation) · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q5

Three facts about chords and tangents need no angle at the circumference, only the centre and the radius.

  1. The angle between a tangent and a radius is 90°.
  2. Two tangents from the same point to a circle are equal in length.
  3. The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord (and the line from the centre to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to it).

A chord joins two points on the circle. An arc is part of the circumference. A sector is the slice between two radii and an arc. A segment is the region between a chord and an arc. A tangent touches the circle at exactly one point.

Two radii from the centre make an isosceles triangle with any chord. Look for that triangle in every circle question: it gives two equal base angles.

Worked example 1: a chord of length 24 cm is in a circle of radius 13 cm. How far is the chord from the centre?

The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord, so it meets the chord 12 cm from each end.

Right-angled triangle with hypotenuse 13 (a radius) and one side 12: distance = √(132 − 122) = √25 = 5 cm.

Worked example 2: PA and PB are tangents from P to a circle, centre O. Angle APB = 50°. Find angle AOB.

Angle OAP = angle OBP = 90° (the angle between a tangent and a radius is 90°).

Angle AOB = 360 − 90 − 90 − 50 = 130° (angles in a quadrilateral add up to 360°).

The angle at the centre, and the angle in a semicircle

Spec 4.6C(i)–(ii) · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q13(a) · 2016 Edexcel Jan 3H Q17(a) (old spec)

The angle an arc makes at the centre is twice the angle it makes at any point on the rest of the circumference.

The two angles must stand on the same arc. Trace the two lines from each end of the arc: to the centre for one angle, to the point on the circumference for the other. The shape they make is an arrowhead or, for a large angle, a reflex angle at the centre.

The angle in a semicircle is the special case where the arc is half the circle. The angle at the centre is then 180°, so the angle at the circumference is 90°. Any triangle drawn on a diameter has a right angle opposite the diameter.

Worked example: L, M and N are on a circle, centre O. Angle MLN = 31°, and L is on the major arc MN. Find angle MON.

Angle MON = 2 × 31 = 62°   (B1)

Reason: the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference   (B1, dependent on the first B1)

2018 1H Q6 used the semicircle fact without saying so: MON was a diameter, so angle MPN = 90° and Pythagoras gave the diameter. That question is worked in module 10.

Angles in the same segment, and cyclic quadrilaterals

Spec 4.6B–C(iii)–(iv) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q16

Angles at the circumference standing on the same arc are equal, and the opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to 180°.

A cyclic quadrilateral has all four vertices on the circle. The centre does not have to be inside it.

  1. Same segment: look for a "bow tie": two triangles on the same chord, with their third vertices on the same side of the chord. The angles at those vertices are equal.
  2. Cyclic quadrilateral: if the two vertices are on opposite sides of the chord, the angles add up to 180°.

Worked example: ABCD is a cyclic quadrilateral. Angle ABC = 104° and angle BAC = 38°.

Angle ADC = 180 − 104 = 76° (opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to 180°).

Angle BDC = angle BAC = 38° (angles in the same segment are equal: both stand on the chord BC, and A and D are on the same side of it).

✗ "Opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral are equal". They add up to 180°. Equal opposite angles is a parallelogram fact.
✗ Calling a quadrilateral cyclic when one corner is the centre O. O is not on the circumference.

The alternate segment theorem

Spec 4.6C(v) · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q13(b) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q16

The angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment.

At the point where the tangent touches, the chord splits the circle into two segments. The angle between the tangent and the chord equals any angle at the circumference that stands on that chord in the other segment, the one on the far side of the chord from the angle.

  1. Find the tangent and the point of contact.
  2. Pick the chord from that point that makes the given angle with the tangent.
  3. Look at the triangle on that chord whose third vertex is on the far side of the chord. The angle at that vertex is equal.

Worked example: A, B, C and D are on a circle. PA is a tangent at A. Angle PAD = 41° and angle BCD = 98°. Calculate angle ADB, giving reasons.

Angle ABD = 41° (alternate segment theorem)   (B1 for the reason)

Angle BAD = 180 − 98 = 82° (opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to 180°)   (B1 for the reason)

Angle ADB = 180 − 82 − 41 = 57° (angles in a triangle add up to 180°)   (M2, A1)

This is the SAM 1H Q16 structure with the numbers changed: the scheme gave B1 for each circle reason and M2 A1 for the calculation.

Intersecting chords

Spec 4.6A (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q22 · 2015 Edexcel Jan 3HR Q19 (old spec)

When two chords cross, the two parts of one chord multiply to the same number as the two parts of the other.

Intersecting chords, inside the circle — memoriseAP × PB = CP × PDchords AB and CD cross at P inside the circle · multiply the two pieces of each chord
Intersecting chords, outside the circle — memorisePA × PB = PC × PDlines from P outside the circle cut it at A, B and at C, D · each is a distance from P: the near part times the WHOLE line
  1. Decide whether the chords meet inside or outside the circle.
  2. Inside: piece × piece = piece × piece.
  3. Outside: (distance to the near point) × (distance to the far point), both measured from the outside point.
  4. If a line passes through the centre, the part inside the circle is a diameter, 2r.

Worked example (2018 2H Q22, numbers changed)

A, D, B and E are on a circle, centre O. AFBC, OEC and OFD are straight lines. AF = 5 cm, FB = 8 cm, BC = 8 cm, FD = 2 cm and CE = x cm. Find x.

The line OFD runs from the centre to the circle, so extended backwards through O it is a diameter. The part on the far side of F is 2r − 2.

5 × 8 = 2 × (2r − 2)   (M1)

40 = 4r − 4, so r = 11   (A1)

From C: CB × CA = CE × (CE + 2r), so 8 × (8 + 8 + 5) = x(x + 22)   (M1)

168 = x2 + 22x, so x2 + 22x − 168 = 0   (A1)

(x − 6)(x + 28) = 0   (M1)

x = 6. The negative root is a length, so reject it.   (A1)

2018 2H Q22 scheme: "Incorrect working giving the radius as 16 cm gains M0A0". 16 was the diameter.

✗ Outside the circle: 8 × 13, using the chord AB instead of the whole line CA.
✓ Both distances start at the outside point: CB × CA.

Why the circle theorems are true

Spec 4.6C · the spec says "formal proof of these theorems is not required" · the school's "Proof of Circle Theorems" worksheet asks for all five

Every circle theorem comes from one idea: two radii make an isosceles triangle.

4MA1 will not ask you to prove these. The school worksheet does, and knowing the proofs is the best protection against misusing the theorems.

Proof: the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference

A, B and C are on a circle, centre O. Join C to O and extend the line to D.

OA = OC (radii), so triangle AOC is isosceles: angle OAC = angle OCA = x.

Angle AOD is an exterior angle of triangle AOC, so angle AOD = x + x = 2x.

In the same way OB = OC, angle OBC = angle OCB = y, and angle BOD = 2y.

Angle AOB = 2x + 2y = 2(x + y) = 2 × angle ACB.

The rest follow from it:

  1. Semicircle: if AB is a diameter, angle AOB = 180°, so the angle at the circumference is 90°.
  2. Same segment: two angles on the same arc are each half of the same angle at the centre, so they are equal.
  3. Cyclic quadrilateral: opposite angles stand on the two arcs, whose angles at the centre add up to 360°. Halving gives 180°.
  4. Alternate segment: draw the diameter from the point of contact. It meets the tangent at 90° and makes a 90° angle in the semicircle. Subtracting from 90° twice gives the result.

04Understanding it

U1

Why do the interior angles of an n-sided polygon add up to (n − 2) × 180°? Why n − 2 and not n?

Show answer

Pick one vertex and draw a diagonal to every other vertex you can. The diagonals split the polygon into triangles, and each triangle's angles add up to 180°.

A diagonal cannot go to the vertex itself or to its two neighbours, so from one vertex you draw n − 3 diagonals, which make n − 2 triangles. A pentagon makes 3 triangles: 540°.

The triangles' angles together fill exactly the polygon's interior angles, no more and no less.

U2

Why do the exterior angles of any polygon add up to 360°, and why is that the quickest route for a regular polygon?

Show answer

Walk round the edge of the polygon. At each corner you turn through the exterior angle. When you get back to the start you face the way you began, so you have turned through one full turn: 360°.

For a regular polygon all the turns are equal, so each is 360 ÷ n. That is one division. Going through the interior sum takes three steps: (n − 2) × 180, then ÷ n. It also gives you n directly from an angle: n = 360 ÷ exterior.

U3

A student writes "angle ACB = angle ADB, angles in the same segment", but C and D are on opposite sides of the chord AB. What has gone wrong?

Show answer

C and D are in different segments. ACBD is then a cyclic quadrilateral with C and D as opposite vertices, so the angles add up to 180°. They are not equal.

They are only equal when both points are on the same arc, on the same side of the chord. Check this before you use the theorem: trace both angles back to the ends of the chord and see which side each vertex is on.

U4

Why must a tangent be perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact?

Show answer

The shortest distance from the centre to a straight line is along the perpendicular. If the tangent were not perpendicular to the radius, some other point of the line would be closer to the centre than the radius.

That point would be inside the circle, so the line would cross the circle and meet it twice. A tangent meets it only once, so the radius must be the perpendicular: 90°.

U5

In the proof that the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference, where exactly is the isosceles triangle used, and why is that enough?

Show answer

Joining the circumference point C to the centre O makes triangles AOC and BOC. OA, OB and OC are all radii, so both triangles are isosceles, with base angles x and y at C.

The exterior angle of each triangle at O is then 2x and 2y. Added together they make the angle at the centre, 2(x + y), while the angle at C is x + y.

Nothing else was assumed, so the result is true wherever C is on the major arc.

U6

Explain why the alternate segment theorem is true, using a diameter from the point of contact.

Show answer

Let the tangent touch at A, and let the chord be AB, with angle x between the tangent and AB. Draw the diameter AC.

The angle between the tangent and the radius AC is 90°, so angle BAC = 90 − x. Angle ABC = 90° (angle in a semicircle). In triangle ABC, angle ACB = 180 − 90 − (90 − x) = x.

Any other angle on chord AB in that segment equals angle ACB (same segment), so it is x too.

U7

Chords AB and CD cross at P. Why does AP × PB = CP × PD?

Show answer

Join A to C and D to B. Angle CAP = angle BDP (angles in the same segment, both on chord CB), and angle APC = angle DPB (vertically opposite). So triangles APC and DPB are similar.

Corresponding sides are in the same ratio: AP/DP = CP/BP. Cross-multiplying gives AP × PB = CP × PD.

U8

Why do three equal angles not prove two triangles are congruent, while RHS does?

Show answer

Three equal angles fix the shape but not the size. A triangle with angles 50°, 60° and 70° can be drawn with any side length, so AAA only proves the triangles are similar.

In RHS, the right angle, the hypotenuse and one side fix the third side by Pythagoras. All three sides are then equal, which is SSS, so the triangles are congruent.
