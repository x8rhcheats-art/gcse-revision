02How it actually works

Sets are just collections, and a Venn diagram shows how they overlap. Probability then asks: out of everything that could happen, what fraction is the thing you want? Venn diagrams, sample spaces, two-way tables and tree diagrams are four ways of counting that fraction without missing anything.

Set notation

Spec 1.5A–D (Foundation), 1.5A, 1.5C (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q4

A set is a collection of elements, and set notation is a short way to say which elements are in which sets.

Two-set Venn diagrams from numbers

Spec 1.5E (Foundation), 1.5B–D (Higher) · 2012 Edexcel Jan 4H Q19

Always fill a Venn diagram from the middle outwards, because the totals you are given include the overlap.

  1. Put the overlap (A ∩ B) in first. If it is not given, find it: n(A ∩ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∪ B).
  2. "A only" = n(A) − overlap. Same for B.
  3. Outside both = total − everything inside the circles. It can be 0: write the 0.
  4. Check: all the regions add up to n(ℰ).
Addition rule for setsn(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B)the overlap is counted twice when you add n(A) and n(B), so take it off once

Worked example: 40 people. 22 drink tea, 18 drink coffee, 6 drink neither.

1. In at least one circle: 40 − 6 = 34 M1

2. Both: 22 + 18 − 34 = 6 M1

3. Tea only 22 − 6 = 16, coffee only 18 − 6 = 12, outside 6. Check: 16 + 6 + 12 + 6 = 40 A1

✗ Writing 22 in the "tea" part of the circle and 18 in the "coffee" part. The 6 who drink both are then counted twice.
✓ Overlap first, then "only" regions, then outside.

Three-set Venn diagrams

Spec 1.5B (Higher) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q15 · 2016 Edexcel Jan 3HR Q17

With three sets, start at the very centre (all three), then the three "exactly two" regions, then the three "only one" regions, then outside.

  1. Centre: in A and B and C.
  2. Each pair overlap given (e.g. "8 are in A and B") includes the centre, so subtract the centre: A ∩ B only = 8 − centre.
  3. Each "only" region = the set total − its two pair regions − the centre.
  4. Outside = total − the sum of all seven regions.

Worked example: 50 students. 3 in all three clubs; 8 in A and B; 7 in A and C; 6 in B and C; 25 in A; 20 in B; 18 in C.

1. Centre 3. Pair regions: A∩B only 8 − 3 = 5, A∩C only 7 − 3 = 4, B∩C only 6 − 3 = 3 B1

2. A only 25 − 5 − 4 − 3 = 13; B only 20 − 5 − 3 − 3 = 9; C only 18 − 4 − 3 − 3 = 8 B1

3. Inside total 3 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 13 + 9 + 8 = 45, so outside 50 − 45 = 5 B1

The SAM scheme gave B1 for three overlapping circles with the centre correct, B2 for that and two more correct numbers, and B3 for the whole diagram.

✗ Putting 8 straight into the A∩B region when "8 are in A and B" and 3 are in all three.
✓ "In A and B" means the whole lens, centre included: the region outside C gets 8 − 3 = 5.

Probability basics

Spec 6.3A–C, 6.3H (Foundation) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q3(c)

When outcomes are equally likely, probability = number of ways the event can happen ÷ total number of possible outcomes, and all the probabilities of the separate outcomes add up to 1.

  1. Probabilities go from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). Anything outside that range is wrong.
  2. P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  3. If a table of probabilities has a missing value, the missing value = 1 − the rest.
  4. Write the answer as a fraction, decimal or percentage.
ComplementP(A′) = 1 − P(A)A′ means "A does not happen"

Worked example: red 0.2, blue 0.35, green x, yellow 2x. Find P(yellow).

1. 0.2 + 0.35 + x + 2x = 1, so 3x = 0.45 M1

2. x = 0.15, so P(yellow) = 2 × 0.15 = 0.3 A1

Sample spaces: listing outcomes systematically

Spec 6.3E–F (Foundation) · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q19

For two events together, a sample space grid lists every combined outcome once, so you can count exactly.

  1. Put one event's outcomes across the top and the other's down the side.
  2. Fill in every cell (e.g. the total of two dice). Two fair dice give 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely cells.
  3. Count the cells you want and divide by the total number of cells.

Two fair dice: 6 of the 36 cells total 7, so P(total of 7) = 6/36 = 1/6. For a list, be systematic: (H, H), (H, T), (T, H), (T, T). (H, T) and (T, H) are different outcomes.

Relative frequency and expected frequency

Spec 6.3G, 6.3J (Foundation)

Relative frequency estimates a probability from an experiment; expected frequency uses a probability to predict how often something will happen.

Relative frequencyrelative frequency = number of times it happened ÷ number of trialsmore trials give a more reliable estimate
Expected frequencyexpected number = probability × number of trialsan estimate, so it need not be a whole number in working

A biased coin landed heads 132 times in 200 throws: relative frequency 132 ÷ 200 = 0.66. If P(blue) = 0.35 and the spinner is spun 200 times, expect 0.35 × 200 = 70 blues.

"OR" means add (mutually exclusive), "AND" means multiply (independent)

Spec 6.3I (Foundation), 6.3B (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q19

Add probabilities for outcomes that cannot happen together; multiply probabilities for independent events happening one after another.

  1. Mutually exclusive (cannot both happen): P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).
  2. Independent (one does not affect the other): P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).
  3. For "one odd and one even", list the ways: (odd, even) or (even, odd). Multiply along each way, then add the ways.
Addition ruleP(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)only when A and B are mutually exclusive
Multiplication ruleP(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)only when A and B are independent

Worked example (2018 1H Q19): spinner A lands on 2, 3, 5 or 7; spinner B on 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6. Win if one is odd and the other even. P(win twice)?

1. P(A odd) = 3/4, P(A even) = 1/4. P(B odd) = 2/5, P(B even) = 3/5 M1

2. P(win) = 1/4 × 2/5 + 3/4 × 3/5 = 2/20 + 9/20 = 11/20 M1

3. Win both games: 11/20 × 11/20 M1

4. = 121/400 A1

✗ Only counting (A odd, B even) and forgetting (A even, B odd).
✗ Adding for "both games": 11/20 + 11/20 is more than a half and gets bigger with more games.
✓ Each way multiplied, the ways added, then × again for the second game.

Tree diagrams with replacement

Spec 6.3A–B (Higher) · 2017 Edexcel Jan 4HR Q12

A tree diagram shows each stage as a set of branches; multiply along a path to get that combined outcome, and add the paths you want.

  1. Each set of branches from one point adds up to 1.
  2. With replacement (or independent events), the second-stage branches are the same as the first.
  3. Multiply along each path. Add down the paths that fit the question.
  4. All the path probabilities together add up to 1: a useful check.

Worked example: P(on time) = 0.7 each day, independently. Find P(on time exactly one of two days).

1. Paths: (on time, late) = 0.7 × 0.3 = 0.21 and (late, on time) = 0.3 × 0.7 = 0.21 M1

2. Add: 0.21 + 0.21 = 0.42 A1

Check: both on time 0.49, both late 0.09; 0.49 + 0.42 + 0.09 = 1.

Without replacement: the second branch changes

Spec 6.3C (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q13 · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q20

If the first item is not put back, there is one fewer item in total, and one fewer of whatever was taken, so every second-stage fraction changes.

  1. First branches: the counts over the total, e.g. 5/10, 4/10, 1/10.
  2. Second branches: denominator goes down by 1 (10 → 9). The colour already taken goes down by 1 on that branch only.
  3. If the only one of something has gone, its second branch is 0 (or leave it out).
  4. Multiply along, add the paths you want.

Worked example: 3 red and 5 blue counters. Two taken without replacement. P(same colour)?

1. P(red, red) = 3/8 × 2/7 = 6/56 M1

2. P(blue, blue) = 5/8 × 4/7 = 20/56 M1

3. P(same) = 6/56 + 20/56 = 26/56 = 13/28 A1

With replacement it would be (3/8)2 + (5/8)2 = 34/64. Different answer, so read which one the question says.

2018 2H scheme for the tree: "0 can be 0/9 or the branch crossed out or left blank".

✗ Second branches still over 10, as if the card was put back.
✓ Over 9, with the colour just taken reduced by 1.

"At least one": use 1 − P(none)

Spec 6.3D (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q13(b)

"At least one" covers many paths but its opposite, "none at all", is a single path, so P(at least one) = 1 − P(none).

  1. Find P(none): multiply the "not" probability along the one path.
  2. Subtract from 1.

Worked example: a fair dice is rolled 3 times. P(at least one six)?

1. P(no sixes) = (5/6)3 = 125/216 M1

2. P(at least one six) = 1 − 125/216 = 91/216 A1

✗ 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/2. That adds events that are not mutually exclusive, and with 6 rolls it would give 1, which is clearly wrong.
✓ 1 − (5/6)3.

Conditional probability: "given that…"

Spec 6.3C (Higher) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q15(c)

"Given that" shrinks the sample space: the denominator becomes the number in the given group only.

  1. Find the group you are told about. Its total is the new denominator.
  2. Count the ones inside that group that also do the other thing. That is the numerator.
  3. From a Venn diagram: P(A given B) = n(A ∩ B) ÷ n(B).

Worked example: 30 students study Drama; 12 of them also study Art. A student who studies Drama is picked. P(Art)?

1. Denominator: only the 30 Drama students count M1

2. P(Art given Drama) = 12/30 = 2/5 A1

The unconditional P(Art and Drama) would use the whole year as the denominator. The words "given that" or "a student who…" tell you to shrink it.

✗ 12/100: the whole year group as the denominator.
✓ 12/30: only the students you were told about.

Dependent events on a tree

Spec 6.3C–D (Higher) · 2014 Edexcel Jan 4H Q14

When the second probability depends on what happened first, the second-stage branches are different on each part of the tree.

  1. Read the question carefully: "if he fails, the probability that he passes next time is 0.8" is a branch after a fail only.
  2. A path can stop early (after a pass there is no second test).
  3. Multiply along; add the paths that fit.

Worked example: P(pass first time) = 0.65. After a fail, P(pass next time) = 0.75.

1. P(pass at the second attempt) = P(fail, then pass) = 0.35 × 0.75 = 0.2625 M1

2. P(pass within two attempts) = 0.65 + 0.2625 = 0.9125 A1

04Understanding it

U1

Why must you fill in the centre of a Venn diagram first?

Show answer

Every total you are given, such as "25 are in A" or "8 are in A and B", includes the people in the centre. You can only work out the other regions by subtracting what is already inside them.

Starting at the outside means guessing how much of each total belongs to the overlaps. Starting at the centre means each region is a simple subtraction.

U2

Why does n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B)?

Show answer

Adding n(A) and n(B) counts everything in A and everything in B. The elements in both are counted twice, once in each total.

Subtracting n(A ∩ B) once removes the double count, leaving each element of A ∪ B counted exactly once.

U3

Why do you multiply along the branches of a tree diagram?

Show answer

The second probability is a fraction of the first. If a red is picked 3/8 of the time, and then a second red 2/7 of those times, both happen 2/7 of 3/8 of the time.

"Of" means multiply: 3/8 × 2/7. Each extra stage takes a fraction of what is left, so a path probability can only get smaller.

U4

Two cards are taken from 5 red and 4 blue without replacement. Why is the second branch after "red" 4/8 for red and 4/8 for blue?

Show answer

After a red is taken there are 8 cards left: 4 red and still 4 blue. So P(red) = 4/8 and P(blue) = 4/8 on that branch.

After a blue is taken, the 8 left are 5 red and 3 blue, so that branch has 5/8 and 3/8. Each branch reflects what is actually left after that first pick, which is why the two halves of the tree differ.

U5

Why is P(at least one six in 3 rolls) not 3 × 1/6 = 1/2?

Show answer

Adding only works for mutually exclusive events. "Six on roll 1" and "six on roll 2" can both happen, so adding counts those outcomes more than once.

The test: with 7 rolls, 7 × 1/6 is more than 1, which is impossible. The only case with no six is (not six, not six, not six), so the answer is 1 − (5/6)3 = 91/216 ≈ 0.42.

U6

What is the difference between "mutually exclusive" and "independent"?

Show answer

Mutually exclusive: the events cannot both happen, e.g. a single dice showing 2 and showing 5. Then P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).

Independent: one happening does not change the chance of the other, e.g. two different coins. Then P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Mutually exclusive events with non-zero probabilities are never independent: if one happens, the other becomes impossible.

U7

A Venn diagram shows 100 students, 46 of whom study Drama, 23 of those also study Art. Why is P(Art given Drama) = 23/46 and not 23/100?

Show answer

"Given that the student studies Drama" means you already know the student is one of the 46. The other 54 students are no longer possible.

Out of those 46, 23 study Art: 23/46. 23/100 answers a different question: the probability that a student picked from the whole year does both.

U8

A spinner lands on red 42 times in 200 spins. Why is 42/200 only an estimate of P(red), and how could it be made more reliable?

Show answer

It is a relative frequency from one experiment. Random variation means another 200 spins would give a slightly different count.

More spins make the relative frequency settle closer to the true probability, so the estimate becomes more reliable. It can then be used for expected frequency: 42/200 × 1000 = 210 reds expected in 1000 spins.
