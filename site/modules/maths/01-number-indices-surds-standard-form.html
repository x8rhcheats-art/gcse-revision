02How it actually works

This module is the number toolkit that the rest of the course leans on. Most questions are short, usually 1 to 4 marks, and follow a fixed routine, so each section below gives you the routine as numbered steps plus one worked example laid out the way the mark scheme wants it.

Prime factor decomposition

Spec 1.4D · 2014 Edexcel Jan 4H Q8 · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q8

Every whole number bigger than 1 can be written in exactly one way as a product of prime numbers. The exam wants it as a product of powers of primes, e.g. 360 = 23 × 32 × 5.

  1. Divide by the smallest prime that goes in (2, then 3, then 5, then 7…). A factor tree does the same job.
  2. Keep dividing until you reach 1.
  3. Collect equal primes into powers, in increasing order, joined with × signs.
  4. Check: multiply it back out on the calculator.

Worked example — write 1260 as a product of powers of its prime factors

1260 ÷ 2 = 630, 630 ÷ 2 = 315, 315 ÷ 3 = 105, 105 ÷ 3 = 35, 35 ÷ 5 = 7, 7 ÷ 7 = 1 (M1 for at least two correct divisions or a correct partial tree)

1260 = 22 × 32 × 5 × 7 (A1)

✗ "2, 2, 3, 3, 5, 7" as a list. That is not a product, and the A1 is lost.
✗ "4 × 9 × 35". The factors are not all prime.
✓ 22 × 32 × 5 × 7 (or 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 × 7 if the question does not say "powers").

HCF and LCM from prime factors

Spec 1.4E · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q8 · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q1

The HCF takes each shared prime to its lowest power; the LCM takes every prime to its highest power.

  1. Write each number as a product of prime powers.
  2. HCF: use only the primes that appear in every number, each to the lowest power it appears.
  3. LCM: use every prime that appears anywhere, each to the highest power it appears.
  4. Leave it in index form if the numbers were given in index form; otherwise multiply out.

Worked example — HCF and LCM of 84 and 360

84 = 22 × 3 × 7 and 360 = 23 × 32 × 5 (M1 for both decompositions)

HCF: shared primes 2 and 3, lowest powers → 22 × 3 = 12 (A1)

LCM: all primes, highest powers → 23 × 32 × 5 × 7 = 2520 (A1)

Word problems hide the LCM: "one light flashes every 12 seconds, another every 18 seconds, when do they next flash together?" is the LCM (36 seconds). "Largest equal groups" or "largest tile that fits exactly" is the HCF.

2018 1H Q8(b) ran it backwards. If the LCM of A and C contains 24 and A has no factor of 2, then C must contain exactly 24. Reason one prime at a time.

The index laws

Spec 1.4C, 2.1A · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q3 · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q4

Powers of the same base combine by adding, subtracting or multiplying the indices; the numbers in front are dealt with separately.

Index laws — memoriseam × an = am+n  ·  am ÷ an = am−n  ·  (am)n = amn  ·  a0 = 1same base only · a bracket raised to a power raises every factor inside it, numbers included
  1. Deal with the numbers first (multiply, divide, or raise them to the power).
  2. Deal with each letter separately using the laws above.
  3. Write the result as one term, e.g. 27x12y3.

Worked example — simplify (3a4b2)3 and 12x7y2 ÷ 4x3y5

(3a4b2)3: 33 = 27, (a4)3 = a12, (b2)3 = b6 → 27a12b6 (B2; B1 for two of the three parts right)

12 ÷ 4 = 3, x7−3 = x4, y2−5 = y−3 → 3x4y−3 or 3x4/y3 (B2)

✗ (2m3)4 = 2m12. The 2 was not raised to the power.
✗ (2m3)4 = 16m7. The indices were added instead of multiplied.
✓ (2m3)4 = 16m12. That was 2018 1H Q3(b), and the scheme gave B1 for 16m12 with one part wrong.

Negative and fractional indices

Spec 1.4C (Higher) · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q15(a) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q4(d)

A negative index means "one over", the denominator of a fractional index is a root, and the numerator is a power.

Negative and fractional powers — memorisea−n = 1/an  ·  a1/n = n√a  ·  am/n = (n√a)mdo the root first — the numbers stay small
  1. Negative? Flip the base (take the reciprocal) and make the index positive.
  2. Denominator of the index: take that root.
  3. Numerator of the index: raise to that power.

Worked example — evaluate 27−2/3 and (16/81)−3/4

27−2/3 = 1/272/3; ∛27 = 3; 32 = 9 → 1/9

(16/81)−3/4 = (81/16)3/4 (M1 for flipping or for the fourth root); 4√(81/16) = 3/2; (3/2)3 = 27/8 (A1)

The same with letters, like 2018 1H Q15(a): (81x12/y8)−3/4 = (y8/81x12)3/4 = y6/(27x9). Multiply every index by the power, and take the root of the number separately.

✗ 25−1/2 = −5. A negative index never makes the answer negative.
✗ 27−2/3 = 27 × −2/3 = −18. The index is not a multiplier.
✓ 25−1/2 = 1/√25 = 1/5.

Writing numbers as powers of a prime

Spec 1.4C · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q10

To solve 2? = … or compare powers, rewrite every number as a power of the same prime, then the indices must be equal.

  1. Rewrite each number as a power of the common base: 8 = 23, 4 = 22, 1/9 = 3−2, √2 = 21/2.
  2. Combine using the index laws into a single power.
  3. Set the indices equal and solve.

Worked example — 4x × 25 = 83, find x

(22)x × 25 = (23)3 → 22x+5 = 29 (M1)

2x + 5 = 9 → x = 2 (A1)

And 8/27 = 23/27 = 2−4, so n = −4 (the 2018 question).

Simplifying surds

Spec 1.4A · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q21(a)

A surd is simplified by taking out the largest square factor: √(ab) = √a × √b.

Surd rules — memorise√(ab) = √a × √b  ·  √(a/b) = √a/√b  ·  √a × √a = athere is no rule for √(a + b) — it is not √a + √b
  1. Find the largest square number that divides the number under the root (4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49…).
  2. Split: √72 = √36 × √2.
  3. Square-root the square: 6√2.
  4. Only like surds (same number under the root) can be added: 4√3 + 3√3 = 7√3.

Worked example — show that √48 + √27 = 7√3

√48 = √16 × √3 = 4√3 (M1 for one of these)

√27 = √9 × √3 = 3√3

4√3 + 3√3 = 7√3 (A1, with both steps visible)

Expanding brackets with surds

Spec 1.4B · 2014 Edexcel Jan 3H Q17

Brackets with surds expand exactly like algebra, with √a × √a = a turning some terms back into whole numbers.

  1. Multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second.
  2. Replace √a × √a by a, and √a × √b by √(ab).
  3. Collect the whole numbers and the surds separately: answer in the form p + q√r.

Worked example — expand (3 + √5)2 and (2 + √3)(2 − √3)

(3 + √5)(3 + √5) = 9 + 3√5 + 3√5 + 5 (M1 for 4 terms, at least 3 correct) = 14 + 6√5 (A1)

(2 + √3)(2 − √3) = 4 − 2√3 + 2√3 − 3 = 1. The surds cancel. That is the idea behind rationalising.

✗ (3 + √5)2 = 9 + 5 = 14. The two middle terms are missing.
✓ Always write the bracket out twice and expand all four terms.

Rationalising the denominator

Spec 1.4B · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q21(b) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q24

Multiplying the top and bottom by the right surd makes the denominator a whole number without changing the value.

  1. Denominator √a: multiply top and bottom by √a.
  2. Denominator a + √b: multiply top and bottom by a − √b (flip the sign). The bottom becomes a2 − b.
  3. Expand the top, simplify the bottom, then cancel any common factor.

Worked example — rationalise 10/√5, then write 4/(3 − √5) in the form a + √b

10/√5 × √5/√5 = 10√5/5 = 2√5

4/(3 − √5) × (3 + √5)/(3 + √5) (M1 for multiplying by (3 + √5)/(3 + √5))

Bottom: 32 − (√5)2 = 9 − 5 = 4. Top: 4(3 + √5)

4(3 + √5)/4 = 3 + √5 (A1)

✗ Multiplying only the denominator by (3 + √5). That changes the value of the fraction.
✗ Multiplying by (3 − √5) again. The surd does not cancel.
✓ Multiply top and bottom by the denominator with the sign flipped.

Standard form

Spec 1.9A · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q5 · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q9

A number in standard form is A × 10n where 1 ≤ A < 10 and n is an integer.

Standard formA × 10n,   1 ≤ A < 10,   n an integerbig numbers: n positive · small numbers (less than 1): n negative
  1. Multiply/divide: multiply/divide the A parts, then use the index laws on the powers of 10.
  2. Add/subtract: make the powers of 10 the same first (or convert to ordinary numbers).
  3. Fix the answer so A is between 1 and 10: 25.2 × 10−4 = 2.52 × 10−3; 0.25 × 104 = 2.5 × 103.

Worked example — (4.2 × 105) × (3 × 10−8) and 3.2 × 105 + 4.5 × 104

4.2 × 3 = 12.6; 105 × 10−8 = 10−3 → 12.6 × 10−3 (M1) = 1.26 × 10−2 (A1)

3.2 × 105 + 0.45 × 105 = 3.65 × 105 (check: 320 000 + 45 000 = 365 000)

On the calculator, use the ×10x key, not "× 10 ^". A display such as 2.52E−03 must be written as 2.52 × 10−3. The E notation is not accepted.

✗ 12.6 × 10−3 left as the final answer. It is not in standard form.
✗ 0.25 × 104 → 2.5 × 105. Making A bigger means making the power smaller.
✓ Check by size: 2.5 × 103 = 2500, and 1.2 × 107 ÷ 4.8 × 103 is about 2500.

Standard form in context

Spec 1.9A · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q9(c)–(d)

Context questions are the same calculations, but the answer must be given in the form asked: standard form, a ratio 1 : n, or "how many times bigger".

  1. "How many times bigger" means divide the larger by the smaller.
  2. "Ratio 1 : n" means divide both parts by the first number: 3.5 × 103 : 1.4 × 106 = 1 : 400.
  3. Round only at the very end, to the accuracy asked.

Worked example — the Earth's mass is 5.97 × 1024 kg and the Moon's is 7.35 × 1022 kg

How many times heavier is the Earth? 5.97 × 1024 ÷ 7.35 × 1022 = 81.22… → 81.2 (3 sf) (M1 A1)

Total mass: 5.97 × 1024 + 0.0735 × 1024 = 6.0435 × 1024 → 6.04 × 1024 kg (3 sf)

Recurring decimals to fractions

Spec 1.2A (Higher) · 2014 Edexcel Jan 4H Q20

Multiply the decimal by a power of 10 that lines up the repeating block, then subtract so the endless tail cancels.

  1. Let x = the recurring decimal. Write out several repeats: x = 0.454545…
  2. Multiply by 10 for a one-digit repeat, 100 for two digits, 1000 for three.
  3. If the repeat does not start straight after the decimal point, first multiply so it does, and subtract those two.
  4. Subtract, solve for x, and simplify the fraction.

Worked example — show that 0.14 (= 0.1444…) = 13/90

x = 0.1444…

10x = 1.444… and 100x = 14.444… (M1 for two correct recurring decimals that will subtract to a whole number)

100x − 10x = 14.444… − 1.444… → 90x = 13 → x = 13/90 (A1)

Two-digit repeat: 0.3636… → 100x − x = 36 → 99x = 36 → x = 36/99 = 4/11.

✗ Writing "100x = 36.36" with no dots. The recurring tail has to be shown, or the subtraction cannot be seen to cancel.
✗ Using 10x − x for 0.1444… This gives 9x = 1.3, not a whole number, and the method stalls.
✓ Choose the two multiples whose decimal tails are identical, then subtract.

04Understanding it

U1

Why does the HCF use the lowest power of each shared prime, but the LCM the highest?

Show answer

A common factor has to fit inside every number. If one number has only 22, no common factor can contain 23, so the biggest you can take is the smallest power available.

A common multiple has to contain every number. If one number needs 23, the multiple must contain at least 23, so you take the biggest power. And any prime in any number must appear.

U2

Why is a0 = 1, not 0?

Show answer

Use the division law: a3 ÷ a3 = a3−3 = a0. But anything divided by itself is 1. So a0 must equal 1 (for any a ≠ 0).

The same argument gives negative indices: a2 ÷ a5 = a−3, and it also equals 1/a3.

U3

Why does a power of ½ mean a square root?

Show answer

a1/2 × a1/2 = a1/2 + 1/2 = a1 = a. So a1/2 is the number which, multiplied by itself, gives a. That is exactly √a.

In the same way, three lots of a1/3 multiply to a, so a1/3 = ∛a.

U4

A student writes √(9 + 16) = √9 + √16 = 7. What is wrong, and what rule were they thinking of?

Show answer

√(9 + 16) = √25 = 5, not 7. Square roots do not split over addition.

They split over multiplication: √(9 × 16) = √9 × √16 = 12. That is why √72 = √36 × √2 works: you must split into a product, never a sum.

U5

Why does multiplying by (3 + √5) get rid of the surd in 1/(3 − √5)?

Show answer

(3 − √5)(3 + √5) is a difference of two squares: 32 − (√5)2 = 9 − 5 = 4. The two surd terms, +3√5 and −3√5, cancel.

You multiply the top by the same bracket, so you are really multiplying by (3 + √5)/(3 + √5) = 1. The value does not change, only its form.

U6

Why is 34 × 103 not in standard form, and how do you fix it without changing its value?

Show answer

The front number must satisfy 1 ≤ A < 10, and 34 does not.

34 = 3.4 × 10, so 34 × 103 = 3.4 × 10 × 103 = 3.4 × 104. Dividing A by 10 means multiplying the power by 10 (adding 1 to n), so the value is unchanged.

U7

For x = 0.1444…, why do you subtract 10x from 100x rather than x from 10x?

Show answer

You need two numbers with identical tails so that the subtraction leaves a whole number. 10x = 1.444… and 100x = 14.444… both end in 444… forever, so 90x = 13 exactly.

x = 0.1444… and 10x = 1.444… have different tails (0.1444 vs 0.444), so 9x = 1.3. That still works if you then multiply by 10, but it is messier. The 1 at the front is not part of the repeat.

U8

A student writes 8−2/3 = −16/3. Explain the two misconceptions and give the right value.

Show answer

They treated the index as something to multiply by: 8 × −2/3. An index is not a multiplier. It tells you which root and power to take.

They also treated "negative" as "the answer is negative". A negative index means the reciprocal.

Correctly: 8−2/3 = 1/82/3; ∛8 = 2; 22 = 4 → 1/4.
