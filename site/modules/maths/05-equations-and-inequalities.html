Equations and Inequalities

The engine room of both papers. Every current-spec paper has a linear equation, a quadratic and a pair of simultaneous equations, and most have an inequality or a shaded region. The marks go to people who show each line of algebra: "show clear algebraic working" means a correct answer with no working can score nothing.

The front of the May 2018 1H mark scheme sets the rule for every "show your working" equation:

"Apart from questions 3c, 11b and 20 (where the mark scheme states otherwise) the correct answer, unless clearly obtained from an incorrect method, should be taken to imply a correct method."

Q3c and Q11b were the linear equation and the quadratic formula. On those, a bare correct answer is not enough. Write every line: expand, collect, divide. For the formula, write the substitution out in full.

02How it actually works

An equation is a balance. Whatever you do to one side you do to the other, until the letter is on its own. Inequalities follow the same rules, with one extra: multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the sign. Quadratics need one more idea: if two things multiply to give zero, one of them must be zero.

Linear equations with brackets and unknowns on both sides

Spec 2.4A · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q3(c) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q4(e)

Get rid of brackets, get every x-term on one side and every number on the other, then divide.

  1. Expand any brackets, taking care with a minus sign in front.
  2. Collect the x-terms on the side where there are more of them, so the x-coefficient stays positive.
  3. Collect the numbers on the other side.
  4. Divide by the coefficient of x. Leave the answer as a fraction or exact decimal unless told otherwise.

Worked example: solve 4(x − 2) = 7x + 13

4x − 8 = 7x + 13   (M1: brackets expanded correctly)

−8 − 13 = 7x − 4x   →   −21 = 3x   (M1: x-terms on one side, numbers on the other)

x = −7   (A1)

Check: 4(−7 − 2) = −36 and 7(−7) + 13 = −36. ✓

✗ 3(x − 4) − 2(x − 5) → 3x − 12 − 2x − 10. The second bracket has a −2 in front, so −2 × −5 = +10.
✗ 3x = 12 → x = 36, or x = 12 − 3. Dividing is the only move that undoes "3 times".
✓ 3x − 12 − 2x + 10 = x − 2.

Equations with fractions, and forming equations

Spec 2.4A, 2.4B · 2017 Edexcel Jan 3HR Q16(a) (old spec)

Multiply every term, on both sides, by the lowest common denominator. Then the fractions disappear and it is an ordinary linear equation.

  1. Find the LCD of all the denominators.
  2. Multiply every term by it, including any term with no fraction. Put each numerator in a bracket first.
  3. Expand, collect and solve as before.

Worked example: solve (2x + 1)/3 − (x − 2)/4 = 2

LCD = 12.   4(2x + 1) − 3(x − 2) = 24   (M1: all three terms multiplied by 12)

8x + 4 − 3x + 6 = 24   (M1: brackets expanded, sign on the +6 correct)

5x = 14   →   x = 2.8   (A1)

Forming an equation. Turn the words into algebra, then solve. For example, the angles of a triangle are a°, (a + 10)° and (a + 20)°, so a + a + 10 + a + 20 = 180, 3a = 150 and a = 50.

✗ Multiplying only the fractions by 12 and leaving the "= 2" alone. The 2 must become 24.
✗ 4(2x + 1) − 3x − 2. Without the bracket the −3 only hits the x, so the sign of the −2 is wrong.
✓ Bracket every numerator before you multiply out.

Linear inequalities and the number line

Spec 2.8 (Foundation A–C) · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q1(b) · 2013 Edexcel Jan 3H Q7 (old spec)

Solve an inequality exactly like an equation, except that multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality sign.

  1. Treat the inequality sign like an equals sign: add, subtract, multiply and divide both sides.
  2. If you multiply or divide by a negative, flip the sign. Or avoid it by moving the x-term to the other side.
  3. For a double inequality, such as −3 < 2x + 1 ≤ 9, do the same thing to all three parts.
  4. On a number line, use an open circle for < or > and a filled circle for ≤ or ≥.

Worked example: solve −3 < 2x + 1 ≤ 9 and list the integers

Subtract 1 from all three parts: −4 < 2x ≤ 8   (M1)

Divide all three parts by 2: −2 < x ≤ 4   (A1)

Integers: −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4. −2 is not included (strict <); 4 is included (≤)   (B1)

Worked example: solve 7 − 3x ≥ 19

−3x ≥ 12. Divide by −3 and flip: x ≤ −4.

Or avoid the negative: 7 − 19 ≥ 3x, so −12 ≥ 3x, so −4 ≥ x, which is the same as x ≤ −4.

✗ −3x ≥ 12 → x ≥ −4. The sign did not flip. Test x = 0: 7 − 0 ≥ 19 is false, so x ≥ −4 cannot be right.
✗ Writing "x = 3" as the answer to an inequality. The answer is a range, with an inequality sign in it.
✓ Test one value from your answer in the original inequality before moving on.

Simultaneous linear equations by elimination

Spec 2.6A · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q9 · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q9

Make the coefficients of one letter the same size, then add or subtract the equations so that letter disappears.

  1. Multiply one or both equations so that x (or y) has the same coefficient in each.
  2. Same signs, subtract. Different signs, add.
  3. Solve the equation in one letter.
  4. Substitute back into an original equation to find the other letter.
  5. Check both values in the other original equation.

Worked example: 3x + 2y = 16 and 5x − 3y = 14

× 3 and × 2:   9x + 6y = 48   and   10x − 6y = 28   (M1: coefficients of y matched)

The y-signs are different, so add: 19x = 76, so x = 4   (M1: correct operation to eliminate y)

3(4) + 2y = 16, so 2y = 4, so y = 2.   x = 4, y = 2   (A1)

Check in the second equation: 5(4) − 3(2) = 14. ✓

If one equation is already "y = …" or "x = …", substitution is quicker. Put that expression into the other equation.

✗ 9x + 6y = 48 and 10x − 6y = 28, subtracted: −x + 12y = 20. That removes nothing.
✗ Multiplying only the left-hand side: 3x + 2y = 16 becomes 9x + 6y = 16.
✓ Multiply every term, including the right-hand side. Add when the signs are different.

Simultaneous equations as intersecting lines

Spec 2.6B · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q12(a)

The solution of two simultaneous equations is the point where their graphs cross.

Every point on a line satisfies that line's equation. The crossing point lies on both lines, so it satisfies both equations at once. In 2018 1H Q12(a) the lines 3y = 2x + 6 and 4x + 3y = 24 crossed at (3, 4), so the answer was x = 3, y = 4, read straight off the grid for 1 mark.

  • Parallel lines never meet, so their equations have no solution.
  • A reading off a graph is an estimate. If the question says "show clear algebraic working", a graphical answer scores nothing.

Quadratic equations by factorising

Spec 2.7A · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q3(d) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q6(b)

Rearrange to "= 0", factorise, then set each bracket equal to zero. If two numbers multiply to give 0, one of them must be 0.

  1. Rearrange so that one side is 0: ax2 + bx + c = 0.
  2. Factorise. For x2 + bx + c, find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b. For ax2 + bx + c, find two numbers that multiply to ac and add to b, then split the middle term.
  3. Set each bracket = 0 and solve. There are usually two answers.

Worked example: solve 2x2 + 5x − 12 = 0

ac = 2 × −12 = −24. Two numbers that multiply to −24 and add to 5: 8 and −3.

2x2 + 8x − 3x − 12 = 2x(x + 4) − 3(x + 4) = (2x − 3)(x + 4)   (M1: correct factors)

2x − 3 = 0 or x + 4 = 0   →   x = 1.5 or x = −4   (A1)

Special cases: x2 − 25 = 0 is a difference of two squares, (x − 5)(x + 5) = 0, so x = ±5. For x2 = 7x, rearrange to x2 − 7x = 0, so x(x − 7) = 0, giving x = 0 or x = 7.

✗ (x − 4)(x + 6) = 0 → x = −4 or x = 6. The signs flip: x − 4 = 0 gives x = +4.
✗ x2 = 7x → divide by x → x = 7. Dividing by x throws away the solution x = 0.
✓ Always rearrange to "= 0" and factorise. Never divide by x.

The quadratic formula

Spec 2.7B · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q11(b) · 2015 Edexcel Jan 4HR Q14 (old spec)

The formula solves every quadratic, factorisable or not. Use it whenever the question asks for decimals or significant figures.

The quadratic formula — on the formula sheetx = (−b ± √(b2 − 4ac)) ÷ 2asolves ax2 + bx + c = 0 (a ≠ 0) · the whole of −b ± √… is divided by 2a
  1. Rearrange to ax2 + bx + c = 0 and write down a, b and c with their signs.
  2. Substitute in full and write it down. That line is the M marks.
  3. Work out b2 − 4ac first, then the two answers: one with +, one with −.
  4. Round at the end, to the accuracy asked.

Worked example: solve 2x2 − 7x + 2 = 0, giving answers to 3 s.f.

a = 2, b = −7, c = 2

x = (7 ± √((−7)2 − 4 × 2 × 2)) ÷ (2 × 2) = (7 ± √33) ÷ 4   (M1: correct substitution · M1: √33 correctly evaluated)

x = (7 + 5.7445…) ÷ 4 = 3.186…   or   x = (7 − 5.7445…) ÷ 4 = 0.3138…

x = 3.19 or x = 0.314   (A1)

✗ b = 7 when the equation says −7x. Then −b is wrong and so is everything after it.
✗ −72 typed into a calculator gives −49. Type (−7)2 = 49.
✗ 7 ± √33 ÷ 4, where only the root is divided by 4. The whole numerator goes over 2a.
✓ Write the substitution with brackets round every negative number.

Completing the square and the turning point

Spec 2.2D, 2.7B · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q22 · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q21(c)

Halve the coefficient of x to make the bracket, then subtract the square of that half to put the expression back as it was.

Completing the square — memorisex2 + bx + c = (x + b/2)2 − (b/2)2 + cb/2 = half the x-coefficient · the turning point of y = (x + p)2 + q is (−p, q)
  1. If the x2 term has a coefficient a ≠ 1, take a out of the x2 and x terms first: a(x2 + (b/a)x) + c.
  2. Write (x + half)2 and subtract half2.
  3. Tidy the numbers. If you took a out, multiply the subtracted square by a.
  4. To solve: make the bracket the subject, square-root (±), then isolate x.
  5. Turning point of y = a(x + p)2 + q is (−p, q): a minimum if a > 0, a maximum if a < 0.

Worked example: x2 + 6x − 5

Half of 6 is 3: (x + 3)2 − 9 − 5 = (x + 3)2 − 14   (M1: (x + 3)2 seen · A1)

Solve x2 + 6x − 5 = 0: (x + 3)2 = 14, so x + 3 = ±√14, so x = −3 ± √14 (0.742 or −6.74).

Turning point of y = x2 + 6x − 5: the bracket is 0 when x = −3, and then y = −14. Minimum at (−3, −14).

Worked example: 2x2 − 12x + 7 in the form a(x + b)2 + c

2(x2 − 6x) + 7   (M1: 2 taken out of the first two terms)

2[(x − 3)2 − 9] + 7 = 2(x − 3)2 − 18 + 7   (M1)

= 2(x − 3)2 − 11, so a = 2, b = −3, c = −11   (A1)

SAM 2H Q22(b) asked why y = 2x2 − 8x + 9 never meets the x-axis. 2x2 − 8x + 9 = 2(x − 2)2 + 1. A square is never negative, so y ≥ 1: the minimum point (2, 1) is above the x-axis.

✗ 2(x − 3)2 − 9 + 7. The −9 is inside the bracket that is multiplied by 2, so it becomes −18.
✗ Turning point of (x + 3)2 − 14 is (3, −14). The x-coordinate is the value that makes the bracket zero: −3.
✓ Expand your answer back out to check it gives the original expression.

Forming and solving quadratics from a context

Spec 2.7C · 2016 Edexcel SAM 1H Q15 · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q22

Write an expression for the quantity you are given (an area, a total, a product), set it equal to the value, rearrange to "= 0" and solve. Then check which answers make sense.

  1. Write the expression (for example, area = length × width) using the lengths in x.
  2. Set it equal to the given value and expand.
  3. Rearrange to ax2 + bx + c = 0. In a "show that", this line must match the question exactly.
  4. Solve, then reject any answer that gives a negative length, a negative number of people, and so on. Say why.
  5. Answer the question asked. It usually wants an area or a perimeter, not x.

Worked example: a rectangle is (x + 4) cm by (x − 1) cm, with area 36 cm². Find its perimeter.

(x + 4)(x − 1) = 36   →   x2 + 3x − 4 = 36   →   x2 + 3x − 40 = 0   (M1 · A1)

(x + 8)(x − 5) = 0   →   x = 5 or x = −8   (M1)

x = −8 gives a width of −9 cm, which is impossible, so x = 5   (A1)

Rectangle is 9 cm by 4 cm, so the perimeter is 2(9 + 4) = 26 cm   (M1 · A1)

✗ Stopping at "x = 5". The question asked for the perimeter.
✗ Keeping both roots. The SAM 1H Q15 scheme said "Ignore negative root". Reject it and say why.

Simultaneous equations: one linear, one quadratic

Spec 2.7D · 2014 Edexcel Jan 4H Q22 · 2013 Edexcel Jan 4H Q26 (old spec)

Rearrange the linear equation to make one letter the subject, substitute it into the quadratic, and solve the resulting quadratic. There are usually two pairs of answers.

  1. Make y (or x) the subject of the linear equation.
  2. Substitute into the quadratic one, using brackets: (2x − 1)2, not 2x − 12.
  3. Expand and simplify to a 3-term quadratic = 0. Divide through by a common factor if there is one.
  4. Solve (factorise or formula).
  5. Put each x into the linear equation to get its partner y. Give the answers as pairs.

Worked example: y = x + 1 and x2 + y2 = 13

x2 + (x + 1)2 = 13   (M1: substitution)

x2 + x2 + 2x + 1 = 13   →   2x2 + 2x − 12 = 0   →   x2 + x − 6 = 0   (M1: 3-term quadratic)

(x + 3)(x − 2) = 0   →   x = −3 or x = 2   (M1 · A1)

y = x + 1:   x = 2, y = 3 and x = −3, y = −2   (A1)

Geometrically, this finds where a straight line crosses a circle (x2 + y2 = 13 is a circle, centre O, radius √13) or a curve. Two points of intersection give two pairs.

✗ (x + 1)2 = x2 + 1. The middle term, 2x, is missing. Write (x + 1)(x + 1) if you need to.
✗ Pairing the answers wrongly: x = 2 with y = −2. Each x gives its own y.
✓ Substitute each pair back into the quadratic equation as a check: 22 + 32 = 13 ✓.

Quadratic inequalities

Spec 2.8A · 2016 Edexcel Jan 3H Q16 (old spec) · 2018 Edexcel May 2H Q17(b)

Find the critical values by solving the equation, sketch the parabola, then read off where the graph is below (< 0) or above (> 0) the x-axis.

  1. Rearrange to quadratic < 0 (or > 0, ≤ 0, ≥ 0).
  2. Solve the equation = 0 to get the two critical values.
  3. Sketch the U-shaped curve (for a positive x2 term) through them.
  4. < 0: the part below the axis, which is between the roots, one inequality: p < x < q.
    > 0: the parts above, which are the two tails, written as two inequalities: x < p or x > q.

Worked example: solve x2 − x − 12 < 0

(x − 4)(x + 3) = 0 gives critical values −3 and 4   (M1 · A1)

U-shaped curve, below the axis between the roots: −3 < x < 4   (A1)

Worked example: solve 2x2 + 5x − 3 > 0

(2x − 1)(x + 3) = 0 gives critical values −3 and ½. Above the axis means the tails: x < −3 or x > ½.

Worked example: solve x2 ≥ 25

Critical values ±5. x2 − 25 ≥ 0 means the tails: x ≤ −5 or x ≥ 5.

✗ x2 < 16 → x < ±4, or x < 4. x = −10 satisfies x < 4 but (−10)2 = 100 is not less than 16.
✗ "−3 > x > 5" for the two tails. No number is both less than −3 and greater than 5.
✓ x2 < 16 → −4 < x < 4. The tails need two separate inequalities joined by "or".

Regions defined by linear inequalities

Spec 2.8 (Foundation D–E, Higher B) · 2018 Edexcel May 1H Q12(b) · 2016 Edexcel SAM 2H Q14

Draw each boundary line, then use a test point to decide which side of each line is wanted. The region is where every inequality is true at once.

  1. Draw each boundary as a straight line. For x + y = 7, the easy points are (0, 7) and (7, 0).
  2. For each line, test a point not on it, usually (0, 0). If it makes the inequality true, that side is wanted.
  3. Find the region that is on the correct side of every line. Shade it, or shade what is not wanted if the question says so, and label it R.
  4. y > … is above a line, y < … is below it, x > … is to the right, and x < … is to the left.

Worked example: y ≥ 2, y ≤ 2x + 1 and x + y ≤ 7

y ≥ 2: on or above the horizontal line y = 2. y ≤ 2x + 1: test (0, 0): 0 ≤ 1 is true, so the side containing O, below the line. x + y ≤ 7: test (0, 0): 0 ≤ 7 is true, so the side containing O.

The region is the triangle with vertices (0.5, 2), (5, 2) and (2, 5). Each vertex is where two boundary lines cross, for example 2x + 1 = 7 − x gives x = 2.

The spec says "conventions for the inclusion of boundaries are not required", so solid and dashed lines both score. The 2018 1H scheme accepted "May shade wanted or unwanted regions", provided the region is clear and labelled.

✗ Shading below y = 2x + 1 by eye without testing a point. For a steep line, "below" and "to the right" are easy to confuse.
✗ Forgetting to label R, which leaves the examiner guessing which region you meant.
✓ Tick each inequality against one point inside your region before you finish.

03Cheat sheet

Methods and formulae to have ready

Linear: expand → x-terms one side → numbers the other → divide. Fractions: multiply every term by the LCD.

Inequality: as an equation, but × or ÷ by a negative flips the sign.

Simultaneous (linear): match coefficients; same signs subtract, different signs add; substitute back; check.

Quadratic: = 0 first. Factorise, or use x = (−b ± √(b2 − 4ac)) ÷ 2a (on the formula sheet), or complete the square.

Completing the square: x2 + bx + c = (x + b/2)2 − (b/2)2 + c. Turning point of y = a(x + p)2 + q is (−p, q).

Linear + quadratic: substitute the linear into the quadratic, solve, and pair each x with its own y.

Quadratic inequality: critical values, sketch. < 0 → between (p < x < q). > 0 → tails (x < p or x > q).

Regions: draw the lines, test (0, 0) for each, label R.

04Understanding it

U1

A student solves x2 = 6x by dividing both sides by x and gets x = 6. What has gone wrong?

Show answer

Dividing by x is only allowed if x ≠ 0, and x = 0 is one of the solutions. 02 = 6 × 0 is true.

The safe method is to rearrange to x2 − 6x = 0, factorise to x(x − 6) = 0, and read off x = 0 or x = 6. A quadratic usually has two solutions. If you only find one, check how you lost the other.

U2

Why does multiplying an inequality by −1 reverse the sign? Use 2 < 5 to explain.

Show answer

2 < 5 is true. Multiply both by −1: −2 and −5. On the number line, −2 is to the right of −5, so −2 > −5. The order has swapped.

Multiplying by a negative reflects every number in 0, so the larger one becomes the smaller one. That is why the sign must flip. You can avoid it altogether by moving the x-term to the side where it will be positive.

U3

Why does x2 < 9 give −3 < x < 3, but x2 > 9 give x < −3 or x > 3?

Show answer

Sketch y = x2 − 9. It is a U-shape crossing the x-axis at −3 and 3.

x2 − 9 < 0 means the curve is below the axis. That happens only between the roots, so it is one joined-up range.

x2 − 9 > 0 means above the axis, which happens on both outer sides. These are two separate pieces, so there are two inequalities joined by "or". Test x = −10: 100 > 9 is true, so the left-hand tail really is included.

U4

How can you tell quickly whether a quadratic will factorise, and what does the formula do when b2 − 4ac is negative?

Show answer

Work out b2 − 4ac. If it is a perfect square (0, 1, 4, 9, 16, …), the roots are rational and the quadratic factorises. If not (e.g. 33), the roots involve a surd, so use the formula or complete the square.

If b2 − 4ac is negative, there is no real square root, so the equation has no real solutions. The graph of y = ax2 + bx + c does not cross the x-axis.

U5

Why does writing y = (x − 2)2 + 5 tell you straight away that the minimum point is (2, 5)?

Show answer

(x − 2)2 is a square, so it is never negative. Its smallest possible value is 0, when x = 2.

So the smallest y can be is 0 + 5 = 5, when x = 2. Every other x adds something positive. That is the minimum point: (2, 5). If there is a negative multiplier in front, −(x − 2)2 + 5, the square is subtracted, so (2, 5) becomes a maximum.

U6

In linear-with-quadratic simultaneous equations, why do you substitute the linear equation into the quadratic, and not the other way round? Why must you find y from the linear equation afterwards?

Show answer

The linear equation is easy to rearrange to "y = …" with no square roots. Rearranging x2 + y2 = 13 would give y = ±√(13 − x2), which is messy and loses solutions.

Afterwards, each x must be paired with the correct y. If you put x into the quadratic, you get two y-values for each x (±), and only one of them is on the line. The linear equation gives exactly one y, the right one.

U7

In the SAM lawn question, solving 6x2 + 7x − 98 = 0 gave x = 3.5 or x = −14/3. Why is only one answer used?

Show answer

x was a length (the lawn was 3x by 2x metres). A negative x would give negative lengths, which cannot happen. The algebra does not know that. It solves the equation for every number.

So you reject x = −14/3, and say why in words, then use x = 3.5: the lawn is 10.5 m by 7 m, area 73.5 m².

U8

Two lines are drawn accurately and cross at about (1.4, 2.7). Why is that only an estimate, and when would the exam refuse to accept it?

Show answer

The crossing point satisfies both equations, but reading it off a grid is limited by the drawing: a line drawn slightly thick, or a point read to the nearest small square. The exact answer might be (1.375, 2.75).

If the question says "Show clear algebraic working", you must solve algebraically. A graphical answer scores nothing there. Graphs are for "use the graph" questions, and to check an algebraic answer is sensible.

05Exam drill

D1
