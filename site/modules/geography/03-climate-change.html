Climate Change

The last part of Paper 1, Section A — The Challenge of Natural Hazards. Evidence for climate change from the start of the Quaternary to today, the natural causes (orbital changes, solar output, volcanic activity) and the human ones (fossil fuels, agriculture, deforestation), the effects on people and the environment, and the two halves of management: mitigation reduces the causes, adaptation responds to the change. There is no named case study here — the marks come from evidence, mechanism and named schemes.

01How it actually works

One spec area, 3.1.1.4, and the smallest of the three in Section A — but it has appeared in every paper since 2019 and has supplied the opening multiple-choice question twice. The structure AQA uses is fixed and worth memorising as a four-part shape: evidence → causes (natural, then human) → effects (people, then environment) → management (mitigation, then adaptation). Almost every question is one of those eight boxes.

The Quaternary period, and what climate change actually is

Spec 3.1.1.4 "Evidence for climate change from the beginning of the Quaternary period to the present day" · AQA GCSE textbook; AQA List of Key Terms

  • Definition — a long-term change in the Earth's climate, especially a change due to an increase in average atmospheric temperature.
  • The Quaternary — the geological period from about 2.6 million years ago to the present; the Earth's most recent ice age.
  • Two epochs — the Pleistocene, then the Holocene, which began about 12,000 years ago when the last glacial ended.
  • Glacials and interglacials — cold glacials lasting roughly 100,000 years, separated by warm interglacials of roughly 10,000 years.
  • We are in an interglacial — the Holocene, which is why ice still covers Greenland and Antarctica and why the climate has been relatively stable.
  • Why this matters — it proves climate has always changed naturally, which is the counter-argument in every "is it human?" question.

Evidence 1 — the instrumental record

Spec 3.1.1.4 "Evidence for climate change" · AQA GCSE textbook; AQA Revision Notes

  • Direct measurement since 1914 — the Met Office has recorded reliable data using weather stations, satellites, weather balloons, radar and ocean buoys.
  • Temperature — the Earth's average surface air temperature has risen by about 1 °C in the last 100 years.
  • Sea level — risen 19 cm since 1900, from thermal expansion plus meltwater from ice sheets reaching the sea.
  • Ice — since 2002, Antarctica has lost 134 billion tonnes of ice a year and Greenland 287 billion tonnes a year; Arctic sea ice has thinned by 65% since 1975.
  • Ocean — ocean temperatures are the warmest since 1850, and the world's glaciers and ice sheets are shrinking.
  • Seasonal change — in the 1990s, 65 bird species nested an average of 9 days earlier than in the 1970s.

✗ "The ice is melting, so the climate is changing." — not enough for the 2-mark 2024 question, which asked how it is evidence.
✓ Say what the measurement shows and why it points one way: "Arctic sea ice extent in September 2020 was well below the 1981–2010 average, and the volume fell from 16,300 km³ in 1980 to 4,400 km³ in 2020 (1). Ice melts only when temperatures rise above freezing for longer, so a sustained decline over forty years is evidence of long-term warming rather than one warm year (1)."

Evidence 2 — proxy data from before the records

Spec 3.1.1.4 · AQA GCSE textbook; Climate Change Revision Mat

  • Proxy data — natural recorders used for the era before instruments: tree rings, fossil pollen, ice cores and ocean sediments.
  • Ice cores — Antarctic snow layers act as time capsules; records reach back about 800,000 years.
  • What ice cores hold — oxygen isotope ratios give past temperature, and melting the ice releases trapped carbon dioxide and methane to compare with today.
  • Ocean sediments — the deeper the sediment the older it is; plankton remains reveal past surface temperatures, oxygen and nutrient levels.
  • Tree rings — wide rings mean a warm, wet year; they indicate a warmer, wetter climate recently.
  • Pollen analysis — preserved pollen shows which plants grew, and so what the climate must have been.

✗ Treating proxy data as if it were a thermometer.
✓ Say the limitation and earn the evaluation mark: proxy records "are not as reliable, because these only indicate climate change rather than providing direct evidence of accurate temperatures". That caveat is the most useful line in the topic for a 9-marker.

More detail

How ice cores work. Antarctica has no permanent residents, so the layers of snow remain unaltered and build up year on year, the deeper snow being older. Cores are drilled out and analysed. Oxygen isotopes — atoms of oxygen with different numbers of neutrons — are the key: the ratio of two isotopes in the ice depends on the temperature when the snow fell, so measuring it reconstructs past temperature. Separately, air bubbles trapped in the ice release CO₂ and methane when the ice is melted, which lets scientists compare past greenhouse gas concentrations directly with present ones. Those two lines of evidence together show that CO₂ concentration tracks temperature, and that CO₂ levels are now higher than at any point in the record.

How ocean sediments work. Billions of tonnes of sediment on the sea floor act as the same kind of timeline. Oxygen isotopes in the remains of plankton are plotted through the Quaternary: the spikes represent interglacials (warmer, less ice) and the troughs represent glacials (colder, more ice). That graph is what shows the 100,000-year glacial rhythm.

Historical sources count too — paintings, diaries, harvest records and records of frost fairs on the Thames give a qualitative record for the last few hundred years.

Natural cause 1 — orbital changes (Milankovitch cycles)

Spec 3.1.1.4 "natural factors — orbital changes" · AQA Revision Notes; AQA List of Key Terms

  • Orbital changes defined — changes in the pathway of the Earth around the Sun.
  • Stretch (eccentricity) — the orbit shifts between circular and elongated over about 100,000 years.
  • Why stretch matters — an elongated orbit means the Earth meets far more variation in the energy it receives from the Sun.
  • Tilt — the axis tilts between 22.2° and 24.5° over about 42,000 years; it is currently 23.5°.
  • Why tilt matters — the greater the tilt, the more solar energy the poles receive, so the ice retreats.
  • Wobble (precession) — the axis wobbles like a spinning top over about 26,000 years, changing where the seasons fall.

✗ "The Earth moves closer to the Sun so it gets hotter."
✓ Name the cycle, give its length, and say what it changes: "Eccentricity — over roughly 100,000 years the orbit stretches from circular to elliptical, so the Earth encounters much more variation in the solar energy it receives. The 100,000-year glacial rhythm in the ice-core record matches that cycle, which is the evidence." That match is what makes it an explanation rather than a guess.

Natural cause 2 — solar output

Spec 3.1.1.4 "natural factors — solar output" · AQA Revision Notes; AQA GCSE textbook

  • Sunspots — dark patches on the Sun's surface caused by magnetic activity; they are how solar output is measured.
  • The 11-year cycle — from least activity to most and back again.
  • Maximum — more sunspots means the Sun gives off more heat, with large solar flares.
  • Minimum — solar output falls, leading to lower temperatures on Earth.
  • Scale — the effect is small and cyclical, so it cannot explain a one-directional rise.
  • Use it as the counter-argument — it is the strongest "it is natural" card, and also the easiest to knock down.

Natural cause 3 — volcanic activity

Spec 3.1.1.4 "natural factors — volcanic activity" · AQA GCSE textbook; AQA Revision Notes

  • Eruptions cool, not warm — the counter-intuitive point, and the one that earns the mark.
  • Ash — violent eruptions blast ash high into the atmosphere, where it blocks sunlight and briefly lowers temperatures.
  • Sulphur dioxide is the bigger effect — SO₂ converts to sulphuric acid droplets that act like tiny mirrors, reflecting solar radiation.
  • Duration — the droplets last far longer than the ash and can affect climate for years.
  • Volcanic winter — the name for that cooling of the lower atmosphere and the surface.
  • The named example — Mount Pinatubo, Philippines, June 1991: global temperatures dropped by approximately 0.5 °C.

Mount Pinatubo, Philippines — June 1991

  • The eruption blasted ash and gases into the upper atmosphere; the ash circled the world in about three weeks.
  • Sulphur dioxide mixed with water vapour to form sulphuric acid droplets, which reflected incoming solar radiation back into space.
  • Global temperatures fell by approximately 0.5 °C.
  • The twist worth knowing: volcanoes also emit carbon dioxide, which should trap heat. The cooling won, which shows the sulphate aerosols outweigh the CO₂ in the short term.

Human causes — the enhanced greenhouse effect

Spec 3.1.1.4 "human factors — use of fossil fuels, agriculture and deforestation" · AQA Revision Notes; Climate Change Revision Mat; 2025 question paper

  • The natural greenhouse effect — the atmosphere lets most of the Sun's short-wave radiation through to heat the surface.
  • The trap — the Earth re-emits that heat as long-wave radiation, which greenhouse gases such as CO₂ and methane absorb rather than letting it escape to space.
  • Enhanced greenhouse effect — more greenhouse gas means more energy trapped, so the planet warms. 2025's multiple-choice answer was exactly this wording.
  • Fossil fuels — burning coal, oil and gas for industry, transport and energy releases CO₂.
  • Agriculture — cattle and rice paddies release methane, a far more potent gas per molecule.
  • Deforestation — fewer trees absorb less CO₂ by photosynthesis, and burning them releases what they stored.

✗ "Greenhouse gases block the heat from the Sun." — this was option B in 2025's multiple choice and it is wrong.
✓ Greenhouse gases trap heat from the Earth in the atmosphere — they let the Sun's short-wave radiation in and absorb the Earth's long-wave radiation on the way out. Getting the direction right is the whole mark.

More detail

The proof that it is us. Natural causes have operated throughout the Quaternary, long before humans. What has changed is the rate: global temperatures have risen far faster than natural factors alone would produce, and the trend of human CO₂ emissions matches the rise in global temperature. CO₂ concentration in the atmosphere is linked to temperature in the ice-core record, and CO₂ levels are now higher than ever before in that 800,000-year record.

The composition of emissions — the UK in 2020, from the 2025 paper's Figure 3b: carbon dioxide 79%, methane 13%, nitrous oxide 5%, fluorinated gases 3%. The UK's total greenhouse gas emissions fell substantially between 1990 and 2020.

The projection — by the end of the century, global temperature could rise by 1.8–4 °C and sea levels by 28–43 cm. The IPCC has warned temperatures could increase by several degrees this century; some scenarios suggest 2–3 °C.

Where the coal is. Coal power plants account for roughly 80% of China's, 70% of India's and 50% of the USA's energy — which is why carbon capture matters as much as switching to renewables.

Effects on people

Spec 3.1.1.4 "Overview of the effects of climate change on people and the environment" · Climate Change Revision Mat; AQA GCSE textbook; 2023 Figure 2

  • Health — more skin cancer and heat stroke from higher temperatures; but winter-related deaths fall with milder winters.
  • Food and water — drought reduces supplies, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, and brings water shortages to south-east England.
  • Flooding — increased flood risk near rivers and coasts; UK flood damage could rise from £2.1 billion to £12 billion by the 2080s.
  • Insurance — difficulty getting home and contents insurance in areas at risk of flooding.
  • Economy — a 2.5 °C rise could cut global GNI by up to 2% every year; Alpine ski resorts may close or shorten their seasons.
  • Some gains — new jobs in prediction and protection; crop yields in northern Europe rise; more UK tourism and staycations.

✗ Copying the key off the figure. The 2023 report: "They listed the effects as shown in the key and linked these to the location, but such lifting was not creditworthy."
✓ Take the clue and develop it: "Figure 2 shows lower crop yields in sub-Saharan Africa. This means that food supplies fall and prices rise, which leads to increased food insecurity, malnutrition and, in the worst cases, people being displaced from their homes in search of food."

✗ Writing about wildlife when the question says "on people", or drifting into causes. Both are named in the 2023 report as reasons candidates lost marks.

Effects on the environment

Spec 3.1.1.4 · Climate Change Revision Mat; AQA GCSE textbook

  • Ice loss — species that depend on sea ice suffer as habitat disappears: polar bears, seals, penguins.
  • Sea level rise — increases coastal flooding and erosion, destroying coastal habitats as well as settlements.
  • Drought and habitat loss — increased drought in sub-Saharan Africa and the Mediterranean removes habitat outright.
  • Fire, pests and disease — more forest fires and more pests and disease, destroying habitats.
  • Warmer and more acidic water — warm rivers affect marine wildlife (the Ganges river dolphin); East Asian fisheries are expected to decline.
  • Shifting zones — the boreal tree line is expected to retreat north; less Arctic sea ice allows more shipping and gas and oil extraction.

Mitigation — reducing the causes

Spec 3.1.1.4 "mitigation — alternative energy production, carbon capture, planting trees, international agreements" · Climate Change Revision Mat; AQA Revision Notes; 2019 and 2025 mark schemes

  • Definition — mitigation reduces the causes of climate change by reducing greenhouse gases in the atmosphere.
  • Alternative energy — solar, wind, geothermal, wave, tidal, biomass, hydro-electric and nuclear all generate power without burning fossil fuels.
  • Carbon capture — CO₂ is trapped from emissions before it reaches the atmosphere and stored underground; up to 90% can be captured.
  • Planting trees — afforestation removes CO₂ from the atmosphere through photosynthesis; cheap and quick at large scale.
  • International agreements — the only mechanism that can force companies and countries to act together.
  • The weakness — most mitigation is expensive, slow to take effect, and relies on cooperation that cannot be enforced.

Named mitigation schemes

  • Carbon capture and storage — Boundary Dam, Canada. Captures 90% of the CO₂ from its assigned unit, although only 15% of the plant's total. CO₂ is removed from the waste gases of power stations and stored underground in old oil and gas fields or coal mines.
  • Afforestation — South Korea. Almost completely deforested after Japanese occupation and the Korean War; a national replanting effort has since planted 11 billion trees, and two-thirds of the country is now covered by forest.
  • Geothermal — Iceland. Supplies 20% of electricity and heats 92% of homes, saving 37% of CO₂ emissions and $8.2 billion — and now powers carbon capture and storage.
  • Nuclear — Hinkley Point, Somerset. Its only negative product is radioactive waste, which has no climate impact; uranium and plutonium supplies will last over 300 years. The risk is a meltdown.
  • Hydro-electric — the Three Gorges Dam, China. Emission-free, but it disturbs natural habitats and requires locks for shipping.
  • The Paris Agreement, 2015. Signed by every country except Nicaragua and Syria, until the US withdrew in 2017. It agreed to keep the global temperature increase below 2 °C, review progress every 5 years, peak greenhouse gases as soon as possible, and provide $100 billion a year to support climate change initiatives. The criticism: there is no means of enforcement — the targets are aims.

Adaptation — responding to the change

Spec 3.1.1.4 "adaptation — change in agricultural systems, managing water supply, reducing risk from rising sea levels" · Climate Change Revision Mat; AQA Revision Notes

  • Definition — adaptation responds to climate change by reducing its negative effects, rather than its causes.
  • Agriculture — production may move location to suit the new climate; irrigation where rainfall falls; new crops and drought-resistant varieties.
  • Water supply, demand side — more efficient devices, re-using grey water, rationing, collecting rainwater.
  • Water supply, supply side — water transfer schemes and desalination plants turning seawater into fresh water; artificial glaciers in the Himalayas.
  • Rising sea levels — sea walls, relocating people at risk, houses on stilts, restoring mangrove swamps, improved river defences.
  • Big engineering — the Thames Barrier model could be replicated on other major rivers.

The Maldives — adaptation at national scale

  • The Maldives is likely the country that will suffer most from sea level rise, because the whole country could disappear.
  • It has built an artificial island, Hulhumalé, raised above projected sea levels, which has attracted thousands of residents from the overcrowded capital, Malé.
  • Why it is the best adaptation example: it shows adaptation working where mitigation cannot help — the Maldives emits almost nothing, so cutting its own emissions would change nothing about its fate.

✗ Using "mitigation" and "adaptation" interchangeably — the single most costly error in this topic.
✓ Mitigation reduces the causes (fewer greenhouse gases): renewables, carbon capture, tree planting, international agreements. Adaptation responds to the effects (living with the change): sea walls, drought-resistant crops, desalination, relocation. A sea wall does nothing about CO₂; a wind farm does nothing about a flood next year.

The 9-mark frame — climate change

The two climate change 9-markers AQA has set are "Managing climate change involves both reducing causes (mitigation) and responding to change (adaptation). Do you agree?" (2020) and "To what extent is climate change the result of human actions?" (2021). Both are three paragraphs plus a conclusion, with evaluation inside each paragraph.

  • Intro — define the key term and state the line. "Mitigation reduces the causes of climate change by cutting greenhouse gases, whereas adaptation responds by reducing its negative effects; both are needed, but mitigation is the only permanent solution."
  • Paragraph 1 — the first side, with a named scheme and figures. "Mitigation works: carbon capture at Boundary Dam in Canada traps 90% of the CO₂ from its unit, and South Korea's 11 billion trees have returned two-thirds of the country to forest. However, Boundary Dam captures only 15% of the plant's total, which shows how partial the technology still is."
  • Paragraph 2 — the other side, with its own evidence. "Adaptation is unavoidable because warming already locked in will raise sea levels by 28–43 cm by 2100. The Maldives' artificial island Hulhumalé rehouses people above projected sea levels — and crucially, the Maldives emits almost nothing, so no amount of its own mitigation would save it."
  • Paragraph 3 — the factor that decides between them. "The real constraint is cooperation. The Paris Agreement committed every country but two to keeping warming below 2 °C with $100 billion a year, but it has no enforcement mechanism and the US withdrew in 2017. Therefore mitigation is theoretically superior but politically fragile, while adaptation can be delivered by one country alone."
  • Conclusion that ranks. "Overall I agree that both are needed, but they are not equal: mitigation is the only approach that addresses the cause, so it must lead — yet because it depends on unenforceable global agreement, adaptation is what actually protects people in the meantime."

Sentence starters: "This means that…" · "As a result…" · "However, the limitation of this is…" · "This is only effective to the extent that…" · "The most significant factor is… because…" · "Overall, I agree definitely / mostly / partly / slightly / not at all, because…"

03Understanding it

U1

If the climate has always changed, how can scientists say the current change is caused by humans?

Show answer

Three arguments, and you need all three.

Rate. Natural causes have operated throughout the 2.6-million-year Quaternary, producing a rhythm of 100,000-year glacials and 10,000-year interglacials. Global temperatures have now risen at a higher rate than natural factors alone would cause — about 1 °C in a century, against cycles that take tens of thousands of years.

Correlation. The trend of human CO₂ emissions matches the rise in global temperature, and the ice-core record shows CO₂ concentration has always tracked temperature. CO₂ is now higher than at any point in the 800,000-year record.

Elimination. The natural causes are each cyclical or short-lived. Sunspot activity runs on an 11-year cycle, so it cannot produce a one-directional rise. Volcanic activity cools the planet. Orbital changes operate over tens of thousands of years, far too slowly.

The exam point: in a "to what extent" question, concede the natural causes first and then show why they cannot account for the rate. That structure is what gets you into Level 3.

U2

Why do scientists drill ice in Antarctica rather than anywhere else, and what exactly do they get from it?

Show answer

Why Antarctica: it is a wilderness with no permanent residents, so the layers of snow remain unaltered. Each year's snowfall is buried by the next, so the deeper you drill the older the ice — an unbroken archive stretching back about 800,000 years.

Two separate records come out of one core. The ice itself contains oxygen isotopes — oxygen atoms with different numbers of neutrons. The ratio between two of them depends on the temperature when the snow fell, so measuring the ratio reconstructs past temperature.

Separately, air bubbles trapped in the ice release carbon dioxide and methane when the core is melted, giving a direct sample of the atmosphere at that time.

Why that combination is so powerful: one core gives you both temperature and greenhouse gas concentration for the same moment, so you can see that the two rise and fall together. That is the evidential link between CO₂ and warming, and it is why a question about ice cores is really a question about causation.

U3

Volcanoes release carbon dioxide. So why does a big eruption make the Earth colder?

Show answer

Because two opposite effects compete, and in the short term the cooling one wins by a long way.

The warming effect: eruptions release CO₂, which should trap heat.

The cooling effects: first, ash blasted into the atmosphere blocks sunlight — after Pinatubo in June 1991 the ash circled the world in about three weeks. Second and more importantly, sulphur dioxide converts to sulphuric acid droplets which act like tiny mirrors, reflecting solar radiation back into space. The ash settles within weeks, but the droplets persist for years, which is why the cooling can affect climate for a long time. The result is called a volcanic winter.

The measured outcome: global temperatures dropped by approximately 0.5 °C after Pinatubo.

Use it like this: this is why volcanic activity is listed as a natural cause of climate change, not of warming — and it is why anyone claiming volcanoes explain modern warming has the sign the wrong way round.

U4

Why does sea level rise even where no ice is melting nearby?

Show answer

Two mechanisms, and only one of them involves ice.

Thermal expansion. Warm water occupies more volume than cold water. As ocean temperatures rise — they are now the warmest since 1850 — the same mass of seawater simply takes up more space, so the surface rises everywhere.

Meltwater. Glaciers and ice sheets on land melt and the water flows into the sea, adding mass. Antarctica has lost 134 billion tonnes a year and Greenland 287 billion tonnes a year since 2002. Note that melting sea ice does not raise sea level, because it is already floating and displacing its own weight.

The numbers: 19 cm since 1900, with a further 28–43 cm projected by 2100.

Why it matters for the answer: low-lying coasts face a double threat — higher baseline sea level plus more intense tropical storms producing more severe storm surges on top of it.

U5

What is the real difference between mitigation and adaptation, and why can't you just do one?

Show answer

Mitigation attacks the cause. It reduces greenhouse gases in the atmosphere: renewable and nuclear energy, carbon capture, planting trees, international agreements. It is the only approach that can actually stop climate change.

Adaptation attacks the effect. It reduces the damage from change that is already happening: sea walls, relocation, drought-resistant crops, desalination, restoring mangroves. It does nothing about CO₂.

Why mitigation alone fails: the warming already in the system is locked in, so sea level will keep rising for decades even if emissions stopped today. The Maldives could cut its emissions to zero and still disappear, because its fate is determined by everyone else's emissions.

Why adaptation alone fails: it is a treadmill. Every sea wall has to be raised again, every crop replaced again, and the cost compounds — UK flood damage alone could rise from £2.1 billion to £12 billion by the 2080s. Adaptation buys time but never ends the problem.

The judgement: they operate on different timescales. Mitigation is the long-term solution; adaptation is the short-term necessity. A country's wealth and its share of emissions determine which it can realistically pursue.

U6

Are international agreements actually essential for managing climate change, as the 2024 question put it?

Show answer

The case that they are: greenhouse gases mix globally, so one country's cuts are cancelled by another's emissions — coal accounts for roughly 80% of China's, 70% of India's and 50% of the USA's energy, which no UK policy can touch. Agreements are also, as the revision notes put it, "one of the few ways to force companies to introduce environmentally friendly programmes". The Paris Agreement 2015 achieved near-universal commitment to keeping warming below 2 °C, with five-yearly reviews and $100 billion a year of support for poorer countries.

The case that they are not: there is no means to enforce these goals — they are solely aims. The USA withdrew in 2017, showing how easily a major emitter can leave. And a great deal of effective action happens without them: South Korea planted 11 billion trees as a national project, Iceland built its geothermal system for its own economic reasons, and the Maldives built Hulhumalé alone.

The judgement worth writing: essential but not sufficient. Agreements are the only mechanism that can coordinate mitigation at the required scale, but because they cannot be enforced they have to be paired with national action and with adaptation.

U7

Climate change has some positive effects. Does that weaken the case for acting on it?

Show answer

No, but naming them is what shows a balanced answer.

The genuine gains: milder winters mean winter-related deaths decrease; less heating is needed in northern Europe, so crops and forests grow more; crop yields in Europe are expected to rise; the UK may see more tourists and more staycations, and a wider variety of crops such as grapes for wine; there will be new jobs in prediction and protection; and less Arctic sea ice allows more shipping and resource extraction.

Why they do not balance out: the gains are concentrated in already wealthy high-latitude countries, while the losses fall on those least able to cope — drought reducing food and water in sub-Saharan Africa, 70% of Asia at increased flood risk, and small island states such as the Maldives facing disappearance. European crop yields rise but will require more irrigation, so even the gains carry costs. And a 2.5 °C rise could cut global GNI by up to 2% every year.

Use it like this: conceding the positives and then showing they are unevenly distributed is a neat AO3 move — it converts a list into a judgement.

U8

Why does AQA keep testing climate change through Arctic sea ice graphs rather than asking about it directly?

Show answer

Because sea ice is the cleanest available evidence, and it tests skills and understanding at the same time.

As evidence it is unusually good: it is measured directly by satellite, it has a long continuous record, and it responds to temperature almost immediately. Both extent (area) and volume have fallen — volume in September from 16,300 km³ in 1980 to 4,400 km³ in 2020 — and that is a sustained forty-year decline rather than a noisy signal.

As an exam question it does three jobs at once: reading a graph or map (AO4), calculating a mean, a difference or a comparison (AO4), and then explaining why the change is evidence of warming (AO2). 2023 and 2024 both opened Section A this way.

What to practise, then: comparing two lines or shaded areas on a map in a single comparative sentence using "whereas"; calculating a mean from a row of figures; and the one-sentence explanation — "ice melts only where temperatures stay above freezing for longer, so a continuous decline indicates sustained warming."

04Exam drill

What each command word actually wants

Climate change supplies Section A's short, precise marks and, in 2020 and 2021, its 9-marker. Match the shape to the tariff exactly.

  • 1 mark, multiple choice. Shade one circle only. The usual trap is the natural/human distinction — in 2023 a minority picked deforestation as a natural factor.
  • 1 mark, calculate. Read whether it wants a difference, a mean or a median, and whether it wants a decimal place. Show your working.
  • Compare (2). Write a comparative sentence using "whereas" or "compared with", with figures from both things. A description of each in turn is not a comparison.
  • Describe changes (2). Overall trend, then a supporting figure, then any anomaly.
  • Explain / Suggest (2). Point (1) plus development (1). For carbon capture, the mark scheme caps you at 1 mark if you link to climate change without mentioning capture or storage.
  • Suggest how… (4). Level-marked. Two developed points, each taking a clue from the figure and extending it with your own understanding.
  • "Do you agree?" (4). Still needs a judgement, however short — not just two reasons.
  • Explain (6). Two or three paragraphs with evidence; explanation is enough, no judgement required.
  • Do you agree? / To what extent? (9 + 3 SPaG). Both sides with named schemes and figures, evaluation in each paragraph, and a conclusion that ranks — definitely, mostly, partly, slightly or not at all.

Twelve questions in the real AQA spread: two short, four 4-markers, four 6-markers and two 9-markers. The 6- and 9-mark answers are plans — those are level-marked, so the mark points are a coverage target rather than a tick list.

D1

Suggest how changes in the extent of Arctic sea ice are evidence of climate change. (2)

Show answer

The September extent and volume of Arctic sea ice have fallen continuously over four decades — volume dropped from 16,300 km³ in 1980 to 4,400 km³ in 2020, and the ice has thinned by 65% since 1975 1

Ice only melts where temperatures stay above freezing for longer each year, so a sustained decline over that timescale indicates long-term warming rather than normal year-to-year variation 1

D2

State three pieces of evidence for climate change from the beginning of the Quaternary period to the present day. (3)

Show answer

Ice cores — Antarctic layers going back about 800,000 years, holding oxygen isotopes and trapped carbon dioxide and methane 1

Temperature records — reliable instrumental data since 1914, showing a rise of about 1 °C in a century 1

Tree rings, which indicate a warmer, wetter climate recently — also accept pollen analysis, ocean sediments, historical sources, shrinking glaciers, rising sea level, or earlier bird nesting 1

D3

Explain how alternative energy production and planting trees may help to reduce the rate of climate change. (4)

Show answer

Alternative energy — solar, wind, geothermal, wave, tidal, biomass, hydro-electric and nuclear — generates electricity without burning fossil fuels 1; this means less carbon dioxide is released into the atmosphere, so less of the Earth's long-wave radiation is trapped and the rate of warming slows. Iceland's geothermal supply heats 92% of homes and saves 37% of the country's CO₂ emissions 1

Planting trees removes carbon dioxide that is already in the atmosphere, because trees absorb it through photosynthesis and lock the carbon into wood 1; afforestation is cheap and can be done quickly at large scale — South Korea, almost completely deforested after the Korean War, has planted 11 billion trees and is now two-thirds covered by forest 1

Note the two different mechanisms: renewables prevent new emissions, trees remove existing CO₂. Saying so is what separates a developed answer from a listed one.

D4

Suggest how climate change may have effects on people. (4)

Show answer

Level-marked, and the focus is people, not wildlife. Take a clue and develop it; do not list what is in the figure.

Lower crop yields and freshwater shortages in regions such as sub-Saharan Africa mean that food supplies fall and prices rise 1; as a result people face increased food insecurity, malnutrition and even starvation, and may be displaced from their homes as they move in search of food and water 1

Increased flood damage affects people near rivers and coasts as sea levels rise and storms intensify 1; this leads to homes being destroyed, difficulty obtaining home and contents insurance, and large economic costs — UK flood damage could rise from £2.1 billion to £12 billion by the 2080s, while a 2.5 °C rise could cut global GNI by up to 2% every year 1

Health effects are equally valid: higher temperatures increase the risk of skin cancer and heat stroke, although milder winters mean winter-related deaths decrease.

D5

"International agreements are essential for managing climate change." Do you agree? Explain your answer. (4)

Show answer

Level-marked, and a judgement is required even at 4 marks.

They are essential because greenhouse gases mix globally, so cuts by one country are cancelled out by another's emissions — coal supplies roughly 80% of China's, 70% of India's and 50% of the USA's energy 1; international agreements are one of the few ways to force countries and companies to act together, and the Paris Agreement 2015 committed almost every nation to keeping warming below 2 °C, reviewing progress every five years and providing $100 billion a year 1

However, they have no means of enforcement — the targets are solely aims, and the USA withdrew in 2017, showing how easily a major emitter can leave 1; much effective action also happens nationally without them, such as South Korea's 11 billion trees or the Maldives' Hulhumalé island. Overall I partly agree: agreements are essential for mitigation at the required scale, but not sufficient on their own 1

D6

Explain how an increase in greenhouse gases causes the Earth to warm. (4)

Show answer

The atmosphere allows most of the Sun's heat, which arrives as short-wave radiation, to pass straight through and heat the Earth's surface 1

The Earth then gives off that heat again as long-wave radiation, and greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide and methane are able to absorb it rather than letting it escape to space — this absorption is what warms the planet, and it is a natural process without which the Earth would be far colder 1

Human activity increases the concentration of these gases: burning fossil fuels for industry, transport and energy releases carbon dioxide; agriculture, especially cattle and rice paddies, releases methane; and deforestation both removes trees that would absorb CO₂ and releases the carbon they stored when they are burnt 1

With more greenhouse gas in the atmosphere, more energy is trapped and the planet warms — the enhanced greenhouse effect. Carbon dioxide made up 79% of UK greenhouse gas emissions in 2020, with methane a further 13% 1

D7

Explain the natural causes of climate change. (6)

Show answer

Level-marked. Three causes, each with a mechanism and a number.

Orbital changes. The pathway of the Earth around the Sun varies in three ways: eccentricity, where the orbit shifts between circular and elongated over about 100,000 years; tilt, which varies between 22.2° and 24.5° over about 42,000 years and is currently 23.5°; and precession, the wobble of the axis, over about 26,000 years 1; these change how much solar energy the Earth receives and where it falls — a greater tilt sends more energy to the poles, melting ice — and the 100,000-year eccentricity cycle matches the glacial rhythm recorded in ice cores 1

Solar output. The Sun's energy output varies with sunspot activity, measured by the dark patches caused by magnetic activity on its surface, on a cycle of about 11 years 1; at maximum the Sun gives off more heat and produces solar flares, while at minimum output falls and temperatures on Earth are lower 1

Volcanic activity. Violent eruptions blast ash, gases and liquids into the atmosphere, where ash blocks out the Sun and briefly lowers temperatures 1; more importantly, sulphur dioxide converts to sulphuric acid droplets which act like tiny mirrors reflecting solar radiation, and because these last far longer than ash they can affect climate for years — this is a volcanic winter, and after Mount Pinatubo erupted in June 1991 global temperatures dropped by approximately 0.5 °C 1

D8

"Human activity is the main cause of climate change." Use evidence to support this statement. (6)

Show answer

Level-marked. Evidence is the command word here, so every claim needs a figure or a record behind it.

The mechanism is established. Greenhouse gases let the Sun's short-wave radiation through but absorb the long-wave radiation the Earth re-emits, trapping heat 1; humans have increased their concentration through burning fossil fuels, agriculture and deforestation, and carbon dioxide alone made up 79% of UK greenhouse gas emissions in 2020 1

The ice-core evidence links CO₂ to temperature. Antarctic ice cores going back about 800,000 years contain both oxygen isotopes, which reveal past temperature, and trapped carbon dioxide and methane, which reveal past atmospheric composition 1; these show that CO₂ concentration has always tracked temperature, and that CO₂ levels today are higher than at any point in that record 1

The rate rules out natural causes. Global temperatures have risen by about 1 °C in a century and sea levels by 19 cm since 1900, which is far faster than natural factors alone would produce 1; the natural causes are each cyclical or short-lived — sunspot activity runs on an 11-year cycle, orbital changes take tens of thousands of years, and volcanic activity actually cools the planet, as Pinatubo's 0.5 °C drop in 1991 shows — so none can account for a sustained one-directional rise, and the trend of human CO₂ emissions matches the temperature curve 1

D9

Explain how adaptation strategies can reduce the impacts of climate change. (6)

Show answer

Level-marked. The spec names three areas — agriculture, water supply and rising sea levels — so use all three.

Agricultural systems. Production may move location to suit the changing climate, irrigation can be installed where rainfall is decreasing, and farmers can switch to different crops and drought-resistant varieties 1; this means food production continues in regions where the previous crop would now fail, reducing the food insecurity that drought is expected to cause in sub-Saharan Africa 1

Managing water supply. Demand can be reduced by installing more efficient devices, re-using grey water, rationing and collecting rainwater 1; supply can be increased by water transfer schemes, by desalination plants that turn seawater into fresh water, and, in mountainous areas such as the Himalayas, by building artificial glaciers to provide water year-round — all of which protect areas facing shortage, including south-east England 1

Reducing the risk from rising sea levels. Sea defences can be built, river defences improved to cope with higher rainfall, mangrove swamps restored as natural buffers, and people at risk relocated; large-scale schemes such as the Thames Barrier could be replicated on other major rivers 1; the Maldives, which could disappear entirely, has built the artificial island of Hulhumalé above projected sea levels and attracted thousands of residents from Malé — which shows adaptation working where mitigation cannot, since the Maldives emits almost nothing itself 1

D10

Explain how proxy data provides evidence of climate change before instrumental records began. (6)

Show answer

Level-marked. Name the proxy, explain the mechanism, state the limitation.

Ice cores. In Antarctica there are no permanent residents, so annual layers of snow build up unaltered, the deeper ice being the older — cores drilled there reach back about 800,000 years 1; the ratio of two oxygen isotopes in the ice depends on the temperature when the snow fell, so it reconstructs past temperature, while air bubbles release trapped carbon dioxide and methane when the core is melted, allowing direct comparison with present levels 1

Ocean sediments. Billions of tonnes of sediment on the sea floor form the same kind of timeline, the deeper sediment being older 1; the remains of plankton reveal past surface water temperatures and oxygen and nutrient levels, and plotting their oxygen isotopes through the Quaternary produces spikes representing warm interglacials and troughs representing cold glacials — which is how the 100,000-year glacial rhythm was identified 1

Tree rings, pollen and historical sources. Wide tree rings indicate warm, wet years and have indicated a warmer, wetter climate recently; preserved pollen shows which plants grew and therefore what the climate must have been; and written and painted historical sources cover the last few centuries 1

The limitation. These records are not as reliable as instruments, because they only indicate climate change rather than providing accurate temperatures — which is why they are combined with each other and with the direct record kept by the Met Office since 1914 1

D11

"Managing climate change involves both reducing causes (mitigation) and responding to change (adaptation)."

Do you agree? Explain your answer. (9)

Show answer

Written as a plan, level-marked. Define both terms in the first sentence, give each a paragraph with named schemes and figures, evaluate inside each, and rank them in the conclusion.

Intro defining the terms and stating the line. Mitigation reduces the causes of climate change by cutting greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, whereas adaptation responds by reducing its negative effects; I agree that both are needed, but they are not equally important 1

Mitigation — what works. Alternative energy avoids emissions altogether: Iceland's geothermal supplies 20% of its electricity, heats 92% of homes and saves 37% of its CO₂ emissions and $8.2 billion 1

Carbon capture traps CO₂ before it reaches the atmosphere and stores it underground — Boundary Dam in Canada captures 90% of the CO₂ from its assigned unit — and afforestation removes CO₂ already there, as South Korea's 11 billion trees have done 1

However, Boundary Dam captures only 15% of the plant's total output, so the technology is partial, and renewables are expensive and intermittent — which is why mitigation has not yet bent the emissions curve far enough 1

Adaptation — why it is unavoidable. Warming already locked into the system means sea levels will rise a further 28–43 cm by 2100 regardless of what is done now, so some response is compulsory rather than optional 1

Adaptation delivers concrete protection: sea walls, relocating people, restoring mangroves, replicating the Thames Barrier, drought-resistant crops, desalination and water transfer schemes. The Maldives, which could disappear entirely, has built the artificial island of Hulhumalé above projected sea levels 1

However, adaptation is a treadmill — every defence must eventually be raised again, and UK flood damage alone could rise from £2.1 billion to £12 billion by the 2080s, so the cost compounds indefinitely 1

The factor that decides between them. The constraint on mitigation is cooperation rather than technology: the Paris Agreement 2015 committed almost every country to staying below 2 °C with $100 billion a year, but it has no enforcement mechanism and the USA withdrew in 2017 — whereas adaptation can be delivered by a single country acting alone 1

Conclusion that ranks. Overall I agree strongly: mitigation must lead, because it is the only approach that addresses the cause and so the only one that can ever end the problem — but because it depends on unenforceable global agreement and the Maldives cannot save itself by cutting its own negligible emissions, adaptation is what actually protects people in the meantime 1

D12

To what extent is climate change the result of human actions? (9)

Show answer

Written as a plan, level-marked. "To what extent" means the answer is a measured judgement: concede the natural causes properly, then show why they cannot account for the recent rate.

Intro. Climate has always changed naturally, through the 2.6-million-year Quaternary with its 100,000-year glacials and 10,000-year interglacials; but the recent warming is human in origin to a very large extent, because natural causes cannot explain its rate 1

The natural case, taken seriously. Orbital changes alter the solar energy the Earth receives: eccentricity over about 100,000 years, tilt between 22.2° and 24.5° over about 42,000 years, and precession over about 26,000 years — and the 100,000-year cycle matches the glacial rhythm in the ice-core record exactly 1

Solar output varies with sunspot activity on an 11-year cycle, giving more heat at maximum and less at minimum; and volcanic eruptions change climate too, as Mount Pinatubo's did in June 1991 1

Why the natural case fails for recent warming. Each natural cause is cyclical or has the wrong sign: sunspots oscillate on an 11-year cycle and cannot produce a one-directional rise, orbital changes operate over tens of thousands of years and are far too slow, and volcanic activity cools the planet — Pinatubo lowered global temperatures by approximately 0.5 °C 1

The human case — mechanism. Greenhouse gases let the Sun's short-wave radiation through but absorb the long-wave radiation the Earth re-emits, trapping heat; humans have raised their concentration by burning fossil fuels, by agriculture releasing methane, and by deforestation, which both removes CO₂ absorbers and releases stored carbon 1

The human case — evidence. Antarctic ice cores going back 800,000 years show CO₂ concentration has always tracked temperature, and CO₂ is now higher than at any point in that record 1

The trend of human CO₂ emissions matches the rise in global temperature, which is about 1 °C in a century, with sea levels up 19 cm since 1900 and Greenland losing 287 billion tonnes of ice a year since 2002 — all far faster than natural factors alone would produce 1

The honest caveat, which earns AO3. Evidence before 1914 comes from proxy data — ice cores, ocean sediments, tree rings and pollen — which only indicates climate change rather than giving accurate temperatures, so the long baseline against which modern warming is judged is less precise than the modern record itself 1

Conclusion that quantifies the extent. To a very large extent: natural factors genuinely drive the long Quaternary cycles and still operate, but they are either too slow, too cyclical or of the wrong sign to explain a 1 °C rise in a century, and only human emissions correlate with it — so natural causes set the background and human actions are responsible for the change we are now living through 1
