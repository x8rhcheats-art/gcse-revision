Maximum extent of ice cover across the UK

Spec 3.1.3.4 · 2019 Q5.1; 2022 Q5.1–5.3

  • Nearly all of the north was covered — ice reached across Scotland, Ireland, Wales and northern England.
  • The southern limit — the ice sheet stopped roughly across the Midlands; southern England was never glaciated.
  • The uplands were the ice centres — Scotland, the Lake District, Snowdonia and the Pennines held the thickest ice.
  • Scandinavia held more — the main European ice sheet was centred over Scandinavia, deeper than over the British Isles.
  • This is why the landforms are where they are — corries and troughs only occur in the glaciated uplands.
  • Describe with compass directions and named places — that is what the 2-mark version is marked on.

✗ "Most of Britain was covered in ice." — one vague statement for a 2-mark describe question.
✓ "Ice covered the whole of Scotland and Ireland, together with Wales and northern England as far south as the Midlands (1), including the Lake District and the Shetland Islands, whereas southern England remained ice-free (1)."

More detail

The 2019 paper gave a map of ice extent across the British Isles with the Shetland Islands, Scotland, Ireland, Wales, England, the Lake District and the Irish Sea labelled, and asked for a 2-mark description. Use the exam technique guide's structure for describing a map: the trend (the distribution is northern, decreasing southwards), the extremities (the far north entirely covered, the far south entirely uncovered), and named places as the evidence.

The 2022 paper took it further, giving a map of ice depth over north-west Europe and asking candidates to complete a cross-section of ice depth between two points, then to compare the maximum depth of ice over the British Isles with that over Scandinavia. The comparison is that the ice was deeper over Scandinavia, which was the centre of the main European ice sheet, than over the British Isles. The true/false statement in the same question tested two common errors: ice did not cover the whole of the British Isles, and the main ice sheet was not centred in southern Europe.

Why this spec point exists. It explains the geography of the rest of the module. The Lake District, Snowdonia and the Scottish Highlands have corries, arêtes, pyramidal peaks, glacial troughs and ribbon lakes because they held the ice; East Anglia has till plains and drumlins because it lay under or at the edge of the ice; southern England has neither. When a question asks why a landform is in a particular place, the extent of the last ice sheet is the first-order answer.

Freeze-thaw weathering

Spec 3.1.3.4 · Glacial Landscapes Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms

  • Also called frost-shattering — it needs temperatures oscillating around freezing and rock with many cracks.
  • Step 1 — water enters cracks in the rock face during the warmer day.
  • Step 2 — it freezes at night and expands by 9%, exerting pressure on the surrounding rock.
  • Step 3 — repeated freezing and thawing enlarges the crack until pieces break off.
  • Two landscape features — scree slopes and block-fields.
  • It feeds the erosion — the shattered debris becomes the abrasive material carried by the glacier.
More detail

AQA's definition: freeze-thaw weathering, "also called frost-shattering… occurs in cold climates when temperatures are often around freezing point and where exposed rocks contain many cracks. Water enters the cracks during the warmer day and freezes during the colder night. As the water turns into ice it expands and exerts pressure on the surrounding rock, causing pieces to break off."

The mat's version adds the quantity and the repetition: "Water enters cracks in the rock face. It freezes and expands. This causes the crack to become larger by 10%. Subsequent freezing and thawing enlarges the crack causing rock to break away. Large blocks of rock can be shattered by repeated freeze-thaw weathering." The school's quick-fire list gives the expansion itself as 9% — that is the number to quote.

The two landscape features the mat names are scree slopes — accumulations of angular shattered fragments at the base of a rock face — and block-fields, areas of shattered blocks on a plateau surface.

Why it matters more than it looks. Freeze-thaw is not just a background process; it is the supply mechanism for glacial erosion. It shatters rock off the back wall of a corrie, which makes the back wall steep and creates the armchair shape; it supplies the angular debris that falls onto the glacier surface and down crevasses to become the sandpaper for abrasion; and it is what keeps the arête sharp once two corries have cut back towards each other. Mention it in every landform answer.

Glacial erosion — abrasion and plucking

Spec 3.1.3.4 · Glacial Landscapes Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms; 2024 Q5.1; 2019 Q5.4

  • Plucking — meltwater at the glacier base freezes around loose rock, and as the ice moves forward it pulls the rock away.
  • Where plucking acts — along rock joints, which is why it leaves a jagged, pitted surface.
  • Abrasion — rocks frozen into the base of the glacier act like sandpaper, scratching and scraping the bedrock below.
  • Abrasion leaves striations — scratches on the bedrock that record the direction the ice moved.
  • The two work together — plucking supplies the tools; abrasion uses them.
  • Weathering is not erosion — freeze-thaw is weathering; only abrasion and plucking are glacial erosion.

✗ Picking freeze-thaw or rotational slip as "a process of glacial erosion" — the 2019 MC used exactly those distractors. The answer was plucking.
✓ Erosion: abrasion and plucking. Weathering: freeze-thaw. Movement: rotational slip and bulldozing. Deposition: till and outwash. Keep the four lists separate.

More detail

The mat's definitions, which are the ones to reproduce: "Plucking — as a glacier moves over the Earth's surface, meltwater freezes around loose rock, pulling them away. Abrasion — rocks frozen to the base of a glacier act like sandpaper scratching and scraping the bedrock below."

AQA's own wording is close: plucking is "a type of erosion where melt water in the glacier freezes onto rocks, and as the ice moves forward it plucks or pulls out large pieces along the rock joints"; abrasion is "erosion caused by rocks and boulders in the base of the glacier acting like a giant file scratching and scraping the rocks below."

The 2024 paper tested abrasion as a 1-mark multiple choice, and the distractors are worth knowing because each is the definition of something else: "glacial ice moves in a circular motion causing hollows to form in the landscape" is rotational slip; "meltwater freezes beneath the ice and tears away pieces of loose rock" is plucking; "water freezes in cracks and expands, causing the rock to break down" is freeze-thaw weathering. The correct answer was "rocks trapped beneath the glacier scratch and smooth the bedrock underneath".

The link to make in a 6-marker. The two processes are in a feedback loop. Plucking pulls rock out of the bedrock along joints; that rock becomes frozen into the base of the ice; it is then dragged over the bedrock and abrades it; the abraded surface is smoothed while the plucked surface stays jagged. That is why a roche moutonnée has a smooth, striated up-ice side and a jagged, plucked down-ice side — and why saying "the glacier eroded the valley" scores nothing while naming the loop scores well.

How glaciers move, and how they transport material

Spec 3.1.3.4 · Glacial Landscapes Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms; 2023 Q5.2; 2025 Q5.1

  • Ice forms by compression — snow accumulates in shady north-facing hollows and is compressed into ice.
  • Basal flow — the glacier slides over the bedrock on a film of meltwater.
  • Internal deformation — where it is too cold for basal flow, the ice moves like plastic.
  • Rotational slip — the ice moves in a circular motion, eroding hollows into bowl shapes.
  • Bulldozing — the snout pushes material of all shapes and sizes ahead of it by the sheer weight of the ice.
  • Three carrying positions — supraglacial (on the surface), englacial (inside) and subglacial (at the base).

Transport — the three positions, from the mat

The 2024 6-marker was "explain the different ways that glacial material is transported and deposited", so these three need to be distinguishable, with the source of the material in each case.

  • Pushed in front — bulldozing. "The snout of the glacier bulldozes material which is moved forward by the sheer weight of the ice." AQA: "ice pushes material of all shapes and sizes as it moves slowly forward."
  • Carried on the surface — supraglacial. "Material is also carried on the surface of the glacier as the result of weathering on the mountainsides above the glacier" — that is, freeze-thaw shatters rock off the valley sides and it falls onto the ice.
  • Carried inside and at the base — englacial and subglacial. "Material is also carried inside the glacier, on the base where plucking has occurred. It is also found inside where rocks fall down crevasses."

Why deposited material is mixed in size and shape — the 2022 1-marker. Because the glacier carries everything, regardless of size, in all three positions at once, and ice has no sorting mechanism: unlike water, it does not drop the heaviest material first. So when the ice melts, boulders, sand and clay are all dumped together — which is exactly what till is, "an unsorted mixture of sand, clay and boulders".

More detail

The mat's account of glacier movement, which is the sequence to learn: "Snow accumulates in shady north-facing hollows in upland areas. It becomes compressed forming ice. Gravity causes it to flow over the lip. The glacier slides over the bedrock on a film of meltwater, a process called basal flow. The glacier can also move when it is too cold for basal flow by a process called internal deformation, when the ice moves like plastic."

AQA defines rotational slip as occurring "when the ice moves in a circular motion. This process can help to erode hollows in the landscape, and deepen hollows into bowl shapes" — which is why it belongs in every corrie answer. The 2024 MC used the phrase "glacial ice moves in a circular motion causing hollows to form in the landscape".

The 2025 paper asked for the term describing "the process where a glacier moves forward and pushes material ahead of it" — bulldozing. The 2023 paper asked, in 1 mark, "give one way glaciers move material" — any of bulldozing, carrying it on the surface, carrying it within the ice, or dragging it at the base will do.

Why north-facing matters. In the northern hemisphere, north-facing slopes receive the least direct insolation, so snow there survives the summer. That is a tiny detail that explains a big one: corries in the Lake District and Snowdonia are overwhelmingly on the north and north-east sides of the mountains, which is why a question asking you to "suggest one reason for the shape of the land" on a north-facing hollow has a ready answer.

Glacial deposition — till and outwash

Spec 3.1.3.4 · AQA List of Key Terms; 2019 Q5.2–5.3; 2022 Q5.5

  • Glaciers deposit when they lose energy — chiefly as they melt, at the snout and during retreat.
  • Till — an unsorted mixture of sand, clay and boulders, dropped directly by the ice as ground moraine.
  • Outwash — sand and gravel deposited by meltwater streams in front of and beneath the glacier.
  • Till is unsorted and angular — ice carries everything together and does not round the fragments.
  • Outwash is sorted and rounded — water sorts by size and rounds material by attrition.
  • That contrast is examined directly — the 2019 paper compared sediment range on a till plain with an outwash plain.

✗ "Glaciers deposit material when they stop." — glaciers deposit mainly as they melt; the ice keeps advancing while the snout retreats.
✓ "As the glacier melts it can no longer carry its load, so material is deposited — directly by the ice as unsorted till, and by meltwater streams as sorted, rounded outwash beyond the snout."

More detail

AQA's definitions: till is "an unsorted mixture of sand, clay and boulders carried by a glacier and deposited as ground moraine over a large area." Outwash is "material, chiefly sand or gravel, deposited by meltwater streams in front of, and underneath, a glacier. The material is sorted and rounded by water action."

The 2019 question is the clearest test of the contrast. The figure showed a till plain and an outwash plain either side of a moraine, with sediment sizes at location A (on the till plain) ranging from 1.4 cm to 26.7 cm — a range of 25.3 cm — and at location B (on the outwash plain) from 3.8 cm to 10.4 cm, a range of 6.6 cm. The explanation for the difference: the till at A was deposited directly by the ice, which carries material of every size together and has no means of sorting it, so the range is wide; the outwash at B was deposited by meltwater, which sorts material by size — dropping the heaviest first and carrying the finest furthest — so at any one point the material is of similar size and the range is narrow.

That same logic answers the 2022 1-marker — "suggest one reason why the material deposited by a glacier is mixed in size and shape": ice carries material on its surface, within itself and at its base simultaneously and, being solid, cannot sort by size or round the fragments by attrition, so everything from clay to boulders is dumped together and stays angular.

Corries, arêtes and pyramidal peaks

Spec 3.1.3.4 · Glacial Landscapes Revision Mat; Y10 feedback model answer; 2023 Q5.5 and 2025 Q5.5 (4 marks each)

  • Corrie defined — an armchair-shaped hollow in the mountainside, formed by glacial erosion, rotational slip and freeze-thaw.
  • It starts as snow in a hollow — on a shady, often north-facing slope where the snow survives the summer.
  • Three processes shape it — freeze-thaw loosens, plucking removes, abrasion deepens.
  • The lip forms because erosion is weaker at the front — thinner, slower ice, plus deposited frost-shattered rock.
  • A tarn is the small circular lake left in the hollow when the ice melts.
  • Arête and pyramidal peak follow — two corries back to back give a knife-edge ridge; three or more give a peak.

Corrie formation — the school's own 4/4 model answer

This has been asked three times in five years. The school's feedback lesson explains the marking rule in one line: "you should link the cause (process) to the effect (a verb) to the result (characteristic of the landform) — e.g. freeze-thaw weathering loosens the rocks and plucking removes them creating a steep back wall." And it notes that at 4 marks you are not strictly required to define each process, though the best answers do; at 6 marks you would be.

"Snow collects in a sheltered hollow, often on a north-facing slope where there is less melting. Over time, the snow is compressed into ice.

Freeze-thaw weathering enlarges the hollow by loosening sediment. Due to pressure, meltwater forms beneath the ice and the glacier begins to move downhill by rotational slip. Meltwater freezes in cracks in the rock, causing pieces of rock to be plucked away and become trapped in the ice. The suspended scree within the ice causes abrasion, which occurs because rocks frozen into the bottom of the glacier scrape against the bedrock, making the hollow deeper. This makes the back wall steeper and creates the armchair shape.

A corrie lip forms at the front because erosion is weaker there as the ice is thinner so moves more slowly. Frost-shattered rock may also be deposited at the front of the corrie, helping to create the lip. After the glacier melts, water often fills the hollow to form a tarn."

The mat's version adds one further detail worth using: "freeze-thaw weathering supports the formation of ice by removing air", and "on the back wall of the corrie, above the ice, freeze-thaw weathering causes the top of the ice to be covered in rock debris. Over time, the back wall retreats backwards, cutting deep into the side of the mountain."

More detail

AQA's definitions: a corrie (also called a cirque) is an "armchair-shaped hollow in the mountainside formed by glacial erosion, rotational slip and freeze-thaw weathering. This is where the valley glacier begins. When the ice melts, it can leave a small circular lake called a tarn." An arête is "a sharp, knife-like ridge formed between two corries cutting back by processes of erosion and freeze thaw." A pyramidal peak is formed "where several corries cut back to meet at a central point, [and] the mountain takes the form of a steep pyramid."

The arête and pyramidal peak sequence follows directly from the corrie one, which is why the 2020 6-marker could ask about all three at once. Two corries developing on adjacent sides of the same mountain each retreat their back wall by freeze-thaw and plucking. As both back walls move towards each other, the rock between them is reduced to a narrow, steep-sided, knife-edge ridge — an arête, such as Striding Edge on Helvellyn. Where three or more corries cut back towards the same point, what is left between them is a steep-sided, sharp-topped pyramidal peak.

Where to point in the Lake District: the corrie is Blea Water; the arête is Striding Edge. The school's own mock set OS map questions on Grisedale and a tarn in grid square 3415, and asked candidates to "suggest one reason for the shape of the land" in a photograph — the mark scheme accepts "it is a tarn", "a corrie formed here", "it represents a hollow enlarged during the ice age", and "erosive processes including plucking and abrasion created the landform".

Glacial troughs, truncated spurs, hanging valleys and ribbon lakes

Spec 3.1.3.4 · AQA List of Key Terms; 2022 Q5.6 (4 marks); 2023 Q5.4; 2024 Q5.2–5.4

  • Glacial trough defined — a river valley widened and deepened by glacial erosion, so it becomes U-shaped not V-shaped.
  • The mechanism — a huge, rigid mass of ice erodes the sides as well as the floor, by abrasion and plucking.
  • Truncated spurs — former river valley spurs sliced off by the glacier, leaving cliff-like edges.
  • Hanging valleys — tributary valleys eroded less, left high above the main floor, often with a waterfall.
  • Ribbon lakes — long, narrow lakes where the glacier had more erosive power, so the floor was over-deepened.
  • Why there — softer rock, a temporarily steeper gradient, or a tributary glacier joining the main valley.

Glacial trough (U-shaped valley) — the sequence for the 4-marker

  1. Before glaciation the valley was a V-shaped river valley with interlocking spurs.
  2. During the ice age, ice accumulated in the corries at the head of the valley and flowed down it as a valley glacier. Unlike a river, the ice filled the whole valley and was rigid, so it could not flow around obstacles.
  3. Abrasion — rock frozen into the base and sides of the glacier acted like sandpaper, scratching and scraping both the floor and the valley sides.
  4. Plucking — meltwater froze around loose rock on the valley sides and floor, and as the ice moved forward it pulled the rock away along its joints.
  5. Freeze-thaw weathering above the ice shattered rock off the valley sides, supplying more abrasive material.
  6. Because the ice eroded laterally as well as vertically, the valley was widened, deepened and straightened into a U-shape with a flat floor and steep sides.
  7. The interlocking spurs that projected into the valley were sliced off, leaving truncated spurs.
  8. Tributary valleys, with far less ice, were eroded much less, so after the ice melted they were left hanging high above the main valley floor, often with a waterfall.
  9. Where the main glacier had more erosive power — softer rock, a steeper gradient, or a tributary glacier joining and adding ice — the floor was over-deepened, and after the ice melted that hollow filled with water to form a ribbon lake.
More detail

AQA's definitions: a glacial trough is "a river valley widened and deepened by the erosive action of glaciers; it becomes 'U'-shaped instead of the normal 'V'-shape of a river valley." A truncated spur is "a former river valley spur which has been sliced off by a valley glacier, forming cliff-like edges." A hanging valley is "a tributary valley to the main glacier, too cold and high up for ice to be able to easily move. It therefore was not eroded as much as the lower main valley, and today is often the site for a waterfall crashing several hundred metres to the main valley floor." A ribbon lake is "a long, narrow lake found in glaciated valleys formed in locations where the glacier had more erosive power, eg in areas of softer rock, where the valley gradient temporarily steepened or a tributary glacier joined the main valley."

Ribbon lakes are the most examined of these, because they are easy to see on an OS map. The 2023 paper gave both a map extract and a photograph of Llyn Ogwen in Snowdonia and asked for two characteristics for 2 marks: it is long and narrow, it lies on the flat floor of a U-shaped valley with steep sides rising on either side, and it is aligned along the direction the valley runs. The 2024 paper asked which grid square showed part of a ribbon lake. Learn what one looks like in plan — a long thin blue shape running along a valley floor, with contours bunched tightly on both sides.

The 2024 2-marker asked candidates to "describe the shape of the valley and measure the width of the valley floor between points X and Y" on a Snowdonia map — so one mark is a description (U-shaped, flat-floored, with steep sides) and one is a measurement in metres, using the scale bar.

Where to point in the Lake District: the U-shaped valley is the Langdale Valley; the ribbon lake is Ullswater.

Moraine — lateral, medial, terminal and ground

Spec 3.1.3.4 · Glacial Landscapes Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms; 2019 Q5.6 (6 marks)

  • Moraine defined — frost-shattered rock debris and material eroded from the valley floor and sides, transported and deposited by glaciers.
  • Lateral moraine — along the sides of the glacier, from freeze-thaw debris falling off the valley walls.
  • Medial moraine — a ridge down the middle, where two glaciers merge and their lateral moraines join.
  • Terminal moraine — a ridge across the valley at the furthest point the snout reached, bulldozed and dumped there.
  • Ground moraine — till spread over the valley floor as the ice melted, giving an uneven hummocky surface.
  • All of it is unsorted till — angular, mixed in size, because ice does not sort or round its load.
More detail

AQA: moraine is "frost-shattered rock debris and material eroded from the valley floor and sides, transported and deposited by glaciers."

The four types, each with the mechanism that produces it:

  • Lateral moraine. Freeze-thaw weathering shatters rock off the valley sides above the ice; the debris falls onto the edges of the glacier and is carried along as a ridge at the margin. When the ice melts, it is left as an embankment along each side of the valley.
  • Medial moraine. Where a tributary glacier joins the main glacier, the two lateral moraines on their adjoining sides meet and continue down the middle of the combined glacier as a single ridge.
  • Terminal moraine. At the snout, the glacier bulldozes material forward by the sheer weight of the ice, and melting releases the load it is carrying. The result is a ridge of unsorted till running across the valley, marking the furthest point the ice reached.
  • Ground moraine. Material carried at the base of the glacier, released as the ice melts, spread across the valley floor as a sheet of till — AQA's definition of till is "deposited as ground moraine over a large area".

The 2019 6-marker gave a diagram of drumlins and a terminal moraine in front of a retreating glacier and asked candidates to "explain the formation of different landforms of glacial deposition" — so the answer needs at least two, with their own sequences, and that is why the moraine types and drumlins should be learned together.

Beyond the terminal moraine lies the outwash plain: meltwater streams emerging from the snout carry sand and gravel out and deposit it in sorted, rounded layers, which is the contrast the 2019 sediment-range question tested.

Drumlins and erratics

Spec 3.1.3.4 · AQA List of Key Terms; 2023 Q5.1; 2025 Q5.2–5.3

  • Drumlin defined — a hill of glacial till, elongated or oval, with its long axis parallel to the former direction of ice flow.
  • The shape — blunt and steep at the stoss (up-ice) end, tapering gently to a lee (down-ice) tail.
  • Why they form — the ice became overloaded with till and deposited it around an obstruction, then moulded it.
  • They occur in swarms — "basket of eggs" topography, which is how you recognise them on a map.
  • Erratic defined — a rock transported and deposited by a glacier some distance from its source region.

More detail

AQA: a drumlin is "a hill made of glacial till deposited by a moving glacier, usually elongated or oval in shape, with the longer axis parallel to the former direction of ice." Erratics are "rocks which have been transported and deposited by a glacier some distance from their source region."

Formation of a drumlin. As the glacier moved forward it became overloaded with till, either because it was melting or because it had picked up more material than it could carry. It deposited some of that till, often around an obstruction such as a resistant outcrop of bedrock. The ice continued to move over the deposit and moulded it, smoothing and streamlining it into the classic shape: steep and blunt on the up-ice (stoss) side where the ice pressed against it, and tapered into a long gentle lee slope on the down-ice side. Because the ice flowed in one direction, every drumlin in an area is aligned the same way, which is why they occur in swarms that look like a "basket of eggs".

The 2025 paper gave a contour map of a drumlin and asked for its length from point A to point B using the scale, and then to describe its shape — which is where the "hill or bump" warning applies. The 2023 paper tested erratic as a 1-mark multiple choice, with moraine, outwash and till as the distractors; the giveaway in the stem was "in areas with a different rock type".

03Understanding it

U1

Why does a glacier produce a U-shaped valley when a river produces a V-shaped one?

Show answer

Because of the shape and rigidity of the eroding agent. A river occupies only a narrow channel at the bottom of its valley, so it erodes downwards; the valley sides are shaped by weathering and mass movement, which gives the V. It also flows around obstacles, which is why it leaves interlocking spurs.

A glacier fills the entire valley and is a rigid solid. Rock frozen into its base and its sides abrades the floor and the walls simultaneously, while plucking pulls material away along the joints. Because it cannot flow around anything, it slices straight through the spurs, leaving truncated spurs. The result is a wide, deep, straight trough with a flat floor and steep sides.

The exam point: "the ice eroded sideways as well as downwards, because it filled the valley and could not bend" is the sentence that explains the U, the truncated spurs and the straightening all at once.

U2

Why does a corrie have a lip at the front?

Show answer

Because erosion is weaker at the front than in the middle. The ice is thickest in the centre of the hollow, where the weight presses hardest and rotational slip carries the greatest load of abrasive debris, so the floor there is over-deepened. At the downhill exit the ice is thinner and therefore moves more slowly, so it erodes much less. In addition, frost-shattered rock from above may be deposited at the front.

So a rock threshold is left standing, and when the ice melts it dams meltwater in the over-deepened hollow behind it to form a tarn.

The exam point: the lip and the tarn are the easiest two extra marks on a corrie question and are routinely omitted. The model answer includes both.

U3

Why are most corries in the UK on north- or east-facing slopes?

Show answer

Because that is where snow survives the summer. In the northern hemisphere, north-facing slopes receive the least direct insolation, so they are shaded and cold; east-facing slopes get only the weaker morning sun and also collect snow blown over the ridge by the prevailing south-westerly wind. On a south-facing slope the snow melts each year, so it never accumulates, never compresses into ice, and no glacier ever forms.

The mat's description starts there deliberately: "Snow accumulates in shady north-facing hollows in upland areas. It becomes compressed forming ice."

The exam point: on an OS map, corries and tarns cluster on one side of a ridge. If a question asks you to "suggest one reason for the shape of the land" or to pick a grid square containing a tarn, aspect is the clue.

U4

Why is till unsorted while outwash is sorted?

Show answer

Because ice and water carry material in completely different ways. Ice is a solid: it carries clay, sand and boulders together, on its surface, inside itself and at its base, and its carrying capacity does not depend on the size of the particle. So when it melts, everything is dumped in one mixture — unsorted, and still angular, because the fragments were locked in place rather than knocking against each other.

Meltwater is a fluid, and its capacity does depend on particle size and velocity. It drops the heaviest material first, close to the snout, and carries the finest furthest, so the deposit is sorted by size along its length. Attrition in transport also rounds the fragments.

The exam point: this is exactly what the 2019 data showed — a range of 25.3 cm on the till plain against 6.6 cm on the outwash plain — and it answers "why is glacial material mixed in size and shape" in one sentence.

U5

Why does freeze-thaw weathering matter so much in a module about glacial erosion?

Show answer

Because it supplies the tools. Abrasion only works if the glacier has rock frozen into it to grind with, and a large part of that rock arrives by freeze-thaw shattering the valley sides and corrie back wall above the ice, from where it falls onto the surface and down crevasses.

It also does shaping work of its own. It is what keeps a corrie back wall steep as the wall retreats into the mountain; it is what keeps an arête knife-edged once two corries have cut back towards each other; and it produces the scree slopes and block-fields you can see in any Lake District photograph. It is also the source of the debris that becomes lateral and then medial moraine.

The exam point: freeze-thaw belongs in almost every landform answer — but call it weathering, never erosion, because the 2019 multiple choice used that exact confusion as a distractor.

U6

Why is land use conflict structural in a glaciated upland rather than just bad luck?

Show answer

Because four economic activities all want the same small area of land and three of them are incompatible with the fourth. Tourism's value depends on the landscape appearing untouched; quarrying removes it; forestry replaces open fell with dense conifer that environmentalists say destroys habitats; farming needs stock left undisturbed by walkers and dogs.

On top of that, the land sits inside a National Park with two purposes that pull against each other — conserving the landscape and supporting the local economy. So every proposal, from a zip wire across the Thirlmere Valley to a wind turbine or a reservoir, is a contest between them. The Honister Slate Mine shows the tension can sometimes be resolved: it is a working quarry that sells climbing and a 1 km zip-wire.

The exam point: say why the uses are incompatible. "They disagree" is not an explanation; "tourism's value is the undisturbed view, which quarrying destroys" is.

U7

Why might managing transport be a more effective strategy than repairing footpaths?

Show answer

Because it addresses a cause rather than a symptom, and because it reaches more problems at once. 89% of visitors arrive by road, so car use drives traffic congestion, air pollution in Ambleside, pressure on narrow roads that farmers need, and the concentration of visitors at honeypot sites with car parks. Park and ride services such as the Honister Rambler, the Park and Sail scheme and dual carriageways bypassing congested towns all reduce that single cause.

Footpath repair, by contrast, treats damage after it has happened. Fix the Fells is repairing 410 miles of path with resistant rock, but — as the school's model answer concedes — "this will take a long time to achieve and is reliant on volunteers. Some visitors will also still not necessarily use these paths."

The exam point: this is a ready-made comparative judgement for the 2025 6-marker, which is exactly what the "discuss" command word wants.

U8

Why can't you answer "the increasing number of visitors can only bring disadvantages" with a list of disadvantages?

Show answer

Because the word being tested is "only". The school's mock mark scheme says there is no correct view and both sides can be credited if supported with evidence, but it also says reference to an example is required for Level 3 and that the answer must "reach a conclusion and substantiate the choice made". D1

Describe the extent of ice cover across the British Isles during the last ice age. (2)

Show answer

Ice covered the whole of Scotland and Ireland, including the Shetland Islands, together with Wales and northern England as far south as roughly the Midlands, and extended across the Irish Sea 1

Southern England was not covered, so the extent decreases from north to south — and the thickest ice lay over the upland areas such as the Scottish Highlands and the Lake District 1

Also creditable: the ice was deeper over Scandinavia, the centre of the main European ice sheet, than over the British Isles.

D2

D3

Explain how a corrie is formed. Use one or more labelled diagrams. (4)

Show answer

Snow collects in a sheltered hollow, often on a north-facing slope where there is less melting, and over time the snow is compressed into ice — freeze-thaw weathering supports this by removing air 1

Freeze-thaw weathering enlarges the hollow by loosening sediment, and because of the pressure of the ice, meltwater forms beneath it so the glacier begins to move downhill by rotational slip, a circular motion that deepens the hollow into a bowl shape 1

Meltwater freezes in cracks in the rock, so pieces of rock are plucked away and become trapped in the ice; that suspended scree then causes abrasion, as rocks frozen into the bottom of the glacier scrape against the bedrock, making the hollow deeper and the back wall steeper and creating the armchair shape 1

A corrie lip forms at the front because erosion is weaker there — the ice is thinner so moves more slowly — and frost-shattered rock may be deposited at the front, helping to create the lip. After the glacier melts, water often fills the hollow to form a tarn, such as Blea Water in the Lake District 1

The marking rule: link cause (process) → effect (verb) → result (characteristic). "Freeze-thaw weathering loosens the rocks and plucking removes them, creating a steep back wall."

D4

Explain the formation of a glacial trough (U-shaped valley). Use one or more diagrams to support your answer. (4)

Show answer

Before glaciation the valley was a V-shaped river valley with interlocking spurs. During the ice age, ice built up in the corries at its head and flowed down it as a valley glacier, filling the whole valley 1

Because the ice was a rigid solid that filled the valley, it eroded the sides as well as the floor. Abrasion — rock frozen into the base and sides of the glacier acting like a giant file — scratched and scraped both the valley floor and the walls 1

Plucking pulled rock away along its joints as meltwater froze around it, and freeze-thaw weathering above the ice shattered more rock off the valley sides, supplying further abrasive material 1

As a result the valley was widened, deepened and straightened into a U-shape with a flat floor and steep sides — the Langdale Valley in the Lake District is an example. Because the rigid ice could not flow around obstacles it sliced off the interlocking spurs, leaving truncated spurs, and tributary valleys with far less ice were eroded much less and were left as hanging valleys high above the main floor 1

D5

Explain why there may be land use conflicts in glaciated upland areas. (4)

Show answer

Glaciated uplands support four main economic activities — tourism, farming, forestry and quarrying — all competing for the same limited area of land, so a use that suits one group damages another 1

Tourism against farming. In the Lake District 18 million tourists a year share the fells with 40,000 residents; walkers with dogs disturb sheep, and because 89% of visitors arrive by road, congestion on narrow lanes obstructs farmers moving stock and machinery 1

Tourism and conservation against development. Tourists and walkers object to wind turbines ruining the landscape, and there were objections to a proposed zip wire across the Thirlmere Valley on the grounds that it would spoil the view and bring extra traffic to narrow roads — because tourism's value depends on the landscape looking undeveloped 1

Conservation against forestry and other development. Environmentalists object to conifer plantations because they destroy habitats; local people object to commercial logging because it can cause localised flooding; constructing dams and reservoirs can cause locals to be relocated; and walkers object to military training 1

Also creditable: second homeowners price out locals, with 20% of houses in the Lake District being second homes.

D6

You have studied a glaciated upland area in the UK. Explain why this area attracts tourists. (4)

Show answer

I studied the Lake District, which attracts 18 million tourists a year compared with 40,000 local people. The main attraction is the glacial scenery itself — corries such as Blea Water, the knife-edge arête of Striding Edge, U-shaped valleys such as Langdale and ribbon lakes such as Ullswater — because the ice left a dramatic landscape of steep fells and deep valleys found almost nowhere else in England 1

Walking is the biggest activity: the steep relief and the network of fell paths mean visitors come to walk and enjoy the beautiful scenery, with honeypot sites concentrating them at the most spectacular viewpoints 1

The lakes provide water-based attractions — boating and sailing on Windermere and the other ribbon lakes, which exist only because glacial erosion over-deepened the valley floors 1

There are also built attractions that use the glacial landscape: the Honister Slate Mine offers climbing and a 1 km zip-wire, so the old quarry is now a visitor attraction in its own right, and the whole area is a National Park, which markets and protects it 1

D7

Explain the formation of different landforms of glacial deposition. (6)

Show answer

Level marked: Basic 1–2, Clear 3–4, Detailed 5–6. "Different" means at least two, each with its own sequence. Plan:

Why glaciers deposit at all. As a glacier melts it can no longer carry its load, so material is released — either directly by the ice as till, an unsorted mixture of sand, clay and boulders, or by meltwater streams as sorted, rounded outwash 1

Terminal moraine. At the snout the glacier bulldozes material of all shapes and sizes forward by the sheer weight of the ice. When the ice melts at its furthest point of advance, this material is dumped as a ridge of unsorted till running right across the valley, marking the maximum extent of the ice 1

Lateral and medial moraine. Freeze-thaw weathering shatters rock off the valley sides above the ice, and the debris falls onto the glacier margins to be carried along as ridges — lateral moraine, left as embankments along the valley sides when the ice melts. Where a tributary glacier joins the main one, two lateral moraines merge and continue down the centre as a medial moraine 1

Ground moraine. Material carried at the base of the glacier, much of it plucked from the bedrock, is released as the ice melts and spread across the valley floor as a sheet of till, giving an uneven, hummocky surface 1

Drumlins. As the glacier moved forward it became overloaded with till and deposited some of it, often around an obstruction such as a resistant outcrop. The ice continued to move over the deposit and moulded it into an elongated or oval hill with a steep, blunt end facing up-ice and a long tapering tail down-ice, aligned parallel to the direction of flow — which is why they occur in swarms, as at Swindale in the Lake District 1

Erratics, and the outwash plain. Erratics are rocks transported and deposited by the glacier far from their source region, so their rock type differs from the local bedrock and records the direction the ice came from. Beyond the terminal moraine, meltwater streams deposit sand and gravel as a sorted, rounded outwash plain — which is why sediment there has a much narrower size range than on a till plain 1

D8

Explain the different ways that glacial material is transported and deposited. (6)

Show answer

Level marked. The 2024 question. Transport first, then deposition, with the source of the material in each case. Plan:

Supraglacial — on the surface. Freeze-thaw weathering on the mountainsides above the glacier shatters rock, which falls onto the ice surface and is carried along. This is the source of lateral and medial moraine 1

Englacial — inside the ice. Material is carried inside the glacier, having fallen down crevasses from the surface, so debris is distributed through the whole thickness of the ice rather than only at its boundaries 1

Subglacial — at the base. Material is carried on the base of the glacier where plucking has occurred: meltwater freezes around loose rock and the forward movement of the ice pulls it out along its joints, so it travels frozen into the sole of the glacier — which is also what makes abrasion possible 1

Bulldozing — pushed in front. The snout of the glacier bulldozes material of all shapes and sizes, moved forward by the sheer weight of the ice 1

Deposition by ice — till. Because the ice carries everything at once and, being solid, cannot sort by size or round the fragments by attrition, melting releases an unsorted, angular mixture of sand, clay and boulders. This is till, deposited as ground moraine over a large area, as terminal moraine at the snout's furthest advance, and moulded into drumlins where the ice moved over it 1

Deposition by meltwater — outwash. Meltwater streams in front of and beneath the glacier deposit sand and gravel, and because water's carrying capacity depends on particle size and velocity it drops the heaviest material first and carries the finest furthest. The outwash is therefore sorted and rounded — which is why the 2019 data showed a sediment range of 25.3 cm on the till plain against just 6.6 cm on the outwash plain 1

D9

Discuss how different strategies are used to manage the impacts of tourism in glaciated areas in the UK. (6)

Show answer

Level marked. The 2025 question. At least two strategies, each with mechanism, figure and limitation, then a comparative judgement. Plan:

The problem, with scale. The Lake District receives 18 million tourists a year against 40,000 local people, so footpath erosion at honeypot sites, boat pollution in Windermere and traffic congestion in Ambleside all follow from the sheer number of visitors 1

Footpath repair. Repairing paths reduces the environmental impact of footpath erosion: when tourists stray from the path they damage the grass, which takes a long time to recover. Fix the Fells is repairing 410 miles of footpath by laying a more permanent pathway made of resistant rocks 1

Its limitation. However, this will take a long time to achieve and relies on volunteers, and some visitors will still not use the paths — so it treats the damage rather than its cause 1

Transport management. The Park and Sail scheme encourages visitors to travel by boat rather than by car, and park and ride services such as the Honister Rambler, together with dual carriageways bypassing congested towns, reduce car use. This matters because 89% of visitors arrive by road, so the schemes reduce traffic jams on narrow roads — improving accessibility for local farmers who use those roads — and reduce the air pollution from idling cars 1

Its limitation, and the lake strategy. However, boat engines still cause water turbulence that can harm aquatic life, and visitors may not be staying near the lakes so still need onward transport. The 10 knot speed limit on Lake Windermere targets that directly, cutting noise, wash and engine pollution, while Friends of the Lake District conserve the environment by restricting planning development 1

Judgement. Managing transport is the more effective approach, because 89% of visitors arrive by road, so it addresses congestion, air pollution and the concentration of visitors at honeypot sites simultaneously — whereas footpath repair deals with damage after the event. Other strategies, such as setting maximum visitor numbers, out-of-bounds signs, seasonal closure and restricting motorbikes and horse riding, work best alongside it rather than instead of it 1

D10

Discuss the possible conflict between development and conservation in glaciated areas. (6)

Show answer

Level marked. The 2023 question, set on the proposed Thirlmere zip wire. Plan:

The structural tension. Glaciated uplands in the UK are National Parks, whose purpose is to conserve the landscape and to support the local economy — so every proposal pits one aim against the other 1

The case for development. The economy depends on it: tourism in the Lake District is worth £2.3bn and 75,000 jobs, forestry £30m, and quarrying at the Honister Slate Mine continues. A new attraction such as a zip wire creates jobs and income in an area with only 40,000 residents, and the Honister mine already runs a 1 km zip-wire alongside climbing, which keeps a working quarry viable 1

The case for conservation. Tourism's own value depends on an undeveloped landscape, so development can destroy the asset it exploits. Objections to the Thirlmere Valley zip wire were exactly that: some people think it will spoil the view, and others are concerned about the extra traffic it will bring to the narrow roads 1

Other conflicts in the same mould. Tourists and walkers object to wind turbines ruining the landscape; environmentalists object to conifer plantations because they destroy habitats; local people object to commercial logging because it can cause localised flooding; constructing dams and reservoirs can cause locals to be relocated; and walkers object to military training 1

How the conflict is managed. Friends of the Lake District restrict planning development to conserve the environment; maximum visitor numbers can be set; signs mark areas out of bounds; seasonal closure lets the environment recover; and activities such as motorbikes and horse riding can be restricted. Fix the Fells and the Park and Sail scheme reduce the damage from the visitors who do come 1

Judgement. The conflict is real but not absolute: Honister shows development and conservation can coexist where the development reuses an existing industrial site rather than an untouched valley. The decisive question is location — which is why Thirlmere was contested and Honister was not, and why planning restriction rather than outright prohibition is the strategy actually used 1

Show answer

Introduction. The Lake District receives 18 million tourists a year against a resident population of 40,000, so the impacts of tourism dominate the area — but they are not all negative 1

Paragraph 1 — environmental disadvantages. Footpath erosion occurs at honeypot sites, where concentrated trampling strips vegetation from steep fell slopes that recover very slowly; boat engines cause oil and noise pollution in Lake Windermere; and because 89% of visitors arrive by road, traffic congestion causes air pollution in Ambleside 1

D12

Stretch — 9-mark shape. Assess the extent to which glacial erosion, rather than glacial deposition, is responsible for the landscape of a UK upland area you have studied. (9)

Show answer

A "to what extent is X rather than Y" question: argue both and then decide. Use the Lake District as the single example throughout. Plan:

Introduction. The Lake District lay beneath the last ice sheet, and the landforms the school identifies there are four of erosion — the arête at Striding Edge, the ribbon lake Ullswater, the corrie at Blea Water, the U-shaped Langdale Valley — against one of deposition, the drumlins in Swindale 1

Paragraph 1 — the case for erosion: the large-scale form. The shape of the whole upland is erosional. Abrasion, where rock frozen into the base of the glacier acts like a giant file, and plucking, where meltwater freezes around rock and the ice pulls it out along its joints, widened and deepened pre-existing V-shaped river valleys into U-shaped troughs, truncating the spurs and leaving tributary valleys hanging 1

Where the ice had most erosive power it over-deepened the floor, and those hollows filled to become the ribbon lakes — Ullswater, Windermere, Thirlmere — that define the region and give it its name 1

Paragraph 2 — the case for erosion: the high ground. On the fells, freeze-thaw weathering, rotational slip, plucking and abrasion cut armchair-shaped corries such as Blea Water into shady north-facing hollows; where two corries' back walls retreated towards one another they left the knife-edge arête of Striding Edge, and where three or more met, a pyramidal peak 1

This is the strongest evidence for erosion, because these landforms are both the most dramatic and the reason 18 million tourists a year visit — the economy rests on erosional scenery 1

Paragraph 3 — the case for deposition. Deposition is not absent, and it dominates where the ice ended rather than where it flowed. Ground moraine blankets valley floors with unsorted till, which is what makes them flat and farmable; terminal moraines mark the furthest advance of the ice and can dam lakes; lateral and medial moraines line the valleys; and the ice moulded till into the drumlin swarm at Swindale 1

Beyond the ice limit, meltwater deposited sorted, rounded outwash, and erratics scattered across the region record where the ice came from. Deposition also explains much of the soil cover, and therefore the farming and forestry economy 1

The interaction. The two are not independent: deposition is only possible because erosion supplied the material. Freeze-thaw and plucking produced the debris that abrasion used and that the ice then dumped as till and moraine — so the depositional landforms are downstream of the erosional ones, in every sense 1

Conclusion that decides. Glacial erosion is responsible to a great extent for the Lake District's landscape: it created the corries, arêtes, troughs and ribbon lakes that give the area its form and its tourist economy, and four of the five landforms the school identifies there are erosional. Deposition is the subordinate but necessary partner — it supplies the flat, soil-covered valley floors and the drumlins, and it exists only because erosion came first 1
