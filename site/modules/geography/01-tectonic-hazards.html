01How it actually works

Two spec areas sit here: natural hazards in general (3.1.1.1) and tectonic hazards (3.1.1.2). The topic has a spine you can see in every paper — why plates move → what happens at each margin → what the hazard does → what people do about it → why they stay anyway. Every question AQA has set since 2018 is a slice of that spine, and the big one at the end of Section A is almost always about contrasting wealth or about responses.

What this tells you to learn hardest. Three things carry nearly every mark: (1) process at the three margins, in the right words — subduction, denser, friction, pressure released; (2) Nepal versus Japan with numbers attached; (3) why people stay, which is now a 6-marker in its own right and needs Iceland's figures. What a natural hazard is, and what makes the risk bigger

Spec 3.1.1.1 · Tectonics Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms

  • Definition — a natural event that threatens people or has the potential to cause damage, destruction and death.
  • No people, no hazard — an earthquake in an empty ocean is an event, not a hazard; the threat to people is what makes it one.
  • Two families — tectonic (earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, tsunamis, tectonic landslides) and atmospheric (tropical storms, droughts, tornadoes, floods).
  • Hazard risk — the probability or chance that a natural hazard takes place.
  • Five risk factors — urbanisation, level of economic development, geographical location, population increase, and rising frequency or magnitude.
  • Wealth is the big one — HICs are better equipped to cope with natural hazards than LICs, which is the hinge of the 9-marker.

✗ "An oil spill is a natural hazard because it damages the environment." — 2022 Q01.1 tested exactly this; the answer was D, oil spill, because it is caused by human activity.
✓ A natural hazard is a natural event — flood, volcanic eruption, earthquake, tropical storm — that threatens people. If humans caused it, it is not one.

More detail

A natural hazard is a natural event (for example an earthquake, volcanic eruption, tropical storm or flood) that threatens people or has the potential to cause damage, destruction and death. That is the AQA wording and it is worth learning to the word, because 2019 opened Section A by asking for a definition.

Factor affecting hazard riskWhy the risk rises
UrbanisationDensely populated areas are at greater risk — more people and more buildings in the same square kilometre, so one event reaches far more of them. Tall buildings concentrate the injuries when they fall.
Level of economic developmentHigher-income countries (HICs) are better equipped to cope with natural hazards than lower-income countries (LICs) — they can afford monitoring, building codes, trained emergency services and reconstruction.
Geographical locationSome places are simply more at risk because of where they are — on a plate margin, in the hurricane belt, on a floodplain.
Population increaseMore people, often pushed onto marginal and dangerous land, means more people exposed to the same event.
Increased frequency or magnitudeIf events become more common or more powerful, the chance of being hit — and hit hard — goes up.

Types of tectonic hazard you can list for a 1-mark or 2-mark question: volcanic eruptions and ash fall, earthquakes, tsunamis, landslides caused by tectonic activity, and avalanches in mountainous areas.

Plate tectonics theory — the evidence, and what moves the plates

Spec 3.1.1.2 "Plate tectonics theory" · Tectonics Revision Mat; AQA GCSE textbook; 2023 examiner's report

  • Two crusts — oceanic is thin (5–8 km) and dense; continental is thick (10–70 km) and less dense. Density decides what subducts.
  • Four pieces of evidence — matching fossils, the jigsaw fit, matching rock formations and mountain chains, matching mineral deposits.
  • Convection currents — heat from the core drives hot rock up through the mantle; it cools at the crust and sinks, dragging plates.
  • Ridge push and slab pull — the modern mechanisms; examiners specifically praised answers that used them.
  • Lithosphere — the rigid crust plus the uppermost mantle, broken into the plates that "float" on the semi-molten rock below.
  • Distribution — 90% of earthquakes and 22 of the 25 biggest eruptions in 12,500 years are on the Pacific Ring of Fire.

✗ "The plates move because of convection currents." left as a bare assertion — the 2023 examiner's report says the best answers "addressed the underlying cause of plate movement, which is a critical part of the process", naming ridge push / slab pull and gravitational sliding.
✓ "Heat from the core drives convection currents in the mantle that drag the plates; at a subduction zone the denser plate also sinks under gravity and pulls the rest of the plate behind it — slab pull."

More detail

The four pieces of evidence for continental drift — the Revision Mat lists these as a straight 3-mark question:

  • The same fossilised plants and animals are found in South America and Africa.
  • The west coast of South America fits the east coast of Africa like a jigsaw puzzle.
  • Rock formations and mountain chains match across South America and Africa.
  • Similar mineral deposits and natural resources such as coal exist along the east coast of Africa and the west coast of South America.

Global distribution. The majority of the world's volcanoes are found around the Pacific Ring of Fire; volcanoes also run along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge; they are typically found along destructive and constructive margins; and some occur at hot spots such as Hawaii, where magma rises in the middle of a plate. Earthquakes follow the same lines, in narrow belts along the margins — the word examiners want when you describe the map is "along" or "in a chain/cluster", not a list of individual volcano names.

Earthquake vocabulary. The focus is the contact point underground where the rock fractures; the epicentre is the point on the surface directly above it, where shaking is strongest. Seismic waves weaken the further they travel from the focus. Magnitude is measured on the Richter scale (1–10), which is logarithmic.

Constructive margins — plates moving apart

Spec 3.1.1.2 "Physical processes at different types of plate margin" · AQA Revision Notes; AQA List of Key Terms

  • Definition — a margin where rising magma adds new material to plates that are diverging, or moving apart.
  • What happens — as the plates separate, magma rises to plug the gap and cools to form new crust.
  • Volcanoes: yes, but gentle — low shield volcanoes, because magma escapes easily so pressure never builds.
  • Earthquakes: yes, but weak — caused by stresses in the crust as magma forces its way to the surface.
  • Named example — the North American plate moving away from the Eurasian plate: the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and Iceland.
  • 2024's 1-marker — "why is new crust formed at Y?" Because the plates diverge and magma rises into the gap and solidifies.

✗ "Earthquakes don't happen at constructive margins." — the 2023 examiner's report lists this as one of the three standard misconceptions.
✓ Earthquakes at constructive margins are relatively weak, but they do occur, caused by the stresses of magma forcing its way up through the crust.

Destructive margins — plates moving together, one going under

Spec 3.1.1.2 · AQA Revision Notes; 2022 mark scheme Q01.9; 2022 and 2023 examiner's reports

  • Definition — two plates converge and the oceanic plate is subducted; associated with violent earthquakes and explosive volcanoes.
  • Why the oceanic plate goes under — because it is denser, not because it is "heavier"; examiners mark the word.
  • Volcanoes — the subducted crust melts in the hot mantle, making magma that rises through the lithosphere and erupts violently.
  • Earthquakes — as the plates converge, friction and pressure build up, the rock fractures, and the pressure is released as an earthquake.
  • Fold mountains — the crumpled crust at the margin is uplifted, e.g. the Andes.
  • Collision margins — two continental plates meet, neither subducts; crust crumples upward into fold mountains like the Himalayas, with earthquakes but no volcanoes.

✗ "The plates collide and that causes an earthquake." — the 2023 report: "Relatively few indicated that it is the release of pressure that caused the earthquake."
✓ "As the plates converge, friction locks them and pressure builds; eventually the rock fractures and that pressure is released as seismic waves — an earthquake."

✗ "The continental plate is subducted." — flagged in both the 2022 and 2023 reports as a misconception.
✓ The denser oceanic plate is always the one subducted beneath the less dense continental plate.

More detail

The 2022 mark scheme for Q01.9 (4 marks, "Explain why earthquakes and volcanic eruptions take place along destructive plate margins") is the cleanest statement of what AQA wants, and it requires both hazards for top Level 2:

  • Destructive plate margins occur when two tectonic plates move towards each other and one is subducted under the other.
  • If an oceanic and a continental plate collide, the denser oceanic plate is subducted and sinks below the continental plate into the Earth's mantle.
  • This causes part of the mantle to melt, and hot magma may rise up through the overlying mantle and lithosphere, eventually erupting at the surface as a volcano.
  • An earthquake occurs because as the plates converge, pressure builds up, the rock may fracture and the pressure is released.
  • Credit is also given for slab pull: the denser plate sinks into the mantle under gravity, pulling the rest of the plate behind it.
  • Collision boundaries are allowed: if two continental plates collide, the pressure and strain may cause an earthquake.

Eruptions here are violent and explosive because the magma is thick and viscous and gas cannot escape easily — the opposite of the runny magma at a constructive margin.

Conservative margins — plates sliding past

Spec 3.1.1.2 · AQA List of Key Terms; AQA Revision Notes

  • Definition — a margin where two plates slide past each other, in the same or opposite directions at different speeds.
  • Earthquakes: yes, and destructive — friction locks the plates, stresses build, then the plates slip and shift suddenly.
  • Volcanoes: never — no plate is destroyed and none is created, so there is no magma source.
  • Named example — the San Andreas Fault in California.
  • Shallow focus — the focus is near the surface, which is why conservative-margin quakes can be badly destructive to buildings.

✗ "Volcanoes occur at conservative plate margins." — listed in the 2023 examiner's report as a misconception that cost marks on the 9-marker.
✓ No crust is destroyed or created at a conservative margin, so there is no magma and therefore no volcanoes — only earthquakes.

Primary and secondary effects

Spec 3.1.1.2 "Primary and secondary effects of a tectonic hazard" · Tectonics Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms

  • Primary — the initial impact on people and property, caused directly by the event, happening immediately.
  • Secondary — indirect after-effects in the hours, days and weeks that follow, sometimes on a much longer timescale.
  • Primary examples — buildings collapsing, people killed or injured, roads and power lines destroyed, water pipes burst.
  • Secondary examples — tsunamis, landslides, fires from ruptured gas mains, disease from contaminated water, unemployment, looting.
  • The test — ask "was this the shaking itself, or something the shaking set off?" Fires are secondary; the collapse that broke the gas main is primary.
  • Three categories AQA uses — social (people's lives), economic (money and jobs) and environmental (landscape and ecology).

✗ Calling the tsunami a primary effect of the Japan earthquake.
✓ The seabed movement is primary; the tsunami it triggered, and the Fukushima radiation leak the tsunami caused, are secondary. 2024 Q01.10 asked for two primary effects for 2 marks — get the category wrong and you score nothing.

Nepal, 25 April 2015 — the LIC

  • Where — landlocked country in South Asia, in the Himalayas, bordering China and India. HDI 142nd of 193.
  • Margin — the Indo-Australian plate colliding with the Eurasian plate: a collision boundary, so no volcano and, being inland, no tsunami.
  • Magnitude 7.9, at 11:56 on Saturday 25 April 2015.
  • Primary — 9,000 killed, 20,000 injured; 8 million affected, a third of the population; 3 million left homeless; 7,000 schools destroyed; 50% of shops destroyed; 1.4 million needed food, water and shelter. Cost $5 billion.
  • Secondary — shaking triggered landslides and avalanches that blocked roads and hampered relief; an avalanche on Everest killed at least 19; 250 left missing in Langtang; a landslide dammed the Kali Gandaki River, forcing evacuations for fear of flooding.
  • Immediate — international search-and-rescue teams from the UK, China and India; half a million tents for the homeless; helicopters rescued climbers and supplied cut-off villages; field hospitals; 300,000 migrated out of Kathmandu; social media and satellites used to map damage.
  • Long-term — 7,000 schools rebuilt; new building regulations and stricter codes; roads repaired and landslides cleared; homeless re-housed; an international reconstruction conference hosted in June 2015; heritage sites and Everest reopened to restart tourism. In late 2015 a blockade at the Indian border cut supplies of fuel, medicine and construction materials.

Japan, March 2011 — the HIC

  • Where — the island of Honshu, city of Sendai. HDI 19th of 187.
  • Margin — a destructive (subduction) margin off the Pacific coast, which is why the seabed movement generated a tsunami.
  • Magnitude 9.0, March 2011 — far larger than Nepal's, and the energy released is what makes the comparison work.
  • Primary — 20,000 killed; 500,000 left homeless. Cost $235 billion.
  • Secondary — a tsunami devastated coastal towns and flooded 560 km²; 100,000 evacuated because of the radiation leak at the Fukushima nuclear power plant.
  • Immediate — 100,000 Japanese Self-Defence Force workers deployed; 500,000 evacuated to high ground; a US$150 billion budget set for the Reconstruction Design Council.
  • Long-term — 30,000 public housing units built; industrial production back at pre-disaster levels by 2020; the tsunami wall upgraded to 12.5 m and the warning system improved.
  • Note — the Case Study Summary Booklet prints "Nazca subducting under South American" in Japan's margin row. That is the Chile margin and is a slip in the booklet; do not write it for Japan.

✗ "Japan coped better because it is rich." — true but unevidenced, and the 2022 report warns that "some included a lot of facts and figures without necessarily being evaluative; others were evaluative but with limited evidence."
✓ "Japan's wealth bought protection before the event: a 12.5 m tsunami wall, a national warning system and 100,000 trained SDF responders. However, a magnitude 9.0 still killed 20,000 and cost $235 billion — more than forty times Nepal's $5 billion — so wealth changed the type of loss, shifting it from lives to money, rather than removing it."

Immediate and long-term responses

Spec 3.1.1.2 "Immediate and long-term responses to a tectonic hazard" · Tectonics Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms

  • Immediate — the reaction as the disaster happens and in the immediate aftermath: days and weeks.
  • What immediate looks like — search and rescue, treating the injured, evacuation, emergency shelter, food and water, restoring power.
  • Long-term — later reactions over months and years: rebuilding and kick-starting the local economy.
  • What long-term looks like — rebuilding homes, schools and infrastructure, new building codes, re-housing, restarting tourism, improved defences.
  • Wealth shapes both — an HIC funds its own response; an LIC depends on international aid, which arrives slower and runs out sooner.
  • The 2019 question — which matters more? The defensible answer is that immediate responses save lives but long-term responses reduce the next disaster.

Why people continue to live in areas at risk

Spec 3.1.1.2 "Reasons why people continue to live in areas at risk" · Tectonics Revision Mat; Case Study Summary Booklet

  • Geothermal energy — volcanic heat is free, constant and carbon-light; Iceland is the example that carries the figures.
  • Fertile soils — volcanic areas have rich soil, ideal for farming, so a population settles and stays.
  • Tourism — volcanoes and geysers draw visitors and create jobs.
  • Minerals — mining for gold, silver, diamonds and other minerals concentrated by volcanic activity.
  • Perception of risk — events are rare in a human lifetime, so people gamble that it will not happen to them.
  • They cannot leave — family, work, land, poverty and the cost of moving tie people to the place; in 2024 this became a 6-mark question.

Iceland — living with earthquakes on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge

Iceland sits on a constructive margin where the North American and Eurasian plates diverge, which is both the risk and the reason to stay.

  • Geothermal — supplies 20% of Iceland's electricity; 92% of homes are heated by it; it saves 37% of CO₂ emissions and $8.2 billion; and it now powers carbon capture and storage.
  • Tourism — 2.3 million tourists in 2024, worth 8.1% of GDP, or $6.2 billion.
  • Micro-organisms — thermophilic ("heat-loving") micro-organisms from the hot springs are used in hi-tech industries.
  • Monitoring makes it liveable — before the 2010 Eyjafjallajökull eruption scientists detected earthquake activity and made accurate predictions, so the risk is managed rather than simply accepted.

✗ "People live there because the soil is fertile and there are jobs." — generic, no place, no figures, Level 1.
✓ "In Iceland, geothermal heat from the constructive margin supplies 20% of electricity and heats 92% of homes, saving $8.2 billion and 37% of CO₂ emissions — this means that the hazard is also the country's cheapest energy supply, so leaving would cost more than staying."

Monitoring and prediction

Spec 3.1.1.2 "How monitoring, prediction, protection and planning can reduce the risks" · Tectonics Revision Mat; AQA Revision Notes; AQA List of Key Terms

  • Monitoring — recording physical changes, such as tremors around a volcano, to help forecast when and where a hazard might strike.
  • Volcano monitoring works — tiltmeters and GPS detect changes in shape, seismographs record tremors, gas and hydrology monitoring detect rising magma.
  • Remote sensing — satellites detect heat and ground deformation; gravity measurements change as magma moves.
  • Prediction for volcanoes is real — based on monitoring: before the 2010 Eyjafjallajökull eruption scientists detected seismic activity and predicted accurately.
  • Prediction for earthquakes is not — there are no reliable warning signs; radon gas and foreshocks help a little, nothing more.
  • What you can do instead — use historical data to predict where, not when, and protect those places.

Protection and planning

Spec 3.1.1.2 · Tectonics Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms; AQA Revision Notes

  • Protection — actions taken before a hazard strikes to reduce its impact, such as improving building design or educating people.
  • Earthquake protection — buildings engineered to withstand shaking: shock absorbers, deep flexible foundations, automatic shutters, cross-bracing.
  • Tsunami protection — sea walls; Japan upgraded its tsunami wall to 12.5 m after 2011.
  • Volcano protection is weak — eruptions are too powerful; earth embankments or explosives can divert lava, and that is about all.
  • Planning — enabling communities to respond and recover: evacuation plans, hazard maps, drills, warning systems, emergency supplies.
  • Hazard maps — show which areas are most susceptible, so governments can plan land use and protect the important buildings.

✗ "Protection means protecting people from the earthquake."
✓ Name the specific strategy and say how it reduces the impact: "Buildings are fitted with rubber shock absorbers in their foundations (1), which absorb the seismic energy so fewer buildings collapse and fewer people are crushed (1)." The 2022 report: "Terms such as 'protection' were often used but did not say what the protection was and how it reduced the impact."

Putting the two case studies side by side — what causes the variation

Spec 3.1.1.2 "Use named examples to show how the effects and responses vary between two areas of contrasting levels of wealth" · Case Study Summary Booklet; 2022 and 2025 mark schemes; the school's full-marks 2022 script

  • Wealth is not the only variable — magnitude, population density, location, time of day and type of margin all matter, and saying so is the evaluation.
  • Wealth buys preparation — building codes, trained emergency services, monitoring equipment, stockpiled supplies.
  • Wealth buys speed — Japan funded its own $150 billion reconstruction; Nepal depended on foreign aid and a border blockade stalled supplies.
  • Deaths versus dollars — Nepal lost 9,000 lives to a 7.9; Japan lost 20,000 to a 9.0 but $235 billion against Nepal's $5 billion.
  • Effects and responses — the 2025 wording asks for both, and the mark scheme caps you at Level 2 if you cover only one.
  • Max Level 2 without named examples — the mark scheme says so explicitly, twice.
More detail

The mark schemes for 2022 Q01.10 and 2025 Q01.12 share nearly identical indicative content. The examiners say: "Tectonic hazards do not discriminate by wealth. However, discussion is likely to focus on how the effects may vary according to how well the country is able to predict, protect against and prepare for a tectonic hazard. This tends to be linked to wealth… There may also be some discussion about how wealthier countries tend to recover more quickly."

The school's own full-marks script (June 2022, 9/9 plus 3/3 SPaG) is built like this, and it is the shape to copy:

  1. Opening judgement in one sentence. "The effects of tectonic hazards massively vary depending on the area affected. This can be due to the population density or the severity of the hazard at that point; however, contrasting wealth has the greatest effect… as it determines the effectiveness of the immediate and long-term responses along with the preparation levels."
  2. The other factors first, with evidence, so the wealth argument has something to beat: population density, tall buildings, location (an offshore quake causes a tsunami), magnitude and aftershocks.
  3. Then wealth, as the strongest factor — contrasted on preparation (plans and protection), immediate response (temporary housing within days versus blocked roads), building design (automatic shutters, rubber shock absorbers, strong foundations), speed of recovery (power restored to 90% in ten days versus heavy reliance on international aid), disposable income allowing households to prepare their own survival kits, and government funding for tiltmeters and seismometers.
  4. A two-line conclusion that decides. "Overall, despite the location of the earthquake along with the population density being very important in determining the effects of a tectonic hazard, the wealth level determines the preparation for the disaster along with the effectiveness of immediate and long-term responses."

The variables worth naming when you explain why two earthquakes differ: building density, construction standards, corruption, how hazard-prone the area is, magnitude, monitoring and prediction, medical facilities, population density, resources and finance, secondary effects such as tsunamis, time of day or year, trained emergency services, transport infrastructure, and the type of plate margin.

02Cheat sheet

The facts you cannot sit the paper without

Three margins: constructive (apart, magma fills the gap, new crust, shield volcanoes, weak quakes — Mid-Atlantic Ridge/Iceland) · destructive (together, denser oceanic plate subducted, melts, explosive volcanoes, violent quakes, fold mountains — Andes) · conservative (sliding past, friction then slip, destructive quakes, no volcanoes — San Andreas). Plus collision: two continental plates, no subduction, fold mountains and quakes, no volcanoes — Himalayas.

Plate movement: convection currents from core heat, plus ridge push and slab pull. Oceanic crust 5–8 km, dense. Continental 10–70 km, less dense. 90% of earthquakes and 22 of the 25 biggest eruptions in 12,500 years are on the Pacific Ring of Fire.

Nepal 2015: 25 April, 11:56, M7.9, collision margin (Indo-Australian/Eurasian), HDI 142/193 · 9,000 dead · 3 million homeless · 8 million affected · $5 bn · 7,000 schools · Everest avalanche 19 dead · half a million tents · 300,000 left Kathmandu · new building regulations.

The 9-mark frame — tectonic hazards

Section A's last question is 9 marks + 3 SPaG, marked in three levels. Level 3 (7–9) needs detailed knowledge illustrated through named examples (AO1), thorough understanding (AO2) and ongoing evaluation (AO3). The mark scheme caps you at Level 2 with no named example. Give it three paragraphs plus a conclusion, and evaluate inside each paragraph rather than saving it all for the end.

  • One-sentence intro that already judges. "The effects of a tectonic hazard vary considerably with wealth; however, magnitude and population density also matter, and it is wealth that determines the responses."
  • Paragraph 1 — the factor you will eventually rank second. "In Nepal, a magnitude 7.9 killed 9,000 people in April 2015. However, in Japan a magnitude 9.0 killed 20,000 — a far larger quake. This suggests that magnitude and population density shape the raw death toll independently of wealth."
  • Paragraph 2 — wealth and the effects. "Japan's $235 billion cost against Nepal's $5 billion reflects what there was to destroy; but 3 million Nepalis were made homeless against 500,000 Japanese, out of a far smaller population. As a result, the human cost fell on the LIC and the financial cost on the HIC."
  • Paragraph 3 — wealth and the responses, which is the decisive one. "Japan deployed 100,000 Self-Defence Force workers and set a $150 billion reconstruction budget within months; Nepal needed search-and-rescue teams from the UK, China and India, and a border blockade later cut its construction materials. Therefore wealth matters most at the response stage, because that is where a country is either self-sufficient or dependent."
  • Two-sentence conclusion that ranks. "Overall, although magnitude and population density determine the immediate scale, wealth determines the preparation and the effectiveness of both the immediate and the long-term responses — which is why Japan's industrial production was back to pre-disaster levels by 2020 while Nepal was still rebuilding."

Sentence starters that earn the AO3 marks: "This suggests that…" · "However, this is less significant than…" · "The most important factor is… because…" · "Whereas in Nepal…" · "This is only true to an extent, because…" · "Overall, the evidence points to…"

03Understanding it

U1

If an earthquake happens in the middle of an uninhabited ocean, is it a natural hazard?

Show answer

No. AQA's definition is "a natural event that threatens people or has the potential to cause damage, destruction and death". The event is natural; the hazard only exists where it meets people and property.

That is why hazard risk is not the same as hazard frequency. Risk depends on urbanisation, population increase and level of development as much as it depends on where the plates are — a magnitude 6 under a dense, poorly built LIC city is far more hazardous than a magnitude 8 under open ocean.

The exam point: this is why "wealth" questions work at all. The geology is indifferent; the hazard is a human outcome.

U2

Why does the oceanic plate always subduct, and why does that single fact explain both the earthquakes and the volcanoes?

Show answer

Oceanic crust is denser than continental crust (and much thinner — 5–8 km against 10–70 km), so when the two converge the oceanic plate sinks into the mantle. The word is denser, not heavier: the 2022 examiner's report names that confusion specifically.

The earthquakes come from the friction of one plate grinding past the other: pressure builds along the locked contact, the rock eventually fractures, and the release of that pressure is the earthquake.

The volcanoes come from the same descent: as the subducted crust reaches the hot mantle it melts, producing magma that is less dense than the rock above, so it rises through the mantle and lithosphere and erupts. Because that magma is thick and gas-rich, the eruptions are violent and explosive.

So one process — subduction — produces both hazards, which is exactly why the question is set as a pair.

U3

Why are there no volcanoes at conservative or collision margins, when there are violent earthquakes at both?

Show answer

Because no crust is being destroyed, so nothing melts and there is no magma supply.

At a conservative margin the plates slide past each other. Friction locks them, stress accumulates, and the sudden slip releases it as an earthquake — the San Andreas Fault. Nothing goes down into the mantle.

At a collision margin, two continental plates meet. Neither is dense enough to subduct, so the crust crumples upward into fold mountains — the Himalayas — and the strain causes earthquakes, as in Nepal in 2015. Again, nothing is subducted and nothing melts.

Why this matters for Nepal: it is a collision margin, which is why Nepal has earthquakes but no volcano, and why an inland quake produced landslides and avalanches rather than a tsunami.

U4

Japan's earthquake was magnitude 9.0 and killed 20,000; Nepal's was 7.9 and killed 9,000. Does that show that wealth does not protect you?

Show answer

No, and spotting why is the AO3 mark. The Richter scale is logarithmic, so a 9.0 releases vastly more energy than a 7.9 — the two events are not comparable in raw force.

Second, most of Japan's deaths were caused by the tsunami, a secondary effect of an offshore destructive margin. Nepal, inland on a collision margin, could not have one. The margin type, not just the wealth, shaped the toll.

Third, look at proportions rather than totals. Nepal had 3 million homeless and 8 million affected — a third of its population. Japan had 500,000 homeless out of 125 million. The LIC lost a far greater share of its people and its capacity.

Use it like this: concede the point, then outrank it. Wealth did not stop a magnitude 9.0 killing 20,000 — but it funded $150 billion of reconstruction within months and had production back to pre-disaster levels by 2020, which Nepal could not do.

U5

Why can volcanic eruptions be predicted but earthquakes cannot?

Show answer

Because magma gives warning as it rises and an earthquake does not.

Volcanoes: rising magma inflates the mountain, which tiltmeters and GPS detect as a change in shape; it cracks rock, which seismographs pick up as tremors; and it releases gases, which gas monitors and hydrology (gases dissolved in water) detect. Satellites detect the heat. So a prediction is built from converging physical signals — as before Eyjafjallajökull in 2010, when scientists detected earthquake activity and predicted the eruptions accurately.

Earthquakes: the pressure builds invisibly in rock and the fracture happens in seconds. Radon gas release and foreshocks exist but are unreliable — foreshocks look exactly like ordinary small quakes until the big one arrives.

The consequence for management: for volcanoes, prediction plus evacuation is the main defence, since the eruption itself cannot be stopped. For earthquakes, you give up on when and invest in where — historical data, hazard maps, building codes and drills.

U6

Why would anyone live in Iceland, which sits directly on a plate margin?

Show answer

Because the plate margin is the economy. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge gives Iceland the geothermal heat that supplies 20% of its electricity and heats 92% of homes, saving 37% of CO₂ emissions and $8.2 billion — and it now powers carbon capture and storage, which turns the hazard into a climate asset.

The same geology brings 2.3 million tourists (2024), worth 8.1% of GDP or $6.2 billion, and supplies thermophilic micro-organisms to hi-tech industry.

And the risk is managed rather than endured: Iceland's constructive margin produces gentle shield-volcano eruptions and weak earthquakes, and eruptions can be predicted and evacuated.

The general version: fertile volcanic soils, mining for minerals, tourism, geothermal energy, plus the human reasons — family, work, land, the cost of leaving, and a perception that rare events will not happen to you.

U7

Are long-term responses really more important than immediate ones, as the 2019 question claims?

Show answer

For immediate responses: they save the lives that are still saveable. Search and rescue, field hospitals and the half a million tents in Nepal all operate in a window measured in days. Nothing done five years later recovers a life lost in week one, and failures here multiply — blocked roads in Nepal meant villages went without aid at exactly the moment it mattered.

For long-term responses: they reduce the next disaster. Nepal's new building regulations and 7,000 rebuilt schools, and Japan's 12.5 m tsunami wall and improved warning system, change the outcome of the following earthquake. They also restore the economy — Japan's production was back to pre-disaster levels by 2020, and Nepal reopened Everest and its heritage sites to bring tourists back.

The judgement worth writing: the two are sequential rather than rival. Immediate responses determine the death toll; long-term responses determine the recovery and the next event's death toll. If forced to rank: immediate responses matter more at the time, long-term responses matter more over a generation — and an LIC like Nepal is most vulnerable in the long term, because that is where the money runs out.

U8

The 2022 and 2025 9-markers both ask about "contrasting wealth". What stops an answer that is full of Nepal and Japan facts from reaching Level 3?

Show answer

Missing AO3. The 2022 examiner's report is blunt: "Some included a lot of facts and figures without necessarily being evaluative; others were evaluative but with limited evidence." You need both, in the same sentences.

Three specific traps the reports name:

  • Using a tropical storm as the example. "Max Level 1 for effects of a non-tectonic hazard." The 2022 report saw this happen because the earlier questions on the paper were about storms.
  • Covering effects but not responses (or vice versa) when the question asks for both — 2025's mark scheme caps that at Level 2.
  • No named example at all — capped at Level 2, stated explicitly in both mark schemes.

What Level 3 sounds like: appraise each factor as you present it. "Population density is important — however, Japan is more densely populated than Nepal and still lost fewer homes proportionally, which suggests construction standards outrank density." That single "however" is the difference between a Level 2 description and a Level 3 evaluation.

04Exam drill

What each command word actually wants

Section A runs from 1-mark multiple choice up to a 9-marker with +3 SPaG, and the parts are marked in completely different ways. The school's exam technique guide and the AQA command-word list give the shapes.

  • 1 mark. Read the instruction. These hide at the bottom of busy pages — almost 20% of students missed 2023's Q01.3 entirely. Shade one circle only; two shaded circles scores zero.
  • 2 marks. "Leave nothing to chance" — point (1) plus development (1). A bare statement rarely suffices. Only one strategy is credited where the question says "one way", so a list of three wastes time.
  • Describe — say what you see. Trends, extremities, data, anomalies. No reasons.
  • Explain — say how or why. Develop every point with "this means that…", "this leads to…", "as a result…", "consequently…". Explanation alone is enough; no judgement needed.
  • Suggest — apply your own understanding to the source; do not lift from it. "Such lifting was not creditworthy" (2023 report).
  • 6 marks. Two or three paragraphs with evidence and explanation. Level-marked, not point-marked. If the command is discuss or evaluate, add a counter-argument and a short judgement; if it is explain, explanation is enough. Keep referring back to the question.
  • 9 marks (+3 SPaG). One-sentence intro, two or three P.E.E.L. paragraphs, one- or two-sentence conclusion that decides. Evaluate inside each paragraph. Named examples are compulsory — Level 2 ceiling without them. Always write something, however short: a genuine attempt still earns SPaG marks.
  • "Using Figure X and your own understanding" — you must refer to the figure ("Figure 5 shows that…") and add material that is not in it.

Twelve questions in the real AQA spread: two short, four 4-markers, four 6-markers and two 9-markers. The 6- and 9-mark answers are written as plans — AQA marks those in levels, not by ticking points, so treat the mark points as a coverage target rather than a tick list.

D2

State three reasons why people continue to live in areas at risk from a tectonic hazard. (3)

Show answer

Geothermal energy — volcanic heat provides cheap, reliable power 1

Fertile volcanic soils, ideal for farming 1

Jobs in the tourism industry — also accept mining for minerals such as gold, silver and diamonds; family and work ties; the cost of moving; a low perception of risk 1

Three distinct, focused points. No explanation needed at 3 marks.

D3

Explain why earthquakes and volcanic eruptions take place along destructive plate margins. (4)

Show answer

At a destructive margin two plates move towards each other, and the denser oceanic plate is subducted beneath the less dense continental plate into the mantle 1

As the plates converge, friction between them means pressure builds up; eventually the rock fractures and that pressure is released as seismic waves, which is the earthquake 1

The subducted oceanic crust melts in the hot mantle, which provides a source of magma 1

This magma is less dense than the rock above, so it rises through the overlying mantle and lithosphere and eventually erupts at the surface as a volcano; because the magma is thick and gas-rich the eruption is violent and explosive 1

Top Level 2 requires both earthquakes and volcanoes. A clear explanation of only one is low Level 2. Slab pull also earns credit: the denser plate sinks under gravity and pulls the rest of the plate behind it.

D4

Explain how monitoring and prediction can reduce the risks from a volcanic eruption. (4)

Show answer

Tiltmeters and GPS detect changes in the shape of the volcano as magma rises and inflates it 1, which means scientists know magma is moving towards the surface before anything is visible 1

Seismographs record the tremors caused by rock cracking as magma forces its way up, and gas monitoring detects sulphur dioxide released from the magma 1; because these signals converge, a prediction can be made — as in Iceland in 2010, when scientists detected earthquake activity beneath the Eyjafjallajökull ice cap and accurately predicted the eruptions later that year, allowing warnings and evacuation so lives were saved 1

D5

Using a named example, explain the difference between the primary and the secondary effects of a tectonic hazard. (4)

Show answer

Primary effects are the initial impacts on people and property caused directly by the event, happening immediately 1 — in Japan in March 2011 the magnitude 9.0 earthquake killed 20,000 people and left 500,000 homeless as buildings collapsed 1

Secondary effects are indirect after-effects that occur in the hours, days and weeks afterwards 1 — the seabed movement triggered a tsunami that flooded 560 km² of coast, and the tsunami in turn caused a radiation leak at the Fukushima nuclear power plant which forced 100,000 people to evacuate 1

Japan is the better example here precisely because the chain is so visible: earthquake → tsunami → reactor failure. Nepal works too — the shaking was primary, the Everest avalanche that killed at least 19 and the landslide that dammed the Kali Gandaki River were secondary.

D6

Explain why earthquakes occur at conservative plate margins but volcanic eruptions do not. (4)

Show answer

At a conservative margin two plates slide past each other, either in opposite directions or in the same direction at different speeds 1

Friction locks the plates together along the fault, so stress builds up in the rock over many years 1

When the plates eventually slip and shift suddenly, that stored stress is released as seismic waves; because the focus is shallow, these earthquakes can be very destructive to buildings — the San Andreas Fault in California is the standard example 1

However, no plate is subducted and no new crust is created, so no rock melts and there is no magma supply; without magma there can be no volcanoes 1

D7

Explain why people continue to live in areas that are at risk from a tectonic hazard. (6)

Show answer

AQA marks this in levels. Two or three developed paragraphs; "explain" means explanation is enough, but a named example lifts it out of the generic.

Economic reasons — energy. Volcanic areas provide geothermal energy, which is cheap, constant and low-carbon 1; in Iceland it supplies 20% of electricity and heats 92% of homes, saving 37% of CO₂ emissions and $8.2 billion a year, so leaving the plate margin would mean abandoning the country's cheapest energy supply 1

Economic reasons — tourism and minerals. The volcanic landscape attracts visitors, creating jobs in hotels, transport and guiding 1; Iceland received 2.3 million tourists in 2024, worth 8.1% of GDP or $6.2 billion, and tectonic areas also allow mining of minerals such as gold, silver and diamonds, and support hi-tech industry using thermophilic micro-organisms from the hot springs 1

Social reasons and the risk calculation. Volcanic areas have rich, fertile soils ideal for farming, so dense farming populations have settled there for generations and their family, land and work are all in that place 1; in addition, major events are rare within one lifetime, so people perceive the risk as low, and many — particularly in LICs — simply cannot afford to move, while monitoring and prediction make the risk feel manageable, as when Iceland predicted the 2010 Eyjafjallajökull eruption in advance 1

D8

Explain how protection and planning can reduce the risks from an earthquake. (6)

Show answer

Level-marked. Name the strategy, then say how it reduces the impact — the 2022 report says vague uses of the word "protection" earned nothing.

Protection — building design. Buildings can be constructed to withstand shaking, using deep flexible foundations, cross-bracing and rubber shock absorbers 1; these absorb seismic energy rather than transmitting it through the frame, so the building sways instead of collapsing and far fewer people are crushed — the primary cause of death in an earthquake 1

Protection — defending against secondary effects. Sea walls protect important coastal buildings from tsunamis 1; after 2011 Japan upgraded its tsunami wall to 12.5 m, which means a comparable surge would flood far less than the 560 km² inundated in 2011 1

Planning. Governments stockpile emergency aid supplies and run earthquake drills, training people to take cover and to turn off their gas and electricity supplies 1; this reduces secondary effects such as fires from ruptured gas mains, and hazard maps based on historical data show which areas are most susceptible, so land-use planning keeps critical buildings away from the worst ground and evacuation routes are known in advance 1

Do not write about prediction here. Earthquakes cannot be reliably predicted — that is precisely why protection and planning carry the load.

D9

Suggest why the immediate responses to the Nepal earthquake were less effective than those to the Japan earthquake. (6)

Show answer

Level-marked. "Suggest" means apply understanding — give reasons, each tied to evidence.

Who did the responding. Japan deployed 100,000 of its own Self-Defence Force workers and evacuated 500,000 people to high ground, because as an HIC ranked 19th of 187 on HDI it had trained, equipped, standing emergency services 1; Nepal, ranked 142nd of 193, depended on international search-and-rescue teams arriving from the UK, China and India, which took days to mobilise and arrive — so the critical first 72 hours were largely unsupported 1

Getting to the people. Nepal's secondary effects obstructed its own response: landslides and avalanches blocked roads, cutting off remote Himalayan villages for days, and helicopters were the only way in 1; Japan's road network was damaged but its transport infrastructure was denser and better built, so aid and personnel could move 1

Money and capacity. Japan set a US$150 billion reconstruction budget almost immediately and could fund shelter at scale 1; Nepal relied on donated tents — half a million of them — and 300,000 people simply left Kathmandu to find shelter with relatives, which is a sign that the official response could not house them 1

D10

Suggest why the secondary effects of some tectonic hazards are more severe than the primary effects. (6)

Show answer

Level-marked. Two or three reasons, each exemplified.

A secondary hazard can be bigger than the first one. An offshore earthquake at a destructive margin displaces the seabed and generates a tsunami 1; in Japan in 2011 the tsunami, not the shaking, devastated the coastal towns and flooded 560 km², and most of the 20,000 deaths came from the water rather than from collapsing buildings 1

Secondary effects cascade. One secondary effect triggers the next: the Japanese tsunami caused the radiation leak at Fukushima, which forced the evacuation of 100,000 people and made an area uninhabitable long after the shaking stopped 1; similarly, contaminated water supplies lead to disease, and destroyed businesses lead to unemployment, so the harm keeps compounding 1

Secondary effects obstruct the response. In Nepal, landslides and avalanches blocked the roads, which meant aid could not reach cut-off villages and people who survived the shaking died waiting 1; terrain and wealth both matter here — an LIC with mountainous terrain and few helicopters suffers far longer from the same secondary effect than an HIC would 1

Written as a plan. AQA marks this in three levels. Level 3 (7–9) needs detailed named-example knowledge, thorough understanding of how wealth changes things, and evaluation running through the answer.
