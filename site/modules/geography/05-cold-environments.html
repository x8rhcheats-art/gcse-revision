01How it actually works

Spec 3.1.2.4 is one of two options — hot deserts or cold environments — and the class did cold environments. It runs: physical characteristics; the interdependence of climate, permafrost, soils, plants, animals and people; how plants and animals adapt; issues related to biodiversity; a case study of a cold environment showing development opportunities (mineral extraction, energy, fishing, tourism) and challenges (extreme temperature, inaccessibility, provision of buildings and infrastructure); the value of cold environments as wilderness areas and why these fragile environments should be protected; and the strategies used to balance economic development and conservation — technology, governments, international agreements and conservation groups.

What a cold environment is — polar and tundra compared

Spec 3.1.2.4 · Cold Environments Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms

  • Cold environment defined — an area that experiences very low temperatures for long periods of time.
  • Two types — polar and tundra; the spec names both, and the comparison is a standard question.
  • Polar is the extreme — temperatures well below freezing all year and permanent darkness in winter. Antarctica is the example.
  • Tundra is less harsh — cold winters but conditions that let life thrive, particularly in summer.
  • Tundra is treeless — AQA's wording: flat, treeless Arctic regions where the ground is permanently frozen; lichen, moss, grasses and dwarf shrubs grow.

✗ "Cold environments are places that are cold." — no mark; and "polar and tundra are both freezing" throws away the compare-and-contrast structure.
✓ "Both polar and tundra environments experience cold temperatures. However, polar environments experience extreme cold and permanent darkness during winter months, whereas tundra environments experience cold winters, though conditions are less harsh, enabling life to thrive, particularly in summer."

More detail

Cold environments are areas that experience very low temperatures for long periods of time. The most extreme cold environments experience temperatures well below freezing throughout the year — an example of an extreme cold environment is Antarctica.

The school's exam technique guide is specific about compare-and-contrast questions: include at least two factors, compare means similarities and differences, contrast means differences, and use connectives such as "whereas". That is exactly the shape of the polar/tundra answer.

AQA's own definitions, worth having word for word: polar — "the regions of Earth surrounding the North and South Poles. These regions are dominated by Earth's polar ice caps, the northern resting on the Arctic Ocean and the southern on the continent of Antarctica." Tundra — "the flat, treeless Arctic regions of Europe, Asia and North America, where the ground is permanently frozen. Lichen, moss, grasses and dwarf shrubs can grow here."

Distribution of cold environments

Spec 3.1.2.4 · Cold Environments Revision Mat

  • Highest latitudes — cold environments are found at the highest latitudes, north and south.
  • Northern hemisphere — the Arctic is polar; tundra covers the countries bordering it.
  • Named countries — Canada, the USA (Alaska), Russia and Northern Europe.
  • Southern hemisphere — Antarctica is a polar environment, the one great continental ice sheet.
  • Altitude matters too — alpine cold environments occur at high altitude even at lower latitudes.
More detail

From the mat: "Cold environments are found at the highest latitudes. In the northern hemisphere cold environments include the Arctic (polar) and countries bordering the polar region including Canada, USA (Alaska), Russia and Northern Europe. In the southern hemisphere Antarctica is a polar environment."

When a question gives a world map and asks you to describe the distribution, use the technique from the exam guide: trend first — the distribution is latitudinal, concentrated above roughly 60–66°N and around Antarctica in the south; then named places — Alaska, northern Canada, Greenland, northern Scandinavia and northern Russia in the north, Antarctica in the south; then the contrast — the northern cold environments form a broad belt across several continents, whereas the southern one is a single continent surrounded by ocean.

Svalbard's position is the detail to have for the case study: a Norwegian territory in the Arctic Ocean, around 400 miles north of mainland Norway, with five major islands — and its capital is Longyearbyen.

Climate — polar and tundra

Spec 3.1.2.4 · Cold Environments Revision Mat; 2025 Paper 1 Q2.7–2.9

  • Polar temperatures — typically below 0°C throughout the year, and in winter can fall below −50°C.
  • Polar precipitation is low — snow, and little of it; cold air holds very little moisture, so polar regions are technically deserts.
  • Polar winters are dark — permanently dark, with no sunshine throughout the winter.
  • Tundra has a large annual range — that large range is the single best distinguishing feature on a climate graph.
  • Tundra precipitation is higher — especially in summer; winter months are near or in total darkness.
  • Latitude is the cause — at high latitudes solar energy strikes at a low angle, so it is spread over a larger area.

✗ "It's colder because it's further from the sun." — the same error the rainforest mark scheme rejects, in reverse.
✓ "At high latitudes the sun's rays strike the surface at a low angle, so the same energy is spread over a larger surface area and is less concentrated; more of it is also reflected, by the atmosphere it passes through and by the snow and ice, which has a high albedo."

More detail

Polar, in the mat's words. "Temperatures typically remain below 0°C throughout the year. Temperatures in winter can fall below −50°C. Precipitation (snow) is low. Winters are permanently dark with no sunshine throughout the winter."

Tundra, in the mat's words. "There is a large annual temperature range. Precipitation is higher, especially in the summer. Winter months experience near or total darkness."

The 2025 paper set this as a data exercise: two climate graphs, Cairo (a hot desert) and Aklavik (a cold environment in the Canadian Arctic), and three questions — read the wettest month off Aklavik's graph, pick the correct comparison statement, then suggest one reason for the difference in temperature for 2 marks. That last one is point plus development: Aklavik lies at a much higher latitude than Cairo (1), so the sun's rays arrive at a lower angle and are spread over a larger area, and in winter there is little or no insolation at all, which gives far lower temperatures and a much larger annual range (1).

For Svalbard specifically: a polar climate with over 60% of the land covered by glaciers, the remainder tundra. Winter temperatures drop to −30°C — the school's model answer quotes lows as extreme as −45°C and polar nights lasting several months — while in summer there is 24 hours of sunlight.

Permafrost

Spec 3.1.2.4 · Cold Environments Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms

  • Permafrost defined — permanently frozen ground, found in polar and tundra regions.
  • Hard and impermeable — it prevents soil formation and acts as a barrier to water transfer.
  • That is why the tundra is waterlogged — summer meltwater cannot drain downwards, so it ponds on the surface.
  • The active layer — the top layer that freezes in winter and thaws in spring and summer.
  • It is the key engineering problem — heat from a building or a pipeline melts it, and the ground then subsides.
  • Melting permafrost is a climate risk — it destabilises infrastructure and is one of Svalbard's named challenges.
More detail

Permafrost is hard, permanently frozen ground. It is hard and impermeable, preventing soil formation and acting as a barrier to water transfer. AQA's shorter definition is simply "permanently frozen ground, found in polar and tundra regions."

Permafrost is doing more work in this topic than its one-line definition suggests, and showing that is how you reach Level 3:

  • It creates the wetland. Because it is impermeable, summer meltwater from the active layer cannot drain away, so the tundra becomes waterlogged — which is why mosses that cope with waterlogging thrive and why musk oxen need wide hooves.
  • It limits the soil. Soil formation needs weathered material, water movement and biological activity; frozen impermeable ground below prevents all three, so tundra soils are very thin.
  • It governs construction. The school's model answer puts the Alaskan case exactly: the oil is hot in the pipes to help it flow, so it "could easily melt the permafrost (a vital part in the arctic ecosystem as it keeps the soil watertight to allow for lakes and wetlands to form)", and so the Trans-Alaskan Pipeline was raised and insulated.
  • It is melting. Melting permafrost and rising sea level are two of the named challenges for Svalbard, where 250 homes were destroyed and £18m was needed for emergency housing.

Tundra soils

Spec 3.1.2.4 · Cold Environments Revision Mat; 2024 Paper 1 Q2.8

  • Very thin — there is little weathered material and almost no biological activity to build depth.
  • Not particularly fertile — decomposition is extremely slow in the cold, so nutrients are released only slowly.
  • Waterlogged in summer, frozen in winter — the permafrost beneath stops any drainage.
  • Depth and fertility increase away from the poles — because the growing season and the active layer both lengthen.
  • The active layer is the usable soil — only the top layer thaws, so roots have to work within it.
More detail

Straight from the mat: "Soils are very thin. Soil is not particularly fertile. The soil is waterlogged in the summer and frozen in the winter. Soil depth and fertility increase with distance from the poles. The top layer of soil freezes during the winter and thaws during spring/summer. This is the active layer."

The 2024 paper asked, from a photograph, for one way the soil can be affected by the climate in 1 mark. For a cold environment the answer is any one link: the low temperatures freeze the ground, so soil formation is prevented and the soil stays very thin; or the impermeable permafrost beneath prevents drainage, so the soil becomes waterlogged in summer; or the cold slows decomposition, so nutrients are released very slowly and the soil is infertile.

The contrast with the rainforest is worth holding in your head, because the papers set the two side by side. Rainforest: fast nutrient cycle, tiny soil store, fertility in the biomass. Tundra: almost no nutrient cycle, thin soil, very little biomass. Both have poor soils, for opposite reasons — too fast in one case, too slow in the other.

How plants adapt to the physical conditions

Spec 3.1.2.4 · Cold Environments Revision Mat; 2019 Q2.9 (6 marks); 2024 Q2.9 (9 marks)

  • Shallow roots — to access nutrients and water close to the surface, within the active layer above the permafrost.
  • Low-growing cushion plants — retain moisture and provide shelter from the drying wind.
  • Mosses — cope with waterlogged conditions in summer and survive the winter drought when water is frozen.
  • Rapid life cycle — flowering plants seed quickly during the very short summers.
  • Dwarf shrubs — lichen, moss, grasses and dwarf shrubs are what AQA says grows on tundra; bearberry is the named red-berry plant.
  • Every adaptation answers a named hazard — permafrost, wind, waterlogging, winter drought, or the short growing season.

✗ "Plants are small so they don't get cold." — the development mark is missing: why does being small help?
✓ "Plants are low-growing cushion plants, which means they sit below the worst of the wind, so they retain moisture and are sheltered from the drying wind that would otherwise desiccate them while liquid water is unavailable."

More detail

From the mat, verbatim: "Shallow roots to access nutrients and water close to the surface within the active layer." "Low-growing cushion plants retain moisture and provide shelter from the drying wind." "Mosses can cope with waterlogged conditions in the summer and are able to survive the winter drought." "Flowering plants have a rapid life cycle, enabling them to quickly seed during the short summers."

Two points that turn these into explanations rather than statements:

  • "Winter drought" is a real thing and a good phrase to use. There is plenty of water in a tundra winter, but all of it is frozen, so it is biologically unavailable — plants face drought conditions at −30°C. That is why mosses, which can dry out and rehydrate, do well.
  • The growing season is the binding constraint. The active layer only thaws for a few weeks, and only then is there liquid water and enough light; so the rapid life cycle of the flowering plants is not an optional advantage but the only viable strategy.

The school's quick-fire list names bearberry as the red berry plant adapted to survive in the cold — a cheap named example that shows this is not generic knowledge.

How animals adapt to the physical conditions

Spec 3.1.2.4 · Cold Environments Revision Mat; 2024 Q2.9 (9 marks)

  • Arctic fox and Arctic hare — thick fur on their bodies and feet to keep them warm.
  • Seasonal camouflage — in winter their fur becomes white, providing camouflage from predators.
  • Generalist diet — Arctic foxes can eat a variety of foods, which helps when food is scarce.
  • Musk oxen — two layers of fur to insulate them, and wide hooves to walk on waterlogged ground and snow.
  • Polar bears are Svalbard's headline — there are more polar bears than people on the islands.
  • Name the animal, name the feature, name what it solves — three steps, one mark each at 6-mark level.
More detail

From the mat, verbatim. Arctic foxes and Arctic hares: "have thick fur on their bodies and feet to keep them warm"; "in winter their fur becomes white providing camouflage from predators"; and "Arctic foxes can eat a variety of foods which helps when food is scarce." Musk oxen: "have two layers of fur to help insulate them"; "have wide hooves so they can walk on waterlogged ground and snow."

The wide hooves point is the best one in the list, because it links two spec ideas at once: the ground is waterlogged in summer because the impermeable permafrost prevents drainage, so an adaptation to waterlogged ground is really an adaptation to permafrost. Examiners reward that kind of linkage.

The 2024 9-marker — "'Plants and animals need to have special adaptations to cope with extreme environments.' Discuss this statement" — looks like a knowledge question but is an evaluation. The discussion is: adaptations are necessary because the conditions are genuinely lethal without them (−30°C and below, frozen water, almost no nutrients, a growing season of weeks, and permanent winter darkness); but they are not sufficient, because species also rely on behaviour such as migration and hibernation, and on the whole fragile ecosystem staying intact — which is why these environments recover so slowly from damage and why specialised species are the first to be lost. AQA's own wording on fragile environments makes that point for you: "plant communities in fragile areas have evolved in highly specialised ways to deal with challenging conditions. As a result, they cannot tolerate environmental changes."

Interdependence of climate, permafrost, soils, plants, animals and people

Spec 3.1.2.4 · Cold Environments Revision Booklet, "Interdependence"

  • Climate creates permafrost — sustained sub-zero temperatures freeze the ground permanently.
  • Permafrost creates the soil and the wetland — impermeable ground blocks drainage and prevents soil formation.
  • Soil limits the plants — thin, infertile and only seasonally thawed, so only shallow-rooted specialists survive.
  • Plants limit the animals — low productivity means short food chains and small populations.
  • People depend on the animals — indigenous cultures in the tundra depend on the natural world.
  • So one change propagates — warming melts permafrost, which changes drainage, vegetation, grazing and livelihoods.
More detail

This is the spec point candidates most often skip, and it is the easiest route to a developed 6-mark answer, because it is a single causal chain you can walk down:

Climate → permafrost. Temperatures below 0°C for most or all of the year freeze the ground permanently; only a thin active layer thaws in summer.

Permafrost → soils and water. The permafrost is hard and impermeable, so it prevents soil formation and acts as a barrier to water transfer. Meltwater cannot drain, so the surface is waterlogged in summer and frozen in winter, and the soils stay very thin and infertile.

Soils and climate → plants. Only plants that can root inside the active layer, tolerate waterlogging then winter drought, shelter from the drying wind and complete a life cycle in a few weeks survive — shallow roots, cushion plants, mosses, rapid-seeding flowering plants, dwarf shrubs and lichen.

Plants → animals. Low plant productivity means little biomass at the base of the food chain, so food chains are short, populations are small and the species present are highly specialised — which is also why the loss of one species matters so much more here than in a rainforest.

Animals → people. "Indigenous people occupy some areas of tundra, and their culture depends on the natural world" — hunting, herding and fishing, so a change to the wildlife is a change to a culture.

And back again. Human activity closes the loop: burning coal for Svalbard's energy "contributes to global warming", warming melts permafrost, melting permafrost destabilises the ground the settlements and pipelines stand on.

Biodiversity and fragility

Spec 3.1.2.4 · AQA List of Key Terms; Cold Environments Revision Mat

  • Biodiversity is low but specialised — few species, each very finely adapted to the conditions.
  • Fragile environment defined — easily disturbed and difficult to restore if disturbed.
  • Specialisation is the weakness — highly specialised communities cannot tolerate environmental change.
  • Recovery is slow — growth rates are tiny, so damage lasts decades; tyre tracks in tundra persist for years.
  • Short food chains amplify loss — remove one species and there are few alternatives to absorb the gap.
  • Svalbard's biodiversity is an asset — 150 species of fish in the Barents Sea; polar bears draw the tourists.
More detail

AQA's definition of a fragile environment is the single most quotable sentence in this module: "an environment that is both easily disturbed and difficult to restore if disturbed. Plant communities in fragile areas have evolved in highly specialised ways to deal with challenging conditions. As a result, they cannot tolerate environmental changes."

The school's model answer opens its 9-marker with the same idea in student language: "Cold Environments such as the Arctic and Antarctic are considered fragile environments because the flora and fauna take a long time to recover from any damage done by human activities."

Why fragility follows from the physical geography, which is the understanding mark: the growing season is a few weeks long and temperatures are low, so biological growth and decomposition are extremely slow; there is very little biomass, so there is little buffer; the soil is thin, so once vegetation is stripped the ground does not revegetate; and the species present are specialists with nowhere warmer to retreat to. None of that is true of a temperate ecosystem, which is why the same disturbance does far more lasting damage here.

Development opportunities — mineral extraction, energy, fishing, tourism

Spec 3.1.2.4 · Case Study Summary Booklet 2026, section 7; 2022 and 2023 Paper 1 9-markers

  • Four spec-named opportunities — mineral extraction, energy, fishing and tourism. Learn them as the four paragraphs.
  • Mineral extraction — 300 people work in mining on Svalbard; coal is the resource.
  • Energy — most of Svalbard's energy is produced by burning coal; summer brings 24 hours of sunlight.
  • Fishing — 150 different species of fish in the Barents Sea, one of the most productive fishing grounds in the world.
  • Tourism — 150 000 tourists a year, double 2011, including 45 000 cruise ship passengers, providing 400 jobs.
  • Each one carries its own challenge — damage to landscape, global warming, overfishing, remoteness and seasonality.

Svalbard — a cold environment case study

John's class's named case study. Background first, then the four opportunities with their figures and the challenge attached to each — that pairing is what the 2022 "challenges and opportunities" 9-marker wants.

Background. Svalbard is a Norwegian territory in the Arctic Ocean, around 400 miles north of mainland Norway. It has a polar climate, with over 60% of the land covered by glaciers and the remaining land tundra. There are five major islands, 2700 inhabitants and more polar bears than people. The capital is Longyearbyen.

  • Mineral extraction. 300 people work in mining — coal, historically the backbone of the economy, with Mine 7 near Longyearbyen still operating. Challenge: damage to the landscape.
  • Energy. Most energy is produced by burning coal. Summer brings 24 hours of sunlight. Challenge: it contributes to global warming — the same warming that is melting Svalbard's permafrost.
  • Fishing. 150 different species of fish in the Barents Sea, including commercially valuable cod and haddock, in one of the world's most productive fishing grounds; unlike tourism it is not stopped by the polar night. Challenge: overfishing — Norway cooperates with Russia on quotas.
  • Tourism. 150 000 tourists per year, double the 2011 figure, of whom 45 000 are cruise ship passengers. Tourism provides 400 jobs for local people. The draw is the polar landscape, the polar bears, the Northern Lights and the midnight sun. Challenge: Svalbard is remote and difficult to access — there is one international airport but few flights — and in winter temperatures drop to −30°C, so tourism happens only in summer and most tourism jobs are seasonal.

Challenges of development, as the spec names them. Extreme temperature: −30°C in winter (the school's model answer quotes lows to −45°C and polar nights of several months); melting permafrost; rising sea level. Construction and services: 250 homes destroyed and £18m needed for emergency housing; a threat to drinking water in two reservoirs; the potential to flood the airport; there are few hotels and attractions other than the natural landscape, and specialised clothing is required. Accessibility: only 50 km of road on the whole territory.

✗ "There are lots of opportunities in Svalbard like mining and tourism and fishing." — three nouns, no numbers, no explanation: Level 1.
✓ "Tourism has grown rapidly: 150 000 tourists now visit each year, double the 2011 figure, including 45 000 cruise passengers, and it supports 400 jobs for a population of just 2700 — so tourism is now a larger employer than mining's 300 workers, which is why it is the fastest-growing opportunity."

More detail

The school's Y10 model answer is the best worked example of how to use these figures, and it is worth reading for the evaluative moves rather than the facts:

  • It names and locates the place in the first sentence — "a Norwegian archipelago located in the Arctic Circle, around 400 miles north of mainland Norway" — then states the climate constraint immediately.
  • For each opportunity it gives the figure, then the mechanism, then a weakness. On tourism: "tourism is largely seasonal. During the polar winter, temperatures plummet and darkness makes the islands inaccessible to most visitors, meaning many tourism jobs are temporary and the economic benefit is unevenly spread across the year."
  • It explicitly ranks: "Unlike tourism, fishing is not significantly restricted by the polar night, making it arguably a more reliable contributor to economic development."
  • Its conclusion decides and explains why: "tourism is the fastest-growing and most visible opportunity, but its seasonal nature means fishing offers greater economic reliability. Mining remains important for energy security but is declining."

Two further details from that answer worth keeping: the multiplier effect — money spent in hotels, restaurants and shops circulates through the local economy — and the direction of travel on energy, with the Norwegian government looking to phase out coal in favour of renewables such as geothermal power.

The booklet gives 400 jobs from tourism; the model answer says tourism "directly employs around 300 local people". If you need one number, use the Case Study Summary Booklet's 400, since that is the school's revision document — and either will be credited.

Challenges of developing cold environments

Spec 3.1.2.4 · Case Study Summary Booklet 2026; 2025 Q2.10; Living World Mock Q1.9

  • Three spec-named challenges — extreme temperature, inaccessibility, and the provision of buildings and infrastructure.
  • Extreme temperature — −30°C winters, specialised clothing, and a tourist season confined to summer.
  • Inaccessibility — one international airport with few flights, and only 50 km of road on Svalbard.
  • Buildings and infrastructure — melting permafrost and rising sea level: 250 homes destroyed, £18m for emergency housing.
  • Services are thin — few hotels or attractions beyond the landscape; drinking water in two reservoirs under threat.
  • And a labour problem — a population of 2700 cannot supply a large workforce, so workers must be attracted in.
More detail

The 2025 9-marker was "To what extent does the physical environment create challenges for development in cold environments?" — note the wording. That question is inviting you to separate physical causes from others, and the best answers do exactly that:

  • Physical challenges. Extreme temperature (−30°C, and permanent winter darkness) limits when work can happen and requires specialised clothing and heated buildings. Permafrost makes construction difficult and, as it melts, unstable. Sea ice, storms and the polar night make sea and air access unreliable. Remoteness is itself physical: Svalbard is 400 miles north of mainland Norway, with only 50 km of road.
  • Non-physical challenges. A population of only 2700 means a very small workforce. Infrastructure is expensive because everything must be imported. International agreements restrict what can be done — the Antarctic Treaty keeps economic development in Antarctica to a minimum. Environmental regulation and conservation groups constrain oil, gas and fishing. And world prices decide whether extraction is worth it at all: coal is declining because it is being phased out, not because it has run out.
  • The judgement. The physical environment creates most of the direct challenges, and it also creates the costs that make the others bite — but it does not by itself decide whether development happens, since technology has overcome several physical barriers (the Trans-Alaskan Pipeline is the proof) while political and economic decisions are what actually halt projects.

The school's Living World mock set the mirror-image question — "to what extent have challenges for economic activity been overcome?" — and its mark scheme lists the determinants: "the availability of water, extremes of temperature, available workforce (population and migration), access and transport and the value of resources along with the provision of buildings and infrastructure. As the polar/tundra ecosystem is fragile, development may not be sustainable." It also warns twice: an answer that does not consider the extent is limited to Level 2, and so is a purely generic answer without clear exemplification.

The value of cold environments as wilderness areas

Spec 3.1.2.4 · Cold Environments Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms; 2019 Q2.10

  • Wilderness area defined — a natural environment not significantly modified by human activity; the most intact places left on Earth.
  • Fragility — they are fragile environments and take a long time to recover from damage.
  • Science — to study change such as climate change, scientists need unspoilt environments to work in.
  • Culture — indigenous people occupy some areas of tundra, and their culture depends on the natural world.
  • Habitat — cold environments provide habitats for a range of species of animals and birds.
  • Ethics — there is a moral responsibility to retain wilderness areas to protect their biodiversity.
More detail

AQA's definition: a wilderness area is "a natural environment that has not been significantly modified by human activity. Wilderness areas are the most intact, undisturbed areas left on Earth — places that humans do not control and have not developed."

The mat gives five reasons they should be protected, and they are worth learning as a list because "give two reasons why wilderness areas should be protected" is a standard short question:

  1. They are fragile environments and take a long time to recover from damage.
  2. In order to study change, such as climate change, scientists need to conduct research on unspoilt environments.
  3. Indigenous people occupy some areas of tundra, and their culture depends on the natural world.
  4. Cold environments provide habitats for a range of species of animals and birds.
  5. There is a moral responsibility to retain wilderness areas to protect their biodiversity.

The scientific argument is the strongest one for an evaluation, because it is the only one that explains why unspoilt matters rather than just protected: Antarctica is, as the school's model answer puts it, "untouched, making it the perfect laboratory", so any modification destroys the baseline against which change is measured. The Antarctic Treaty "has recognised the importance of Antarctica for scientific research".

Strategies to balance development and conservation

Spec 3.1.2.4 · Cold Environments Revision Mat; 9-Marker Cold Environments Model Answer

  • Four spec-named routes — use of technology, the role of governments, international agreements, and conservation groups.
  • Technology — the Trans-Alaskan Pipeline, 1300 km long, raised and insulated to avoid melting the permafrost.
  • International agreements — the Antarctic Treaty, in force 1961: research co-operation, no nuclear waste, controlled tourism, restricted military use.
  • Governments — fisheries and marine habitats protected around Alaska; the Western Arctic Reserve and ANWR.
  • Conservation groups — charities such as the WWF work with governments, businesses and local communities.
  • Fishing controls — quotas, bigger net holes to let juveniles escape, and the Arctic Council's 2021 agreement.

The four strategies, with the detail that earns Level 3

  • Technology. At Prudhoe Bay, Alaska, there are large oil reserves but it is almost impossible to move the oil out by tanker, so a 1300 km pipeline was built across Alaska. The oil is hot in the pipes to help it flow and would melt the permafrost, so the pipeline was raised and insulated. Alaska has frequent earthquakes, which could crack the pipe, so it was built to slide during an earthquake and to stop the flow of oil when a leak is detected. The school's model answer judges it a success: "the pipeline is still running after 20 years and so far there have been no destructive oil spills."
  • Governments. The National Environmental Policy Act ensures that companies involved in the transportation of oil "protect the natural environment and recognise the rights of native people". Fisheries and marine habitats are protected around Alaska, and the Western Arctic Reserve protects vast areas from oil and gas developments. The named protected area on Alaska's North Slope is ANWR, the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge.
  • International agreements. The Antarctic Treaty was signed by the countries with territorial claims and came into force in 1961. It promotes international co-operation for scientific research, bans the disposal of nuclear waste, encourages controlled tourism and restricts military use. It has kept economic development to a minimum despite the discovery of valuable resources — but it is up for renegotiation in 2041, which is why the 2041 Foundation exists. On fishing, the Arctic Council agreed in June 2021 to prevent all unregulated fishing in Arctic waters; the agreement was signed by the US, China, Russia, Canada, Japan, Denmark, Norway, Iceland, Korea and the EU.
  • Conservation groups. Charities such as the WWF work with governments, businesses and the local community to protect biodiversity. Fishing controls include quotas limiting the catch of a given species and an enforced increase in the size of holes in fishing nets so juvenile fish can escape and mature. The NOAA oversees sustainable fisheries in Alaska and protects marine habitats.

The honest weakness to include in any evaluation, from the teacher's own comment on the model answer: these waters are difficult to police, so the agreements rely on countries committing to them voluntarily.

More detail

The 1959 / 1961 question comes up: the Antarctic Treaty was signed in 1959 and came into force in 1961. The school's quick-fire list gives 1961 as the answer to "what year was the Antarctic treaty signed", and the mat says it "came into force in 1961", so 1961 is the figure to write; the model answer's 1959 is the signing date. Either will be credited if you say which.

The school's 9-marker model answer is organised as three strategies — the Alaskan pipeline, Arctic fishing, and the Antarctic Treaty — each ending with a judgement sentence that refers back to "success". The teacher's comment identifies why it scored top Level 3: "V good focus at the end of each paragraph on referring back to 'success' in the question title", and warns about the real risk in the exam — "challenge will be selecting key info and abridging this for a 9-marker". In 9 marks you have room for three paragraphs of roughly four sentences each, so pick two figures per strategy, not six.

02Cheat sheet

The facts you cannot sit the paper without

Polar vs tundra. Polar: below 0°C all year, winter below −50°C, low snowfall, permanently dark winters. Tundra: large annual temperature range, higher precipitation especially in summer, near or total winter darkness. Extreme example: Antarctica.

Distribution. Highest latitudes. North — the Arctic (polar) plus Canada, USA (Alaska), Russia, Northern Europe. South — Antarctica.

Permafrost. Hard, permanently frozen ground; impermeable, so it prevents soil formation and blocks water transfer. The active layer is the top layer that thaws in spring and summer.

Tundra soils. Very thin · not fertile · waterlogged in summer, frozen in winter · depth and fertility increase with distance from the poles.

Plant adaptations. Shallow roots in the active layer · low-growing cushion plants (moisture, wind shelter) · mosses (waterlogging then winter drought) · rapid life cycle for the short summer · bearberry.

Animal adaptations. Arctic fox and hare — thick fur on body and feet, white in winter for camouflage, varied diet. Musk oxen — two layers of fur, wide hooves for waterlogged ground and snow.

The 9-mark frame

This is the module where the Section B 9-marker actually lands, so the frame matters more here than anywhere else in Paper 1. Marked in levels — Basic 1–3, Clear 4–6, Detailed 7–9 — with 3 marks each for AO1 knowledge, AO2 understanding and AO3 evaluation. Two caps to avoid: no exemplification → Level 2; no consideration of extent → Level 2.

  • Introduction — name and locate Svalbard in one sentence. "Svalbard is a Norwegian territory in the Arctic Ocean, around 400 miles north of mainland Norway, with a polar climate, over 60% glacier cover and just 2700 inhabitants."
  • Paragraph 1 — the strongest opportunity or challenge, with figures. "One major opportunity is tourism: 150 000 tourists now visit each year, double the 2011 figure, supporting 400 jobs. However, tourism is seasonal, because winter temperatures fall to −30°C and the polar night makes the islands inaccessible — so the jobs are temporary and the benefit is unevenly spread across the year."
  • Paragraph 2 — the second one, and rank it against the first. "Fishing is arguably more reliable: the Barents Sea holds 150 species including cod and haddock, and unlike tourism it is not restricted by the polar night. Nevertheless, overfishing is a long-term threat, which is why Norway cooperates with Russia on quotas."
  • Paragraph 3 — the third, plus the direction of travel. "Mineral extraction has historically been the backbone: 300 people work in mining and Mine 7 near Longyearbyen still operates. However, coal is finite and environmentally damaging, and Norway is phasing it out — so this opportunity is declining."
  • Conclusion that decides and explains. "Overall, tourism is the fastest-growing and most visible opportunity, but its seasonality means fishing offers greater economic reliability, while mining is declining — so the most sustainable path lies in balancing all three while protecting the fragile Arctic environment that underpins them all."

Evaluation starters: "This is the most significant because…" · "Unlike tourism, fishing…" · "This is only true to a limited extent, because…" · "In the long term this matters more, since…" · "Although technology has overcome this, …" · "Overall, the physical environment creates challenges to a great extent, because…"

03Understanding it

U1

Why is the tundra waterlogged in summer when its precipitation is low?

Show answer

Because the water cannot go anywhere. The permafrost beneath the surface is hard and impermeable, so it acts as a barrier to water transfer: when the thin active layer thaws in spring and summer, the meltwater and what rain does fall cannot drain downwards. It therefore ponds on the surface, producing the lakes and wetlands the Arctic is full of. Low evaporation rates in the cold make it worse.

So the tundra manages to be both a near-desert by precipitation and a wetland by drainage, and that combination explains several adaptations at once — mosses that cope with waterlogging, and musk oxen with wide hooves for waterlogged ground.

The exam point: permafrost is the hinge between climate, soils, plants and animals. Mention it and the interdependence spec point answers itself.

U2

Why do cold environments recover from damage so much more slowly than temperate ones?

Show answer

Because every biological process that would do the repairing is slow. The growing season is a few weeks long, so plant regrowth is minimal each year. Temperatures are low, so decomposition and soil formation are extremely slow and there is almost no organic matter to rebuild a soil with. The soil is very thin to begin with, so once vegetation is stripped there may be nothing left to root in. And the species present are highly specialised — AQA's wording is that they "cannot tolerate environmental changes" — so they cannot simply adjust.

That is what "fragile" means in the key terms list: "easily disturbed and difficult to restore if disturbed".

The exam point: this is the sentence that makes the protection argument work. Without it, "cold environments are fragile" is an assertion.

U3

Why does the school's model answer argue that fishing is a more reliable opportunity than tourism in Svalbard?

Show answer

Because the polar night shuts tourism down and does not shut fishing down. Winter temperatures drop to −30°C and there are several months of darkness, so visitors cannot realistically come: tourism is concentrated into the summer, which makes many of its 400 jobs temporary and spreads the benefit unevenly across the year. Fishing in the Barents Sea continues year-round, is not restricted by darkness, and the catch — 150 species including cod and haddock — can be exported globally, bringing external revenue in.

The counter is that fishing has its own ceiling: overfishing, which is why quotas, larger net holes and Norwegian–Russian cooperation exist.

The exam point: this is what ranking looks like. The exam technique guide says that when a question has more than two themes you should identify them and then rank them — "reliable" is the criterion that does the ranking here.

U4

How can development in a cold environment be self-defeating?

Show answer

Because the development attacks the conditions it depends on. Most of Svalbard's energy is produced by burning coal, which contributes to global warming; global warming melts the permafrost that the settlements, roads and airport are built on, and raises sea level. The consequences are already costed: 250 homes destroyed, £18m needed for emergency housing, drinking water in two reservoirs under threat, and the potential to flood the airport.

The same logic runs through tourism: visitors come for an untouched polar landscape with polar bears and the Northern Lights, so the growth of tourism — 150 000 visitors, double 2011, 45 000 of them from cruise ships — erodes the very thing being sold.

The exam point: this is a ready-made evaluative paragraph for any "to what extent are cold environments at risk from economic development" question.

U5

Why was the Trans-Alaskan Pipeline raised and insulated rather than buried?

Show answer

Because the oil is deliberately kept hot so that it flows, and hot pipes buried in permafrost would melt it. The permafrost is, as the school's model answer puts it, "a vital part in the arctic ecosystem as it keeps the soil watertight to allow for lakes and wetlands to form" — and melting it would also make the ground subside under the 1300 km pipeline, cracking it and causing spills. Raising the pipeline on supports and insulating it keeps the heat in the oil and out of the ground.

Two more engineering answers to two more physical problems: Alaska has frequent earthquakes, so the pipeline was built to slide rather than snap, and the flow is stopped automatically when a leak is detected.

The exam point: each design feature answers a named physical challenge. That pairing — challenge, then technological response, then evidence it worked (still running after 20 years, no destructive spills) — is a complete Level 3 paragraph.

U6

Why is the scientific argument the strongest reason for protecting a wilderness area?

Show answer

Because it is the only one that depends on the environment being unspoilt rather than merely protected. Habitats, culture and biodiversity can in principle be conserved within a managed landscape. But to measure change — climate change above all — scientists need a baseline that human activity has not already altered, which is why Antarctica is described as untouched and therefore "the perfect laboratory". Modify it and you destroy the measurement, not just the place. That is why the Antarctic Treaty explicitly recognises Antarctica's importance for scientific research.

The counter-argument a good evaluation includes: research itself brings bases, flights, fuel and waste, so even science has a footprint — and the Treaty is up for renegotiation in 2041.

The exam point: when a question asks you to assess the importance of several reasons, pick the one with the distinctive logic and say why it is different in kind.

U7

Why do international agreements matter more in cold environments than almost anywhere else?

Show answer

Because no single government controls them. Antarctica has overlapping territorial claims and no permanent population, and the Arctic Ocean and the Barents Sea are shared between many states and partly beyond any national jurisdiction. A national law can protect ANWR or the Western Arctic Reserve, but it cannot stop fishing in international waters or mining under another country's claim. That is why the Antarctic Treaty and the Arctic Council's June 2021 agreement — signed by the US, China, Russia, Canada, Japan, Denmark, Norway, Iceland, Korea and the EU — are the tools that actually bite.

And it is why the weakness matters: as the teacher's comment on the model answer notes, these waters are extremely difficult to police, so the agreements depend on countries committing to them voluntarily.

The exam point: naming the enforcement problem is the evaluation. "The Antarctic Treaty has worked, but only while the signatories want it to" is a Level 3 sentence.

U8

The 2025 question asked to what extent the physical environment creates challenges for development. Why is that wording a trap?

Show answer

Because it invites you to write everything you know about cold and remoteness and then stop — which answers only half the question. "To what extent" requires you to weigh physical causes against the others, so you have to produce the alternatives: a population of just 2700 and therefore a tiny workforce; the cost of importing everything; international agreements and environmental regulation that restrict what may be done; conservation groups; and world commodity prices, which are the actual reason Svalbard's coal is being phased out.

Then you judge. The physical environment creates most of the direct challenges and generates the costs that make the others bite — but technology has overcome several of them, and the 1300 km Trans-Alaskan Pipeline proves it, so physical geography constrains development without determining it.

The exam point: for any "to what extent is X the cause" question, name a not-X before you conclude. Without an alternative, there is no extent to assess.

04Exam drill

What each command word actually wants

Paper 1 is 1 hour 30 minutes, 88 marks, 35%. Section B is compulsory and worth 25 marks, and the cold environments option sits at the end of it, carrying the 6- and 9-markers. From the school's exam technique guide and the AQA mark schemes:

  • 1 mark. Read the instruction. Shade one circle only. Don't miss these — they hide on busy pages.
  • 2 marks — outline / suggest. Point plus development, directly linked to the question. "It is rare that just a straightforward statement suffices."
  • 2 marks — compare / contrast climate data. At least two factors, connectives such as "whereas", and figures read off the axis.
  • 4 marks — explain. Level marked: Basic 1–2, Clear 3–4. Develop with "this means that… this leads to… as a result… consequently…".
  • 6 marks — explain / suggest / discuss. Level marked: Basic 1–2, Clear 3–4, Detailed 5–6. Even where the question does not demand a case study, use one — and if it names a figure you must write "in Figure…". Level 3 is called "detailed": include costs, dates and percentages.
  • 9 marks — discuss / to what extent / assess. Level marked across AO1, AO2 and AO3, 3 marks each. Two or three clear paragraphs plus a one- or two-sentence conclusion that links directly back to the question. Evaluate within each paragraph. "You can get up to 6 marks out of 9" on knowledge and explanation alone — the evaluation is what separates the top band. A generic answer, or one that ignores the extent, is capped at Level 2.
  • The ranking move. "Assess the importance…" questions have more than two themes: identify them, rank them, and if they are linked save the most important for last so you can tie everything together.

Twelve questions in the real Section B spread. The 6s and 9s are written as plans — AQA marks them in levels, so the mark points are a coverage target rather than a tick list.

D1

Compare and contrast polar and tundra environments. (2)

Show answer

Both polar and tundra environments experience cold temperatures, with winter months that are near or in total darkness 1

However, polar environments experience extreme cold — below 0°C throughout the year, falling below −50°C in winter — and permanent darkness in winter, whereas tundra environments have a large annual temperature range and conditions that are less harsh, enabling life to thrive particularly in summer 1

The connective "whereas" is doing the work of the second mark. A list of polar facts followed by a list of tundra facts is not a comparison.

D2

Suggest one reason for the difference in temperature between a cold environment such as Aklavik in the Canadian Arctic and a hot desert such as Cairo. (2)

Show answer

Aklavik lies at a much higher latitude than Cairo 1

This means the sun's rays strike the surface at a much lower angle, so the same amount of energy is spread over a larger surface area and is less concentrated — and in winter there is little or no insolation at all because of the polar night — which gives far lower temperatures and a much larger annual range 1

Also creditable as the development: more energy is reflected, both by the greater depth of atmosphere the rays pass through and by the high albedo of snow and ice.

✗ "Because it is further from the sun." The mark schemes reject this explicitly.

D3

Explain how permafrost affects the soils and vegetation of the tundra. (4)

Show answer

Permafrost is hard, permanently frozen ground, and because it is impermeable it acts as a barrier to water transfer and prevents soil formation, so tundra soils are very thin 1

Only the top layer — the active layer — thaws in spring and summer, so the soil is waterlogged in the summer and frozen in the winter, and roots are confined to that thin thawed layer 1

As a result plants have shallow roots, to access the nutrients and water close to the surface within the active layer 1

Low temperatures also slow decomposition, so nutrients are released very slowly and the soil is not particularly fertile — which, with the waterlogging, is why only specialists such as mosses, lichen, grasses and dwarf shrubs can grow 1

D4

Explain two ways plants are adapted to survive in a cold environment. (4)

Show answer

Many tundra plants are low-growing cushion plants 1; growing close to the ground means they sit below the worst of the wind, so they retain moisture and are sheltered from the drying wind that would otherwise desiccate them at a time when all the water in the ground is frozen 1

Flowering plants have a rapid life cycle 1; because the active layer only thaws and the light only returns for a few weeks, they must germinate, flower and seed within that very short summer or they will not reproduce at all — bearberry is an example of a plant adapted to these conditions 1

Equally creditable, developed the same way: shallow roots that stay within the active layer above the impermeable permafrost; and mosses, which cope with waterlogged conditions in the summer and survive the winter drought.

D5

Explain two ways animals are adapted to survive in a cold environment. (4)

Show answer

Musk oxen have two layers of fur 1; this traps a layer of air next to the body and insulates them against temperatures that can fall below −30°C, so they lose heat far more slowly than an unadapted animal would 1

Arctic foxes and Arctic hares have fur that becomes white in winter 1; this camouflages them against the snow, so hares are less visible to predators and foxes can approach prey unseen — and because snow cover is seasonal, the colour change rather than permanent white is what makes it effective 1

Also creditable: thick fur on bodies and feet for warmth; musk oxen's wide hooves, which let them walk on waterlogged ground and snow — waterlogging that exists because the permafrost blocks drainage; and the Arctic fox's varied diet, which helps when food is scarce.

D6

Explain two challenges of developing a cold environment. Use a case study. (4)

Show answer

On Svalbard, extreme temperature is a major challenge: winter temperatures drop to −30°C 1; this means specialised clothing and heated buildings are needed, and tourism can only happen in summer, so many of the 400 tourism jobs are seasonal and the economic benefit is spread unevenly across the year 1

Inaccessibility is the second: Svalbard lies around 400 miles north of mainland Norway, has only 50 km of road and one international airport with few flights 1; this means everything has to be imported at high cost and workers and visitors are hard to move in and out, which limits the scale of any development 1

The third spec challenge, equally creditable: the provision of buildings and infrastructure — melting permafrost and rising sea level have destroyed 250 homes, required £18m for emergency housing, threatened the drinking water in two reservoirs and risk flooding the airport.

D7

"Plants and animals adapt in order to survive in a hostile environment." Explain this statement for a cold environment. (6)

Show answer

Level marked: Basic 1–2, Clear 3–4, Detailed 5–6. Each adaptation must be named, explained, and tied to a named feature of the environment. Plan:

Establish the hostility first, with figures. Polar temperatures stay below 0°C all year and fall below −50°C in winter; Svalbard reaches −30°C; winters are permanently dark; the ground is permanently frozen and the active layer thaws for only a few weeks 1

Plant adaptation 1 — roots. Plants have shallow roots to access nutrients and water close to the surface within the active layer, because the impermeable permafrost beneath makes deeper rooting impossible 1

Plant adaptation 2 — form and timing. Low-growing cushion plants retain moisture and shelter from the drying wind; mosses cope with summer waterlogging and survive the winter drought when all water is frozen; flowering plants have a rapid life cycle so they can seed within the short summer 1

Animal adaptation 1 — insulation. Arctic foxes and hares have thick fur on their bodies and feet; musk oxen have two layers of fur, which traps air and dramatically slows heat loss 1

Animal adaptation 2 — camouflage and diet. In winter the fur of Arctic foxes and hares turns white, giving camouflage against snow; Arctic foxes eat a variety of foods, which helps when food is scarce 1

Animal adaptation 3 — the ground itself. Musk oxen have wide hooves so they can walk on waterlogged ground and snow — waterlogging that exists because permafrost blocks drainage, which shows the adaptations respond to the whole interlinked system, not just the cold 1

✗ "Animals have thick fur and plants are small."
✓ Name the species and the hazard every time. The school's feedback lesson caps generic answers at Level 1.

D8

Suggest how different strategies are used to reduce environmental damage in a cold environment. (6)

Show answer

Level marked. The spec names four routes — technology, governments, international agreements, conservation groups — so use them as the structure. Plan:

Technology — the pipeline. At Prudhoe Bay in Alaska the oil could not be moved out by tanker, so a 1300 km pipeline was built across Alaska; because the oil is kept hot to help it flow it would melt the permafrost, so the pipeline was raised and insulated 1

It was also engineered to slide during the earthquakes Alaska experiences frequently, and to stop the flow of oil automatically when a leak is detected — and it is still running after 20 years with no destructive oil spills 1

Governments. The National Environmental Policy Act requires companies transporting oil to protect the natural environment and recognise the rights of native people; fisheries and marine habitats are protected around Alaska, and the Western Arctic Reserve and ANWR protect vast areas from oil and gas development 1

International agreements — Antarctica. The Antarctic Treaty, in force in 1961, promotes international co-operation for scientific research, bans the disposal of nuclear waste, encourages controlled tourism and restricts military use; it has kept economic development to a minimum despite the discovery of valuable resources 1

International agreements — fishing. Quotas limit the catch of each species, net hole sizes were increased so juvenile fish can escape and mature, and in June 2021 the Arctic Council agreed to prevent all unregulated fishing in Arctic waters — signed by the US, China, Russia, Canada, Japan, Denmark, Norway, Iceland, Korea and the EU 1

Conservation groups, and a judgement. Charities such as the WWF work with governments, businesses and local communities to protect biodiversity, and the NOAA oversees sustainable fisheries in Alaska. The strategies have been largely successful, but they depend on enforcement: these waters are very hard to police, so the agreements rely on countries honouring commitments, and the Antarctic Treaty is up for renegotiation in 2041 1

D9

Explain why cold environments are fragile and should be protected as wilderness areas. (6)

Show answer

Level marked. Plan:

Define fragile and explain the mechanism. A fragile environment is one that is easily disturbed and difficult to restore if disturbed; cold environments qualify because the growing season is only a few weeks long and temperatures are low, so regrowth, decomposition and soil formation are all extremely slow and the flora and fauna take a long time to recover from damage 1

Specialisation. Plant communities here have evolved in highly specialised ways to deal with challenging conditions — shallow roots in the active layer, cushion forms, rapid life cycles — and as a result they cannot tolerate environmental change, so a disturbance that a temperate ecosystem would absorb removes species here 1

Scientific value. To study change such as climate change, scientists need to conduct research on unspoilt environments; Antarctica is untouched, which makes it the perfect laboratory, and the Antarctic Treaty explicitly recognises its importance for research — modify it and the baseline is destroyed 1

Cultural value. Indigenous people occupy some areas of tundra, and their culture depends on the natural world, so damage to wildlife is damage to a way of life 1

Habitat and ethical value. Cold environments provide habitats for a range of species of animals and birds — Svalbard has more polar bears than people and the Barents Sea holds 150 species of fish — and there is a moral responsibility to retain wilderness areas to protect their biodiversity 1

Judgement. The strongest of these is the scientific argument, because it is the only one that requires the environment to remain genuinely unspoilt rather than merely managed — although even research brings bases, flights and waste, so protection is a matter of degree 1

D10

Discuss the opportunities for economic development in a cold environment. Use a case study and your own understanding. (6)

Show answer

Level marked. This is the 2023 9-marker compressed to 6, which is a common school-mock shape. "Discuss" means weigh them, not list them. Plan:

Name and locate. Svalbard is a Norwegian territory in the Arctic Ocean, around 400 miles north of mainland Norway, with a polar climate, over 60% glacier cover, five major islands and 2700 inhabitants 1

Tourism. 150 000 tourists visit each year, double the 2011 figure, including 45 000 cruise ship passengers, and tourism provides 400 jobs for local people — more than mining — with a multiplier effect as money spent in hotels, restaurants and shops circulates locally 1

But tourism is seasonal. Winter temperatures drop to −30°C and the polar night makes the islands inaccessible to most visitors, so many of those jobs are temporary and the benefit is unevenly spread across the year 1

Fishing. The Barents Sea holds 150 different species of fish including commercially valuable cod and haddock, and unlike tourism it is not restricted by the polar night, so it provides year-round income and export revenue — though overfishing is a long-term threat, managed by quotas agreed with Russia 1

Mineral extraction and energy. 300 people work in mining, with Mine 7 near Longyearbyen still operating and most of the territory's energy produced by burning coal; summer brings 24 hours of sunlight. But coal is finite and damaging, and Norway is phasing it out in favour of renewables 1

Judgement. Tourism is the fastest-growing and most visible opportunity, but its seasonality means fishing offers greater economic reliability, while mining is declining — so the most sustainable path lies in balancing all three while protecting the fragile Arctic environment that underpins them all 1

D11

Discuss the challenges and opportunities for development in a cold environment. Use a case study. (9)

Show answer

Level marked in three bands (Basic 1–3, Clear 4–6, Detailed 7–9) with 3 marks each for AO1, AO2 and AO3. The real 2022 question. Pair each opportunity with its challenge rather than writing two separate lists. Plan:

Introduction. Svalbard is a Norwegian territory in the Arctic Ocean about 400 miles north of mainland Norway: polar climate, over 60% of the land under glaciers, five major islands, 2700 inhabitants, capital Longyearbyen 1

Paragraph 1 — tourism, the opportunity. 150 000 tourists a year, double the 2011 figure, 45 000 of them cruise passengers, supporting 400 jobs in a population of 2700, drawn by the polar landscape, the polar bears, the Northern Lights and the midnight sun 1

Tourism, the challenge. Winter temperatures drop to −30°C and there are months of darkness, so tourism happens only in summer and the jobs are largely temporary; there are also few hotels and attractions other than the natural landscape, and specialised clothing is required. So the opportunity is real but its benefit is unevenly spread across the year 1

Paragraph 2 — fishing, the opportunity. The Barents Sea is one of the world's most productive fishing grounds, with 150 different species including cod and haddock, exportable globally, and it continues through the polar night 1

Fishing, the challenge. Overfishing threatens the stock, so quotas are agreed with Russia, net hole sizes are regulated and the Arctic Council agreed in June 2021 to prevent unregulated fishing. This is the more reliable opportunity, because the constraint on it is political and manageable rather than physical and permanent 1

Paragraph 3 — mining and energy, the opportunity. 300 people work in mining, Mine 7 near Longyearbyen still operates, most energy comes from burning coal, and summer brings 24 hours of sunlight 1

Mining and energy, the challenge. Mining damages the landscape, burning coal contributes to global warming, and that warming melts the permafrost the settlements stand on: 250 homes destroyed, £18m needed for emergency housing, two reservoirs' drinking water threatened and the airport at risk of flooding. This development is therefore self-undermining, which is why Norway is phasing coal out 1

The cross-cutting challenge. Underlying all three is inaccessibility: only 50 km of road, one international airport with few flights, and a workforce of 2700 — so everything is expensive and nothing scales easily 1

Conclusion that decides. Overall the opportunities are substantial but each is capped by a physical challenge, and the challenges are more fundamental because they cannot be removed, only engineered around: tourism is the fastest-growing but the most seasonal, fishing the most reliable, mining the most damaging and in decline — so development in Svalbard is possible but only within limits that the environment, not the market, sets 1

✓ Pair them, rank them, and finish with a conclusion that says which side dominates and why.

D12

To what extent are cold environments at risk from economic development, and therefore in need of protection? (9)

Show answer

Level marked across AO1, AO2 and AO3. The real 2019 question. "To what extent" means the risk has to be weighed against the protection already in place. Plan:

Introduction — the fragility that creates the risk. Cold environments such as the Arctic and Antarctic are fragile: easily disturbed and difficult to restore, because the growing season is weeks long and the flora and fauna are so specialised that they cannot tolerate environmental change 1

Paragraph 1 — the risk from extraction and energy. Svalbard has 300 people working in mining and produces most of its energy by burning coal, which damages the landscape directly and contributes to global warming; Alaska's North Slope holds large oil reserves at Prudhoe Bay 1

The warming that burning coal contributes to is already melting Svalbard's permafrost and raising sea level: 250 homes destroyed, £18m needed for emergency housing, two reservoirs' drinking water threatened. So the risk is not hypothetical and it feeds back on the development itself 1

Paragraph 2 — the risk from fishing and tourism. The Barents Sea's 150 fish species are vulnerable to overfishing, and tourism has doubled since 2011 to 150 000 visitors a year with 45 000 arriving by cruise ship — concentrated into a short summer in a landscape that recovers very slowly 1

This risk is more diffuse than extraction but harder to contain, because it is driven by the very wilderness quality the visitors come for, so success erodes the asset 1

Paragraph 3 — the protection that already exists. Technology has reduced the risk substantially: the 1300 km Trans-Alaskan Pipeline was raised and insulated so the hot oil would not melt the permafrost, built to slide in earthquakes and to shut off on detecting a leak — and it is still running after 20 years with no destructive oil spills 1

Governments and agreements add more: the National Environmental Policy Act, ANWR and the Western Arctic Reserve, fishing quotas and larger net holes, the Arctic Council's June 2021 agreement against unregulated fishing, the NOAA, the WWF — and above all the Antarctic Treaty, in force 1961, which bans nuclear waste disposal, restricts military use, encourages controlled tourism and has kept economic development to a minimum despite the discovery of valuable resources 1

The limits of that protection. But these waters are extremely difficult to police, so the agreements depend on voluntary commitment; and the Antarctic Treaty is up for renegotiation in 2041, which is why the 2041 Foundation exists — so the protection is real but contingent 1

Conclusion that decides. Cold environments are at risk to a great extent, because their fragility means even modest development does lasting damage and because the main driver — global warming from energy use — reaches them regardless of local rules. But the pipeline and the Treaty show that protection genuinely works where it is enforced, so the need is less for new protection than for the existing agreements to be kept and policed beyond 2041 1
