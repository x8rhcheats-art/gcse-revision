What an ecosystem is, and the scales it works at

Spec 3.1.2.1 · Ecosystems & Rainforests Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms

  • Ecosystem defined — a community of plants and animals interacting with each other and with their physical environment.
  • Biotic means living — plants, animals, bacteria, fungi. Abiotic means non-living — soil, rock, water, climate.
  • Scales run small to global — a hedgerow, a pond, a wood, a garden; up to a biome covering a continent.
  • Biome defined — a very large, naturally occurring community of flora and fauna occupying a major area.
  • Interrelationships are the examined idea — the spec word is interaction; nothing in an ecosystem sits on its own.

✗ "An ecosystem is all the animals in an area." — leaves out plants and leaves out everything abiotic, which is half the definition.
✓ "An ecosystem is an area containing all the biotic parts, such as plants and animals, and the abiotic parts, such as soil, rocks and climate, sharing one environment and interacting with each other."

More detail

An ecosystem is an area that includes all the biotic (living) parts such as plants and animals and the abiotic (non-living) parts such as soil, rocks and climate, sharing an environment. The AQA key terms list splits those two words out separately because they are each worth a mark: biotic — relating to living things; abiotic — relating to non-living things.

A biome is a very large, naturally occurring community of flora and fauna occupying a major area. AQA's own wording for global ecosystem is "very large ecological areas on the earth's surface (or biomes), with fauna and flora adapting to their environment" — the examples it gives are tropical rainforest and hot desert.

The three named small-scale UK examples from the school mat are a hedgerow, a pond, a wood and a garden. John's class studied a pond, which is the one to write about. The spec wants a small-scale UK ecosystem "to illustrate the concept of interrelationships within a natural system" — so the pond is not decoration, it is the vehicle for proving you understand producers, consumers, decomposers, food chains, food webs and nutrient cycling.

Producers, consumers, decomposers, food chains and food webs

Spec 3.1.2.1 · AQA List of Key Terms; 2019 and 2023 Paper 1 Q2

  • Producer — an organism or plant that absorbs energy from the sun through photosynthesis; always the base of the chain.
  • Consumer — a creature that eats herbivores and/or plant matter; primary, secondary and tertiary stack upwards.
  • Decomposer — a bacterium or fungus that breaks down dead tissue, which is then recycled to the environment.
  • Food chain — the connections between organisms that rely on one another as their source of food; one line of energy.
  • Food web — a complex hierarchy of plants and animals relying on each other; a collection of interlinked food chains.
  • Biomass falls at every level — energy is lost as movement, heat and waste, so each level supports fewer organisms.

✗ "A food web is a bigger food chain." — the examiner wants the structural difference, not the size.
✓ "A food chain shows the flow of energy from producer to tertiary consumer in a single line, whereas a food web shows a collection of food chains and how they are interlinked."

More detail

The 2019 paper put a UK woodland food web in front of candidates — plants at the base, caterpillars, rabbits, beetles and voles as primary consumers, moles, mice, badgers and small birds as secondary consumers, tawny owl, sparrowhawk and fox as tertiary consumers, with decomposers down the side — and asked only three things: read one true statement off it, say what happens if foxes go extinct, and state one role of decomposers.

The "what if a species is removed" question is the one worth practising, because the answer is always a two-step chain: the thing it ate increases, and the thing that ate it decreases or has to switch prey. For the 2019 fox question: the number of rabbits, voles and beetles that foxes preyed on would increase (1), which would then put more pressure on the plants at the producer level, and other tertiary consumers such as the tawny owl would face more competition or have more prey available (1).

Biomass. The 2019 pyramid gave 100 000 units of deciduous tree leaves, 12 000 caterpillars, 300 small birds and 1 sparrowhawk, then asked for the percentage loss between primary and secondary consumer — 12 000 to 300 is a loss of 11 700, which is 97.5%. The two reasons biomass changes are that energy is lost at each transfer (used for movement and respiration, lost as heat, and lost in waste and in the parts of an organism that are not eaten), and that therefore each level can support fewer and larger organisms.

AQA's own wording for nutrient cycling is worth having verbatim: "a set of processes whereby organisms extract minerals necessary for growth from soil or water, before passing them on through the food chain — and ultimately back to the soil and water."

A small-scale UK ecosystem — the pond

Spec 3.1.2.1 · Case Study Summary Booklet 2026, section 5

  • The chain to learn — algae → midge larvae → fish such as perch → heron, with rag-tailed maggots as the decomposers.
  • Algae are the producers — they photosynthesise using sunlight entering the water, so they are the energy entry point.
  • Remove one component and the rest shifts — this is the point of the example, and 2023 asked exactly that about trout.
  • Nutrient cycling in the pond — decomposers break down dead leaves and animals, releasing nutrients back into the water for the algae.
  • Name it, don't generalise — 2023's 2-marker said "for a small-scale ecosystem that you have studied".

A small-scale UK ecosystem: a pond

The school's named example. Five trophic roles, each with its organism — learn them as a ladder, because the exam asks you to identify a level or to say what happens when one is removed.

  • Producer — algae. Absorb energy from the sun by photosynthesis.
  • Primary consumer — midge larvae. Feed on the algae.
  • Secondary consumer — fish, e.g. perch. Feed on the midge larvae.
  • Tertiary consumer — heron. Top of the pond food chain.
  • Decomposers — rag-tailed maggots. Break down dead tissue so the nutrients are recycled to the water and sediment.

Abiotic components of the pond: the water itself, the sunlight entering it, the dissolved oxygen, the sediment on the bottom and the temperature. Interrelationship to quote: the algae depend on sunlight and dissolved nutrients; the midge larvae depend on the algae; the perch depend on the midge larvae; the heron depends on the perch; and all of them depend on the decomposers returning nutrients to the water so the algae can grow again.

More detail

The 2023 paper asked "For a small-scale ecosystem that you have studied, outline the link between producers and consumers" for 2 marks — which means a point plus a development, both grounded in the named place. The answer is: in the pond I studied, the algae are the producers, absorbing energy from sunlight by photosynthesis (1); the midge larvae are the primary consumers that eat the algae, so the energy fixed by the algae passes up to the perch and then the heron, and without the algae none of the consumers could be supported (1).

The 2023 trout question — "state one effect on the food web if disease killed most of the trout" — is the same move in one mark: the organisms the trout fed on would increase in number, and the predators that fed on the trout would decline or have to find alternative prey.

Nutrient cycling in a pond. Dead leaves blown into the water, dead algae and dead animals form a layer of litter on the bottom. Decomposers break this down, releasing nutrients into the water and the sediment. The algae take those nutrients up and grow, and the cycle restarts. The pond is a closed enough system that a change to one store — an input of fertiliser, say, or the loss of the heron — is visible all the way through it, which is why the spec uses a small-scale ecosystem to teach interrelationships.

Ecosystem balance — change one component and the rest follows

Spec 3.1.2.1 · Ecosystems & Rainforests Revision Booklet, "Ecosystem balance"

  • Ecosystems are fragile — a change to one component has a knock-on effect on the rest, positive or negative.
  • Natural causes — disease, drought, fire, storms, climate change, a natural change in a predator population.
  • Human causes — deforestation, pollution, introducing or removing a species, farming, building, over-fishing.
  • Yellowstone is the classic case — re-introducing wolves changed grazing, vegetation and even the rivers.
  • Answer in a chain — name the change, then the direct effect, then the knock-on effect: two steps minimum.
More detail

Ecosystems are fragile environments and changes to one component can have a knock-on effect on the rest of the ecosystem. The impacts can be both positive and negative. The booklet's named illustration is the re-introduction of wolves to Yellowstone National Park in the USA.

Two worked scenarios from the booklet, both good practice for the 1- and 2-mark versions:

  • Deforestation in a small woodland ecosystem. The producers are removed, so the primary consumers lose their food source and decline, and the consumers above them follow. Habitats are lost. With no leaf fall there is no litter layer, so the decomposers have nothing to break down and the nutrient cycle stalls. Bare soil is exposed to rain and is eroded, and with the topsoil gone the ecosystem cannot easily recover.
  • More trout (predators) added to a pond. The trout eat the primary and secondary consumers, so those populations fall. With fewer consumers eating the algae, the algae may increase, which can reduce the oxygen and light in the water. Competition between the trout and the existing predators — the heron — increases, so the heron population may fall or move away.

Nutrient cycling, and why it is so efficient in the rainforest

Spec 3.1.2.1, 3.1.2.2 · Living World Mock mark scheme Q1.6; 2023 Paper 1 Q2.6–2.8

  • Three stores — biomass (the living vegetation), litter (the dead material on the ground) and soil.
  • Three flows — fallout (biomass to litter), decay (litter to soil) and uptake (soil to biomass).
  • Biomass is the biggest store — in the 2023 figure, trunk 120, branches 80, roots 30, leaves 10 tonnes C/ha.
  • It cycles fast because it is hot and wet — ideal conditions for decomposers, so litter breaks down in weeks, not years.
  • The soil itself is poor — thin, red and heavily leached; the nutrients are in the trees, not the ground.
  • Remove the trees and the cycle collapses — no fallout, no decay, and the soil becomes infertile and erodible.

✗ "The soil in the rainforest is really fertile because there's so much vegetation." — the exact opposite, and a very common error.
✓ "The soil is a small store: nutrients are taken up almost as soon as they are released, and heavy rainfall leaches what is left, so the fertility is held in the biomass, not the soil."

More detail

The school's Living World mock set a 6-marker on exactly this — "Explain why the nutrient cycle is so efficient in the tropical rainforest" — and its mark scheme is the best list of points available:

  • The high biodiversity provides a significant amount of fallout as tissues die; the vegetation is evergreen, so there is a consistent input into the litter layer all year rather than one autumn drop.
  • High temperatures and the wet climate provide ideal conditions for decomposers to thrive and rapidly break down organic matter such as leaves, twigs and dead animals.
  • Decomposers convert dead matter into a form that can be re-used, so the chemicals and nutrients can be absorbed by the vegetation.
  • Where vegetation is dense there is little leaching of nutrients, because of interception in the canopy.
  • Many trees have wide buttress roots that rapidly absorb nutrients released from the litter layer into the topsoil.
  • Regular convectional rainfall and weathered rock beneath the soil both add extra nutrients into the cycle.
  • And the sting in the tail: if vegetation is removed this leads to the rapid destruction of the nutrient cycle, the soils quickly become infertile and vulnerable to erosion.

The 2023 paper tested the same material three ways in three marks: a percentage off the biomass table (roots 30 out of 120+80+10+30 = 240, so 12.5%), one reason nutrient cycling is rapid, and one human activity that interferes with the cycle — deforestation, logging, farming, or mining, all of which remove the biomass store that drives it. The rainforest soil is a latosol, the red soil named in the school's quick-fire list.

Global ecosystems — where they are and what they are like

Spec 3.1.2.1 · Ecosystems & Rainforests Revision Mat; 2022 and 2023 Paper 1 Q2

  • They run in belts — broad east–west bands roughly parallel to lines of latitude, because climate is controlled by latitude.
  • Tropical rainforest — on and close to the Equator, between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn; hot and wet all year.
  • Hot desert — around 30° north and south; very high temperatures, very low precipitation.
  • Tundra — high latitudes of the northern hemisphere; treeless, permanently frozen ground, lichen, moss and dwarf shrubs.
  • Temperate deciduous forest is the UK's biome — trees drop their leaves because winter temperatures are lower.
  • Three big rainforest blocks — Amazonia, central and west Africa, and south-east Asia and Indonesia.

✗ "Rainforests are near the Equator." — one bare statement; the 2-mark describe-distribution question needs named places and a second idea.
✓ "Tropical rainforests are found along the Equator and between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn. The largest area is Amazonia in South America; there are also large areas in central and west Africa and in south-east Asia and Indonesia, with smaller areas on the north and east coasts of Australia."

More detail

The school mat's biome map identified three: A tropical rainforest, B hot desert, C tundra. The UK's global ecosystem is temperate deciduous forest — that is a one-mark recall fact from the school's quick-fire list.

Why the belts run east–west. Ecosystems are controlled mainly by climate, and climate is controlled mainly by latitude: the angle of incoming solar radiation, the resulting temperature, and the global circulation of air that produces rising wet air at the Equator and sinking dry air around 30°. Because latitude runs east–west, so do the ecosystems. Relief, ocean currents and continentality distort the pattern, which is why the belts are not perfectly straight.

The 2022 paper took the biome map and asked three one-markers off it, and the lesson from the mark schemes is to read the key: the true statement was "Coniferous forests occur in large areas of North America, Europe and Asia", and the temperate deciduous forest characteristic was "the trees drop their dead leaves because of lower temperatures in winter" — not "the vegetation is evergreen", not "rainfall is low".

The 2025 paper gave a bar chart of rainforest areas and asked for the second largest and then for the Amazon as a percentage of the 968 million hectare world total to one decimal place. Read the axis, do the division, and give the decimal place the question asks for — the school's feedback lesson notes that candidates lost that mark by ignoring "to one decimal place".

The physical characteristics of a tropical rainforest — climate and structure

Spec 3.1.2.2 · Ecosystems & Rainforests Revision Mat; Case Study Summary Booklet 2026

  • Hot and constant — temperatures typically above 25–35°C, with almost no annual range because the sun is overhead all year.
  • Wet all year — high convectional rainfall every month; the 2022 Iquitos graph never drops below roughly 150 mm.
  • Five layers — emergents, canopy, under canopy, shrub layer, ground layer.
  • The canopy holds the wildlife — a continuous sea of tree tops containing over 50% of rainforest wildlife.
  • Dense and dark below — the ground layer is dark and damp, with a litter layer of rotting leaves and dead animals.
  • Hugely biodiverse — more than two-thirds of the world's plant species and half its animal species live in rainforests.
More detail

Climate. From the Case Study Summary Booklet: temperatures are high and constant, typically above 25–35°C. The reason is latitude — the energy of the sun is more concentrated near the Equator, the curvature of the Earth means less energy is reflected, and the climate is consistent all year round. The mock mark scheme is explicit that you must reject the idea that tropical regions are closer to the sun. Rainfall is convectional: intense daytime heating causes air to rise, cool and condense, giving a heavy afternoon downpour almost daily.

Structure, in the school mat's own words. "Emergents are the tallest trees that emerge from the canopy. The canopy is the sea of tree tops that contain over 50% of rainforest wildlife. The under canopy mainly contains bare tree trunks and lianas. Lianas are vines that climb the vegetation in a bid to reach sunlight. The shrub layer contains shrubs and ferns and other plants needing less light. Saplings of emergents and canopy trees can also be found here. The ground layer is usually dark and damp. It contains a layer of rotting leaves and dead animals called litter."

The 2019 paper put a labelled height diagram in front of candidates — emergent layer above 35 m with widely spaced trees, upper canopy of medium-spaced trees, lower canopy, understorey of shrubs and saplings, ground cover of herbs and ferns — and asked which layer matched "an almost continuous layer of branches and leaves between 15 and 30 metres high" (the upper canopy) and for one characteristic of the base of the taller trees (the wide buttress roots). Learn the heights with the names.

Interdependence of climate, water, soils, plants, animals and people

Spec 3.1.2.2 · Ecosystems & Rainforests Revision Mat and Booklet, "Interdependence"

  • Climate drives everything — the heat and rain give year-round growth, which gives the biomass everything else depends on.
  • Vegetation drives the water cycle — trees intercept rain and transpire it back, generating the next day's convectional rainfall.
  • Soils depend on vegetation, not the reverse — leaf fall is the only real nutrient input, so the biomass maintains the soil.
  • Animals depend on the layers — each storey offers different food and shelter, which is why there are so many niches.
  • People are inside the system — indigenous groups live by shifting cultivation, hunting and gathering within it.
  • Break one link and the rest unravels — this is the sentence that turns a 4-mark answer into a 6-mark one.
More detail

Interdependence is the spec's word and it is an easy 6-mark structure: pick three links and explain each as a chain.

  • Climate → plants → climate. Constant heat and heavy rainfall allow continuous growth, so the forest is evergreen and dense. The dense canopy then intercepts rainfall and transpires moisture back into the atmosphere, which feeds the convectional rainfall that keeps the climate wet. The forest partly makes its own weather, which is why large-scale clearance can dry a region out.
  • Plants → soil → plants. The soil is a latosol: thin and leached by the heavy rainfall. Nutrients come almost entirely from the litter layer, broken down fast by decomposers in the warm, wet conditions and taken straight back up by shallow roots and buttress roots. Remove the trees and the only nutrient input goes with them.
  • Plants → animals. The layered structure creates habitats at every height: emergents for birds of prey, the canopy for monkeys and most of the wildlife, the dark ground layer for insects and decomposers. Specific plants support specific animals, which is why habitat loss translates so directly into species loss.
  • People. Indigenous people have lived in the Amazon for thousands of years by small-scale shifting cultivation, hunting, fishing and gathering, which the forest can absorb. Commercial activity at scale — ranching, soy, logging, mining, dams — cannot be absorbed, which is the contrast the 9-markers want.

How plants and animals adapt to the physical conditions

Spec 3.1.2.2 · Ecosystems & Rainforests Revision Mat; 2022 Q2.4 (6 marks); 2025 Q2.3

  • Drip tips — leaves taper so water runs off, avoiding damage from heavy tropical rainfall and stopping fungal growth.
  • Buttress roots — large roots on all sides of a shallowly rooted tree; stop it falling and gather litter-layer nutrients.
  • Thin, smooth bark — no cold to protect against, and smoothness stops lianas and epiphytes colonising the trunk.
  • Lianas and thick waxy leaves — lianas climb to reach canopy sunlight; waxy leaves protect from intense sunlight.
  • Animal adaptations are about moving and hiding — prehensile tails, curved feet, sticky toe pads, camouflage, poison.
  • Name the animal every time — poison dart frog, sloth, spider monkey, gecko, jaguar; "animals have camouflage" scores nothing.

✗ "Plants have adapted by growing tall to reach the light." — generic, and the school's feedback lesson flags exactly this: "ensure the adaptation is specific to rainforest plants" and "most wrote generically about vegetation whereas the question required you to be specific about rainforest vegetation."
✓ "Drip tips on the leaves allow the heavy convectional rainfall — over 2000 mm a year at Iquitos — to run straight off, which means the leaf is not damaged by the weight of water and fungi cannot grow on a permanently wet surface."

More detail

Plant adaptations, in the mat's own words.

  • Thin, smooth bark, as trees do not need protection from the cold. The bark is smooth to protect the tree from being colonised by lianas and epiphytes.
  • Buttress roots are large roots on all sides of a shallowly rooted tree. Typically they are found in nutrient-poor rainforest soils and do not penetrate to deeper layers. They prevent the tree from falling over while also gathering more nutrients released from the litter layer into the topsoil.
  • Lianas are vines that climb tree trunks in a bid to reach sunlight in the canopy.
  • Drip tips allow water to run off leaves. This avoids damage from heavy, tropical rainfall.
  • Thick, waxy leaves protect the plant from sunlight.

Animal adaptations, in the mat's own words.

  • The poison dart frog excretes poison through its skin, and its bright colour warns potential predators against eating it.
  • Sloths are built for life in the trees because their arms are longer than their legs and their feet are curved, which helps them grasp branches. Sloths move very slowly, which helps them avoid being seen by predators.
  • The prehensile tail of the spider monkey allows it to grasp the branches of trees.
  • Geckos have developed large, flattened toe pads with sticky scales on their undersides. These help them grip the smooth tree trunks commonly found in the rainforest, allowing them to climb vertically up trees.
  • The jaguar's fur keeps it camouflaged in the tropical rainforest.

The 2022 6-marker gave a climate graph for Iquitos, Peru and asked how plants are adapted to the climate. That wording matters: every adaptation you give must be tied back to a climate figure read off the graph — temperatures around 25–27°C every month, rainfall over 150 mm every month with a peak over 300 mm. Drip tips and waxy leaves answer the rainfall; thin bark answers the absence of cold; buttress roots answer the leached soil that the rainfall produces.

Biodiversity and the issues around it

Spec 3.1.2.2 · AQA List of Key Terms; 2024 Q2.1–2.2

  • Biodiversity defined — the variety of life in the world or in a particular habitat.
  • Rainforests hold most of it — more than 50% of the plant and animal species on Earth live in tropical rainforests.
  • Why so high — constant heat and rainfall give year-round growth, and the layered structure creates many niches.
  • The threat is habitat loss — deforestation for ranching, logging, mining and dams removes the habitats directly.
  • Loss is hard to reverse — species become endangered then extinct, and medicinal plants used against diseases such as cancer are lost.
More detail

AQA's definition: biodiversity is "the variety of life in the world or a particular habitat". The 2023 fact file states that tropical rainforests are home to more than 50% of the plant and animal species on Earth; the Case Study Summary Booklet puts it as more than two-thirds of the world's plant species and half the world's animal species.

The 2024 paper asked for one reason why biodiversity is high, for 2 marks — so point plus development. The point: the climate is hot and wet all year (1). The development: this means there is no growing season to interrupt plant growth, so vegetation is dense and layered, which creates a very large number of different habitats and food sources and therefore supports a very large number of species (1).

And then one threat, for 1 mark: deforestation, or any named cause of it — cattle ranching, soy, logging, mining, road building, dams. The mat's version of why it matters: "the loss of rainforest reduces biodiversity. This can lead to individual species becoming endangered and then possibly extinct. The availability of certain types of medicinal drugs that are used to fight diseases such as cancer" is reduced.

Deforestation — changing rates, and the causes

Spec 3.1.2.2 · Case Study Summary Booklet 2026, section 6; 2022, 2023 and 2024 Paper 1

  • Seven named causes — subsistence and commercial farming, logging, road building, mineral extraction, energy development, settlement, population growth.
  • Cattle ranching dominates — 80% of Amazon deforestation; another 10% is other commercial agriculture.
  • Brazil is the global centre — over 40% of global tropical deforestation occurs in Brazil.
  • Rates are falling but still huge — Amazon annual deforestation around 13 000 km² in 2021, far below the 2004 peak.
  • A fifth is already gone — 20% of the Amazon rainforest has already been destroyed.
  • Rates fall for reasons you can name — government protection, monitoring, international pressure, certification, ecotourism income.

The Amazon rainforest — causes of deforestation

John's class's named case study, and the one to use for every "use a case study" rainforest question. The figures are what lift an answer out of Level 2.

  • Subsistence and commercial farming — 80% of deforestation in the Amazon is due to cattle ranching, and a further 10% is due to other commercial agriculture. Soybean covers 25 million hectares, half of which is exported.
  • Logging — over 40% of global tropical deforestation occurs in Brazil. Slow-growing hardwoods such as mahogany take many hundreds of years to mature, so the loss is effectively permanent. Inhabitants rely on the revenue from logging.
  • Road building — the Trans-Amazonian Highway is 4000 km long, and it makes the forest accessible for others to exploit.

More detail

Changing rates. The 2024 6-marker — "Suggest how deforestation in the tropical rainforest can be caused by a range of different activities. Use a case study" — is asking for breadth with figures. Three or four of the Amazon causes above, each with its number and a sentence of explanation, is a Level 3 answer.

  • Climate change — CO₂ is no longer captured; deforestation 2001–2019 released 105 gigatonnes of CO₂.

More detail

Local impacts, in the mat's words. "When vegetation is removed soil is left exposed to the heavy equatorial rainfall. It is rapidly eroded. The removal of topsoil means little vegetation will grow. Also, soil erosion leads to flooding as the soil is deposited on river beds." "The nutrient cycle is destroyed when vegetation is removed. Global impacts, in the mat's words. "Increase in CO₂ in the atmosphere as it is no longer being captured by the rainforest. This supports climate change." "The loss of rainforest reduces biodiversity. This can lead to individual species becoming endangered and then possibly extinct. The availability of certain types of medicinal drugs that are used to fight diseases such as cancer" is reduced.

The 2023 fact file — Figure 10 on the real paper — is a ready-made set of evidence, so learn it as one block: tropical rainforests are home to more than 50% of the plant and animal species on Earth; deforestation from 2001 to 2019 released 105 gigatonnes of carbon dioxide; cattle ranching accounts for 80% of current deforestation in Brazil; Indonesia's forests are being cleared to create oil palm plantations; bauxite, iron ore, manganese, gold and diamonds are mined in tropical forests; and removal of forest increases the risk of flooding and soil erosion.

The value of tropical rainforests, and sustainable management

Spec 3.1.2.2 · Ecosystems & Rainforests Revision Mat; 2022 Q2.8; 2024 Q2.7; 2025 Q2.6 and Figure 7

  • Value to people — indigenous homes, medicines and research products, timber and food, and tourism income.
  • Value to the environment — a carbon sink, it maintains the water cycle, and it holds over half of all species.
  • Selective logging — cutting only mature or inferior trees so the remaining forest keeps growing.
  • Ecotourism — responsible, small-group travel that conserves the environment, sustains local wellbeing and educates.
  • Global strategies — international agreements on hardwoods and endangered species, debt reduction by HICs, NGO conservation and education.
  • National strategies — protected areas and reserves, ecology on the curriculum, stopping illegal exploitation.

Sustainable management — the strategies, split local and global

Straight from the school mat, plus AQA's own definitions. The spec names six: selective logging and replanting, conservation and education, ecotourism, international agreements about tropical hardwoods, and debt reduction.

  • Global — inter-governmental agreements on hardwoods and endangered species; debt reduction by HICs (countries are relieved of some of their debt in return for protecting their rainforests); conservation and education by NGOs.
  • National — creating protected areas or reserves; making subjects such as ecology compulsory on the curriculum; stopping illegal exploitation of the tropical rainforest.
  • Selective logging — "the cutting out of trees which are mature or inferior, to encourage the growth of the remaining trees in a forest or wood." Because the canopy is not removed wholesale, the nutrient cycle and the soil survive and the forest regenerates.
  • Ecotourism — "responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, sustains the wellbeing of the local people, and may involve education. It is usually carried out in small groups and has minimal impact on the local ecosystem."
  • Sustainability — "actions and forms of progress that meet the needs of the present without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their needs."

The 2025 figure listed the rainforest's value as: medicines and other research products, home to indigenous people, maintains the water cycle, and carbon sink. Learn those four as the spine of any "value" answer and attach a figure to each.

More detail

The 2022 paper asked candidates to "explain how ecotourism can be a sustainable management strategy" for 2 marks — point plus development. Point: ecotourism is carried out in small groups and has minimal impact on the local ecosystem, so the forest is left standing (1). Development: it gives local people an income that depends on the forest surviving rather than being cleared, so there is an economic incentive to conserve it, and it usually involves education that spreads conservation further (1).

The 2024 paper asked for one way selective logging manages the rainforest sustainably in 1 mark: only mature or inferior trees are taken, so the canopy and the younger trees remain and the forest regenerates.

The 2025 6-marker — "'Tropical rainforests are of value to people and the environment.' Discuss this statement" — wants both halves. People: home to indigenous groups who have lived there for thousands of years; a source of medicines used against diseases such as cancer; timber, food and other research products; tourism income. Environment: a carbon sink holding carbon out of the atmosphere; maintenance of the water cycle through interception and transpiration; habitat for over 50% of the world's plant and animal species; protection of soils from erosion. Then discuss: the value to the environment is global and irreplaceable, whereas much of the value to people can in principle be obtained elsewhere — which is exactly why the two come into conflict.

The 9-mark frame

Section B's big question is 9 marks, marked in levels (Basic 1–3, Clear 4–6, Detailed 7–9) across AO1 knowledge, AO2 understanding and AO3 application. Three paragraphs plus a one- or two-sentence conclusion, built on P.E.E.L, and with the evaluation inside each paragraph rather than saved for the end. A generic answer with no exemplification is capped at Level 2; so is an answer that never addresses the "extent".

  • Open by naming the case study in one sentence. "The Amazon rainforest in South America is the world's largest tropical rainforest, and over 40% of global tropical deforestation occurs in Brazil."
  • Paragraph 1 — the economic case. "One major economic activity is cattle ranching, which accounts for 80% of Amazon deforestation. This means… As a result… This is highly significant because…"
  • Paragraph 2 — the environmental case. "However, the environmental impact is severe: deforestation released 105 gigatonnes of CO₂ between 2001 and 2019. Consequently… This outweighs the economic gain because…"
  • Paragraph 3 — management, or the other side. "On the other hand, strategies such as selective logging, ecotourism and debt reduction mean development and conservation are not entirely incompatible, because…"
  • Conclusion that decides. "Overall I mostly agree, because the environmental losses are global and irreversible — 20% of the Amazon has already gone — whereas the economic gains are concentrated and could partly be achieved through sustainable strategies instead."

Sentence starters for the evaluation: "This is the most significant impact because…" · "This matters more in the long term, since…" · "This is only true to a limited extent, because…" · "Compared with the previous point, this is…" · "Overall, the statement is true to a great extent, because…"

03Understanding it

U1

Why does biomass fall so sharply at each level of a food chain?

Show answer

Because energy is lost at every transfer. An organism uses most of the energy it takes in for respiration, movement and keeping warm, and loses more in waste; only what is left is stored as new tissue, and only that stored tissue is available to whatever eats it. On top of that, not every organism at a level gets eaten, and not every part of the ones that are eaten is digestible.

The 2019 pyramid makes the scale visible: 100 000 units of leaves support 12 000 caterpillars, which support 300 small birds, which support one sparrowhawk. The loss from primary to secondary consumer is 97.5%.

The exam point: this is why there are always far fewer top predators than producers, and why removing a top predator has such a visible effect — it sits on an enormous pyramid of energy.

U2

Why is the rainforest so productive if its soil is so poor?

Show answer

Because the fertility is not in the soil — it is in the biomass, and it is recycled almost instantly. Litter falls all year because the vegetation is evergreen, and the hot, wet climate lets decomposers break it down in weeks. Shallow and buttress roots take the released nutrients straight back up before the heavy rainfall can leach them away. So the soil is only a staging post.

That is also why the system is so fragile. Clear the trees and you remove the only real nutrient input, the litter layer stops being replaced, the thin latosol is leached and then eroded by the equatorial rainfall, and the land cannot support dense vegetation again.

The exam point: "fast cycle, small soil store" explains both the productivity and the fragility, so one idea answers two different questions.

U3

Why do global ecosystems run in broad east–west belts rather than in patches?

Show answer

Because the main control on an ecosystem is climate, and the main control on climate is latitude. The angle at which solar radiation strikes the surface, and therefore how concentrated that energy is, varies with latitude; so does the global circulation of the atmosphere, which gives rising, wet air at the Equator and sinking, dry air around 30° north and south. Lines of latitude run east–west, so the resulting climate zones — and the ecosystems adapted to them — do too.

The pattern is distorted by relief, by the distribution of land and sea, by ocean currents and by continentality, which is why the belts on a biome map are not straight lines.

The exam point: when a question asks you to describe a distribution, use latitude and named continents; when it asks you to explain it, use the angle of the sun and the global circulation.

U4

Why does the school tell you to name the pond rather than write generally about small-scale ecosystems?

Show answer

Because the spec point is "an example of a small scale UK ecosystem to illustrate the concept of interrelationships", and the exam wording follows it: "for a small-scale ecosystem that you have studied". An answer that says "producers make energy and consumers eat them" has illustrated nothing; one that names algae, midge larvae, perch, heron and rag-tailed maggots has demonstrated the concept with real organisms, which is what the second mark is for.

The school's feedback lesson makes the same complaint about the rainforest adaptation question: "most wrote generically about vegetation whereas the question required you to be specific." Specificity is the difference between Level 1 and Level 2 throughout this topic.

The exam point: a named organism, place or figure in every sentence of a case-study answer.

U5

Why is cattle ranching, rather than logging, the dominant cause of Amazon deforestation?

Show answer

Because ranching needs cleared land, permanently, and a lot of it. Logging removes individual trees — often selectively, since slow-growing hardwoods such as mahogany are what is valuable — whereas pasture requires the forest to be removed completely and kept removed. Global demand for beef makes that profitable, and the same logic drives the 25 million hectares of soy, half of which is exported as animal feed.

So 80% of Amazon deforestation is cattle ranching and a further 10% is other commercial agriculture: 90% of clearance is agricultural.

The exam point: this is why consumer and retailer pressure in HICs, and debt-reduction deals, can actually slow deforestation — the driver is an export market, not local subsistence.

U6

How can road building be a cause of deforestation when a road itself clears very little forest?

Show answer

Because it is an enabling cause. The Case Study Summary Booklet puts it in one line: the Trans-Amazonian Highway, 4000 km long, "makes the forest accessible for others to exploit." Before the road, ranching, logging, mining and settlement in the interior are uneconomic because nothing can be moved out. After it, all of them become viable, and clearance spreads outwards from the road corridor.

The same argument applies to the Belo Monte Dam and to mining concessions: the infrastructure is not the damage, it is the permission for the damage.

The exam point: when a question says "a range of different activities", show how the causes interact rather than listing them as independent items — that is understanding rather than knowledge.

U7

Why is ecotourism counted as sustainable management when it brings more people into a fragile environment?

Show answer

Because of the incentive it creates and the scale at which it operates. AQA's definition builds the safeguards in: it is "usually carried out in small groups and has minimal impact on the local ecosystem", it "conserves the environment", it "sustains the wellbeing of the local people", and it "may involve education". The forest has to stay standing for the income to continue, so local people and governments gain financially from conservation rather than from clearance — which is the only durable way to compete with an 80% ranching figure.

The counter-argument, which a 6-marker will reward: it still means flights, vehicles, lodges and disturbance; the income is seasonal and unevenly spread; and it only protects the areas tourists want to visit.

The exam point: sustainable does not mean zero impact, it means meeting present needs without reducing future generations' ability to meet theirs.

U8

Why does an answer that lists impacts without weighing them get capped at Level 2 on a 9-marker?

Show answer

Because the third assessment objective is not knowledge or understanding but application — AO3 asks you to apply what you know "in a reasoned way in evaluating" something. A list demonstrates AO1 and some AO2 and stops. The mark schemes say it explicitly: "an answer that lacks consideration of the extent… is limited to Level 2", and "a purely generic answer without clear exemplification is limited to Level 2."

So evaluation is not a flourish at the end; it is a third of the marks. The school's exam technique guide says to evaluate inside each paragraph and to finish with a conclusion that picks a degree — definitely, mostly, partly, slightly, not at all.

The exam point: after every piece of evidence, add a sentence that says how much it matters and why, compared with what you said before.

04Exam drill

What each command word actually wants

Paper 1 is 1 hour 30 minutes, 88 marks, 35% of the GCSE. Section B (The living world) is compulsory and worth 25 marks. The question types and what the school's exam technique guide and the mark schemes say about each:

  • 1 mark — state / give / identify / multiple choice. "The main issue with these is that people do not read the instructions clearly." Shade one circle only; two shaded circles score nothing. Don't miss them — "they can tend to hide on a busy page."
  • 2 marks — outline / suggest / describe. "Leave nothing to chance — these questions often require points to be developed and directly linked to the question; it is rare that just a straightforward statement suffices." Point + development. Use geographical terminology.
  • 2–3 marks — describe a graph or map. Trend, extremities, data, anomalies. Quote figures off the axis. Watch the units and any "to one decimal place".
  • 4 marks — explain. Level marked (Basic 1–2, Clear 3–4). For a process or a landform: step by step, name and explain each process, nothing implied.
  • 6 marks — explain / discuss / assess / "do you agree". Level marked (Basic 1–2, Clear 3–4, Detailed 5–6), with AO2 and AO3 split evenly. Develop points with "this means that… this leads to… as a result… consequently…". If the question names a figure you must write "in Figure…". Level 3 is called "detailed" — include facts such as costs, dates or percentages. Reference to a named example is required for Level 3.
  • 9 marks — discuss / evaluate / to what extent / assess. Level marked across AO1, AO2 and AO3 (3 marks each). Two or three clear paragraphs plus a one- or two-sentence conclusion that links directly back to the question. Use S.E.E.P for impacts and P.E.E.L for each paragraph. "Knowledge and understanding can be shown by including factual information from your case studies and examples. If you also explain key ideas you can get up to 6 marks out of 9" — the evaluation is the discriminator for the top three.

D1

What is the difference between a food chain and a food web? (2)

Show answer

A food chain shows the flow of energy in a single line, from a producer through to a tertiary consumer 1

A food web shows a collection of food chains and how they are interlinked, so one organism may appear in several chains and be eaten by more than one predator 1

The 2023 paper asked this for 1 mark, so one half of the contrast would have scored there; at 2 marks you need both sides stated.

D2

For a small-scale ecosystem that you have studied, outline the link between producers and consumers. (3)

Show answer

In the pond ecosystem I studied, the producers are the algae, which absorb energy from sunlight by photosynthesis 1

The primary consumers are the midge larvae, which feed on the algae, so the energy fixed by the algae is passed on to them 1

That energy then passes up to the secondary consumer, a fish such as perch, and the tertiary consumer, the heron — so without the producers none of the consumers could be supported 1

The 2023 version of this was 2 marks. Either way, the organisms have to be named: "producers are eaten by consumers" is the question restated, not answered.

D3

Explain why nutrient cycling is very rapid in the tropical rainforest. (4)

Show answer

Temperatures are high and constant, typically above 25–35°C, and rainfall is heavy every month, which provides ideal warm, moist conditions for decomposers such as bacteria and fungi 1

This means litter — leaves, twigs and dead animals — is broken down in weeks rather than years, converting dead matter into a form that can be re-used and releasing nutrients into the topsoil 1

Because the vegetation is evergreen and biodiversity is very high, there is a constant input of fallout into the litter layer all year, rather than a single autumn leaf drop 1

Shallow roots and wide buttress roots then absorb those nutrients rapidly before the heavy convectional rainfall can leach them out of the thin latosol, so the nutrients return to the biomass store almost immediately 1

D4

Explain how plants in the tropical rainforest are adapted to the climate. (4)

Show answer

Rainfall is heavy all year, so many leaves have drip tips — tapered points that allow water to run off the leaf, which avoids damage from the weight of heavy tropical rainfall and stops fungi growing on a permanently wet surface 1

Leaves are also thick and waxy, which protects the plant from the intense sunlight at these latitudes and sheds water 1

The heavy rainfall leaches the thin latosol, so trees have buttress roots — large roots on all sides of a shallowly rooted tree, which stop it falling over and gather nutrients released from the litter layer into the topsoil rather than from deeper layers 1

Because there is no cold season, bark is thin and smooth — no insulation is needed, and smoothness stops lianas and epiphytes colonising the trunk; lianas themselves are vines that climb tree trunks to reach sunlight in the canopy 1

D5

Explain how deforestation affects the soils of the tropical rainforest. (4)

Show answer

When vegetation is removed the soil is left exposed, with no canopy to intercept the heavy equatorial rainfall, so raindrops hit the bare surface directly 1

As a result the thin topsoil is rapidly eroded, and because the topsoil is the most fertile layer, little vegetation will grow afterwards 1

The nutrient cycle is also destroyed: with no leaf fall there is no litter layer and therefore no decay pathway, so the one real source of nutrients has been removed and the remaining soil is leached and becomes infertile — this can lead towards desertification 1

The eroded soil is then deposited on river beds, which raises the channel and leads to flooding downstream 1

D6

Explain how two strategies can be used to manage tropical rainforests sustainably. (4)

Show answer

Selective logging means cutting out only trees which are mature or inferior, to encourage the growth of the remaining trees 1; because the canopy and the younger trees are left standing, the litter layer and nutrient cycle continue and the soil is not exposed, so the forest regenerates and timber income continues indefinitely 1

Ecotourism is responsible travel to natural areas in small groups, with minimal impact on the local ecosystem 1; it gives local people an income that depends on the forest surviving rather than being cleared, so conservation becomes the profitable option, and it usually includes education which spreads that attitude further 1

Also acceptable, developed the same way: conservation and education, international agreements about tropical hardwoods and endangered species, debt reduction by HICs in return for protection, and the creation of protected areas or reserves.

D9

"Tropical rainforests are of value to people and the environment." Discuss this statement. (6)

Show answer

Level marked. The real 2025 6-marker, set on a figure listing medicines and research products, home to indigenous people, maintains the water cycle, and carbon sink. Plan:

Value to people — livelihoods and homes. Rainforests are home to indigenous peoples who have lived in the Amazon for thousands of years by shifting cultivation, hunting and gathering, and who lose ancestral lands when the forest is cleared 1

Value to people — medicine and resources. They supply medicines and other research products, including drugs used against diseases such as cancer, as well as timber, food and tourism income — and over 50% of the Earth's plant and animal species are the pool those discoveries come from 1

Value to the environment — carbon. The forest is a carbon sink: standing trees capture CO₂ through photosynthesis, and clearance released 105 gigatonnes of CO₂ between 2001 and 2019, so the forest's value is measured in avoided climate change 1

Value to the environment — water and soil. It maintains the water cycle: the canopy intercepts rainfall and transpires moisture back, generating the convectional rainfall the region depends on, and tree cover protects the thin latosol from the erosion and flooding that follow clearance 1

Value to the environment — biodiversity. Rainforests hold more than two-thirds of the world's plant species and half its animal species, concentrated in a layered structure where the canopy alone contains over 50% of rainforest wildlife 1

Judgement. The statement is true to a great extent, but the two kinds of value pull against each other: the value to some people lies in clearing the forest for ranching and mining, while the value to the environment and to indigenous people lies in leaving it standing — which is precisely why sustainable strategies such as ecotourism and debt reduction matter 1

D10

Explain why there are such high levels of biodiversity in the tropical rainforest, and outline the threats to it. (6)

Show answer

Level marked. Plan:

Climate. Temperatures are high and constant, typically above 25–35°C, with heavy rainfall every month, so there is no growing season to interrupt plant growth and vegetation grows densely all year 1

Structure creates niches. That growth produces five distinct layers — emergents, canopy, under canopy, shrub layer and ground layer — each with different light, humidity and food, so a very large number of different habitats exists in a small area; the canopy alone holds over 50% of rainforest wildlife 1

Scale of the result. Consequently rainforests contain more than two-thirds of the world's plant species and half its animal species — the 2023 paper's fact file puts it at more than 50% of all species on Earth 1

Threat — habitat loss. Deforestation removes those habitats directly: 80% of Amazon clearance is cattle ranching, 10% other commercial agriculture, and 20% of the Amazon has already been destroyed 1

Threat — the supporting systems. Clearance also destroys the nutrient cycle and exposes the thin latosol to erosion, so the forest cannot regrow; and mining pollutes water, with over 50 000 hectares used for gold mining 1

Why it matters. Loss is effectively irreversible: species become endangered then extinct, and medicinal plants used to fight diseases such as cancer are lost before they are even identified 1

D12

Level marked across AO1, AO2 and AO3. This is a should question, so the judgement has to be argued rather than described. Plan:

Open by naming the case study and the tension. In the Amazon, over 40% of global tropical deforestation occurs in Brazil, an NEE that needs economic growth — so the question is whether protection and development can coexist 1

Paragraph 1 — the case for protection: the environment. Deforestation released 105 gigatonnes of CO₂ between 2001 and 2019, and the standing forest is a carbon sink and maintains the water cycle through interception and transpiration 1

Rainforests hold more than two-thirds of the world's plant species and half its animal species, and 20% of the Amazon has already been destroyed. This argument is strong because the losses are global and permanent, whereas economic gains are national and temporary 1

Paragraph 2 — the case for protection: people. Indigenous people lose ancestral lands they have occupied for thousands of years, and medicinal plants used against diseases such as cancer are lost before they are identified 1

Clearance is also self-defeating economically: soil erosion and the destruction of the nutrient cycle mean cleared land loses fertility, so ranching must keep moving on — development that consumes its own resource base 1

Paragraph 3 — the case against: development is needed. Soy covers 25 million hectares with half exported; the Belo Monte Dam is the 4th largest HEP project in the world; bauxite, iron ore, manganese, gold and diamonds are mined in tropical forests. These bring jobs, export earnings, electricity and tax revenue to a country that needs them, and the Amazon's population grew from 4.7 million to 13.7 million between 1980 and 2000, so those people need livelihoods 1

It is also arguably unjust for HICs that cleared their own forests centuries ago to demand that an NEE forgoes the same development — which is exactly why debt reduction exists: countries are relieved of some of their debt in return for protecting their rainforests 1

The synthesis. The real choice is not protection versus development but which development: selective logging, replanting, ecotourism, protected areas, conservation and education, and international agreements on hardwoods all generate value from a forest left standing 1
