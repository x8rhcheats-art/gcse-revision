Measuring development — the indicators and why they mislead

Spec 3.2.2.1 · CEW Revision Workbook pp. 7–9; AQA List of Key Terms

  • GNI — total national income divided by population, including income earned from investments overseas.
  • HDI is the composite — GDP per capita plus life expectancy plus adult literacy, combined into one index.
  • Every measure is an average — so it hides the regional and social inequality inside a country.
More detail

Development is the progress of a country in terms of economic growth, the use of technology and human welfare. Measures split into economic (GNI, employment structure) and social (health, education, water).

MeasureWhat it isLimitation

Birth rateBirths in a year per 1,000 of the populationAffected by culture and religion as well as development; can fall for reasons unrelated to wealth
Death rateDeaths in a year per 1,000 of the populationMisleading on its own: an HIC with many elderly people can have a higher death rate than an LIC with a young population

The overarching limitation, which earns the mark on its own: economic and social measures are averages for the whole country, so they conceal differences between regions, between urban and rural areas and between rich and poor. Nigeria is the worked example — GDP per head is US$3,617 in the south against US$292 in the poorest region, so a single national figure describes nobody.

Nigeria against the UK, from the school's booklet: infant mortality 94 per 1,000 live births against 5; life expectancy 54 against 81; internet users 47% against 93%; HDI rank 164th against 13th.

  • Development defined — the progress of a country in economic growth, the use of technology and human welfare.
  • Three groups — LICs, NEEs and HICs, separated by GNI per head and by the characteristics that go with it.
  • Social measures — birth rate, death rate, infant mortality, life expectancy, people per doctor, literacy, access to safe water.
  • Every single measure is an average — so it hides regional and social inequality inside a country, which is the limitation examiners want.

The Demographic Transition Model

Spec 3.2.2.1 · CEW Revision Workbook pp. 11–12; 2019 Q02.4 and 2022 Q02.9

  • Stage 2 early expanding — death rate falls (clean water, food, medicine) while births stay high, so population rises fast.
  • Stage 3 late expanding — birth rate falls fast (contraception, women in work, lower infant mortality); population still rising.
  • Stage 4 low fluctuating — both rates low, population high and stable; most HICs, including the UK.
  • Stage 5 natural decrease — death rate slightly exceeds birth rate, so population falls and ages; Japan is the example.
More detail

The Demographic Transition Model shows how populations should change over time in terms of their birth rates, death rates and total population size. It is a model, so it describes the European experience and does not fit every country — a point worth making if you are asked to evaluate it.

StageBirth and death ratesLinks to developmentExample
1 High fluctuatingBoth high and variable; population low and stableSubsistence farming, no healthcare, disease and famine; children needed to workRemote tribal groups
2 Early expandingDeath rate falls sharply; birth rate stays high; population rises rapidlyClean water, better food supply and basic medicine cut deaths, but attitudes to family size have not yet changedNigeria, Afghanistan
3 Late expandingBirth rate falls rapidly; death rate low; population still rising but more slowlyContraception available, women enter paid work, infant mortality falls so fewer children are needed, education costs moneyBrazil, Mexico, India
4 Low fluctuatingBoth low; population high and stableIndustrial and post-industrial, good healthcare, small families the normUK, USA
5 Natural decreaseDeath rate slightly above birth rate; population falls and agesVery high living costs, career priorities, very long life expectancy, so the elderly outnumber birthsJapan, Germany, Italy

The Japan 3-marker (2019). "Japan is in stage 5 of the DTM and is a highly developed country. Explain this statement." The chain: Japan has 28% over 65 and only 12% under 15, so deaths exceed births and the population is falling (1); this is possible only because healthcare is excellent and life expectancy very high, which requires wealth (1); and the low birth rate reflects expensive housing, women in long careers and very low infant mortality, all features of a highly developed economy (1).

The Stage 3 economic benefits 3-marker (2022). In Stage 3 the birth rate falls while the large cohort born in Stage 2 reaches working age, so the working-age population grows relative to dependents (1); fewer children per family means women can enter paid employment, increasing the workforce and household income (1); and with fewer dependents, more income can be saved, invested or taxed, funding infrastructure and education — the demographic dividend (1).

  • What it models — how birth rate, death rate and total population change as a country develops, in five stages.
  • Stage 1 high fluctuating — high birth and death rates, so population stays low and stable; almost no country now.
  • Stage 3 late expanding — birth rate falls rapidly (contraception, women in work, lower infant mortality); population still rising.

The population structures AQA expects you to recognise

  • Mexico, 2020 — 45% under 15, 4% over 65. A wide-based pyramid: Stage 2–3, a youthful population with a high dependency ratio of children.
  • Japan, 2020 — 12% under 15, 28% over 65. Top-heavy: Stage 5, an ageing population with a shrinking workforce.
  • Nigeria — life expectancy 54, infant mortality 94 per 1,000: Stage 2 moving into Stage 3.
  • The UK — Stage 4, with rural areas such as the Outer Hebrides locally in Stage 5 through out-migration of the young.

Causes of uneven development

Spec 3.2.2.2 · CEW Revision Workbook pp. 14–15; Revision Mat — Development Gap

  • Three families of cause — physical, economic and historical. Name the family, then the mechanism.
  • Physical — extreme weather and climate, difficult relief, being landlocked, tropical disease, and water shortage.
  • Economic — poverty traps, and trade: LICs export low-value primary goods and import high-value manufactures.
  • Historical — colonialism, slavery, and borders drawn across ethnic groups, which left unstable states and extractive economies.
More detail

Physical causes. Weather and climate — extremes of drought and flood destroy harvests, so farmers have nothing to sell and no surplus to invest; a reliable growing season is the foundation of a farming economy. Relief — steep, mountainous land is hard to farm and expensive to build roads and railways across, so interior regions stay cut off. Landlocked countries — with no coastline there is no port, so trade must cross a neighbour's territory, adding cost and political risk. Tropical environments — disease, including malaria, reduces the working population and diverts spending into healthcare. Water shortages — without reliable water, irrigation, industry and sanitation are all limited.

Economic causes. Poverty is self-reinforcing: low incomes mean low tax revenue, so the government cannot fund the schools, clinics and infrastructure that would raise incomes. Trade is the bigger structural cause — LICs typically export primary products (raw materials and crops) whose prices are low and volatile, and import manufactured goods whose prices are high. Because raw materials are exported before being refined, the profit from processing them accrues elsewhere. Nigeria is the example: oil is 98% of export earnings but only 14% of GDP, and the country still imports chemicals and machinery.

Historical causes. Colonisation organised economies around extracting raw materials for the colonial power rather than developing industry; the slave trade removed millions of working-age people; and borders drawn by colonial powers cut across ethnic and religious groups, leaving states with internal tensions. Nigeria became independent of the UK only in 1960, is multi-ethnic with the Yoruba at 21% of the population, and has a Christian south and Muslim north — and "economic inequality and regional differences have created ethnic tensions".

Consequences. Wealth — 34% of global wealth is held in North America, which has a small fraction of the world's people. Health — 400,000 people died of malaria in 2019, almost all in LICs; Nigeria's infant mortality is 94 per 1,000 against the UK's 5. International migration — 590,000 people have fled Syria, where 80% of the remaining population live below the poverty line and unemployment is 55%.

  • Landlocked is the clearest — no coast means no port, so every import and export pays to cross a neighbour's territory.
  • Consequences — disparities in wealth and health, and international migration.

✓ "A tropical climate supports mosquitoes carrying malaria, which killed 400,000 people in 2019; illness removes adults from work, so output and tax revenue fall and the money that might have been invested in development is spent on treatment instead."

Consequences of uneven development — and international migration

Spec 3.2.2.2 · Case Study Summary Booklet quiz facts; 2024 Q02.4

  • Wealth — 34% of global wealth sits in North America, so investment concentrates where money already is.
  • Health — 400,000 malaria deaths in 2019; Nigeria's infant mortality is 94/1000 against the UK's 5.
  • Economic migration — the pull of higher wages, jobs, healthcare and education in HICs; the push of unemployment and poverty.
  • Forced migration — conflict and environmental disaster; 590,000 have fled Syria, 80% of those left in poverty.
More detail

The 2024 6-marker — "Suggest how international migration can be a consequence of uneven development" — wants the mechanism, in both directions.

Why uneven development causes migration. Where development is low, there are few well-paid jobs, healthcare is limited, schooling is poor and basic services are unreliable; where it is high, wages, services and security are all better. People therefore move from LICs towards NEEs and HICs. Conflict, which is itself often a consequence of poverty and competition for resources, forces far larger movements: 590,000 have fled Syria, where 80% of the remaining population lives below the poverty line and 55% are unemployed.

Why it also widens the gap. Migrants are predominantly young, healthy and often the better-educated, so the source country loses exactly the workers it needs — the brain drain. Against that, remittances sent home can exceed the value of official aid, and returning migrants bring skills and capital.

The receiving-country side, worth a sentence for balance: London's migrant streams were dominated by the former British Empire and then Eastern Europe, 37% of Londoners were born outside the UK, and migrants contributed £20 billion to UK public finances between 2000 and 2011 — while also increasing competition for housing, school places and jobs.

  • Migration is the third consequence — people move from where opportunity is absent to where it exists.
  • Forced migration — conflict and environmental disaster; 590,000 have fled Syria, with 80% of those left below the poverty line.
  • It feeds back — remittances help the source country, but the loss of skilled workers (brain drain) widens the gap further.

Reducing the development gap — the eight strategies

Spec 3.2.2.3 · Revision Mat — Development Gap; Case Study Summary Booklet quiz facts

  • Learn all eight — investment, industrial development, tourism, aid, intermediate technology, Fairtrade, debt relief, microfinance.
  • The common mechanism — each creates employment, which raises incomes, which raises tax revenue, which funds services.
  • Fairtrade — a guaranteed fair price plus a community premium; 90% of eastern Uganda's coffee farmers joined Gumutindo.
  • Microfinance — very small loans to start a business; the Grameen Bank has lent $12.5 billion.
More detail
  • Fairtrade — a guaranteed fair price plus a premium invested in the community; 90% of small coffee farmers in eastern Uganda joined the Gumutindo co-op.
  • Debt relief is cancellation, not a loan — $75 billion was written off across 19 HIPCs, freeing money for roads and healthcare.
  • Microfinance — very small loans to start a business; the Grameen Bank has lent $12.5 billion, including $200 to a woman to buy a phone.
  • Aid — the UK gives 0.5% of GNI, including about £300 million a year to Pakistan, where 35% live in poverty.

The eight strategies, with the figures that make them specific

  • Investment. Countries, organisations such as the World Bank, and TNCs invest in LICs to increase their own profits. The investment improves infrastructure (road networks, airports), services (water, sanitation, electricity), dams and reservoirs for hydro-electric power, and industrial developments.
  • Industrial development. Brings employment in construction, manufacturing and services. Increased individual wealth leads to improvements in health, education and service provision through the payment of more taxes — that tax link is the mechanism, and it is what the mark schemes credit.
  • Tourism. See Jamaica below. The engine is the multiplier effect: visitor spending → jobs and tax → public spending → better infrastructure → more visitors.
  • Aid. Money, goods and services given by one government, or by a multilateral institution such as the World Bank or IMF, to improve another country's quality of life and economy. Long-term aid funds development projects — sanitation, water supply, education; short-term aid responds to natural disasters. The UK gives 0.5% of GNI as aid, including about £300 million a year to Pakistan, where 35% of the population lives in poverty. Oxfam runs Goat Aid.
  • Intermediate technology. Simple, easily learned and easily maintained technology serving local needs — the point is that it can be repaired locally without imported parts or expertise. A 15 m dam built at Adis Nifas provides irrigation; the Chambamontera micro-hydro scheme in Peru is the same idea applied to energy.
  • Fairtrade. Producers in LICs are given a better price for what they grow — usually cocoa, coffee or cotton — plus a premium invested in the local community, and fair wages for the workforce. 90% of small coffee farmers in eastern Uganda have joined the Gumutindo Co-operative. The better price improves income and reduces exploitation.
  • Debt relief. In the 1970s and 1980s many countries borrowed heavily for large-scale development projects and then fell into debt repaying loans at high interest. Debt relief cancels money owed, so the repayments can be redirected into development — road building, health care, education. Total debt relief across the 19 Heavily Indebted Poor Countries was $75 billion.
  • Microfinance loans. Very small loans to individuals or community groups to start a small business. If the business succeeds it creates jobs and raises incomes. The Grameen Bank has lent $12.5 billion in total and lends around $200 to a woman to buy a phone she can rent out.

✗ "Debt relief gives countries money to develop." — the 2023 report's exact complaint: candidates conflated debt relief with loans.
✓ "Debt relief cancels debt that already exists, so the money a government was spending on interest repayments — across the 19 HIPCs, $75 billion of debt was written off — can instead be spent on roads, clinics and schools."

Tourism in Jamaica — the facts and figures

  • Economy. In 2019 tourism contributed 35% of Jamaica's GDP, with 4 million visitors a year. It generates $2 billion each year and over 50% of the country's total foreign exchange earnings. Taxes paid to the government allow more public spending — the multiplier effect.
  • Growth. Visitor arrivals grew almost exponentially between 1994 and 2016. Total visitor expenditure rose from US$128,706,000 in 1975 to US$2,608,798,000 in 2016. Arrivals still rose in 2009, but more slowly, because the recession cut disposable income — a reminder that tourism is vulnerable to events elsewhere.
  • Employment. Tourism is the main source of employment, with over 200,000 people employed directly and indirectly — the second largest employer in the country.
  • Infrastructure. New port and cruise liner facilities at Falmouth; a new water treatment plant at Logwood, which benefits residents as well as hotels.
  • Quality of life. Local farmers benefit by supplying hotels, which keeps the money in the Jamaican economy rather than leaking abroad.
  • Environment. The Negril Marine Nature Park attracts many tourists, so protecting the reef and marine life becomes economically worthwhile.
  • The limits. Wealthier parts such as Montego Bay have benefited far more than others; pockets of poverty still exist; and infrastructure such as roads came much later because the authorities prioritised port and airport investment — so the benefit to ordinary Jamaicans lagged behind the benefit to the tourist industry.

  • Location. West Africa, sharing borders with Benin, Niger, Chad and Cameroon; to the south it borders the Gulf of Guinea, part of the Atlantic Ocean.
  • Global importance. Nigeria's economy and the role of Shell

  • Main exports. Oil, natural gas, rubber, cocoa and cotton. Oil accounts for 14% of GDP and 98% of export earnings, so the national income rises and falls with the world oil price.
  • The changing structure. Employment in agriculture has fallen by 40%, through mechanisation and better pay in other sectors. Manufacturing now accounts for 27% of the economy and is the fastest growing sector; the service sector is 53%, with considerable growth in IT.
  • How manufacturing stimulates development. It creates better-paid, more secure work than subsistence farming; wages are spent locally so other businesses grow (the multiplier effect); companies and workers pay tax, giving the government money for schools, clinics and infrastructure; processing raw materials in Nigeria rather than exporting them raw keeps the value added in the country; and it reduces reliance on imported manufactured goods such as chemicals and machinery.
  • TNCs in Nigeria. Around 40 transnational corporations operate in the country, and 920 Chinese companies. Countries such as the USA, China and South Africa have invested heavily.
  • Shell — the case study TNC. An Anglo-Dutch oil TNC, in Nigeria since 1958 with the discovery of oil and controversial extraction operations in the Niger Delta.
  • The advantages of Shell. $250,000 spent on healthcare; 3,000 scholarships to local schools; 90% of contracts awarded to local companies (AQA's 2025 mark scheme gives 91%, with direct employment of 65,000 Nigerians and 250,000 more indirectly, while an updated textbook figure is 2,700 direct employees and 9,000 contractors, 97% of them Nigerian). Shell makes significant contributions to Nigeria's tax revenue, increasing national wealth.
  • The disadvantages of Shell. Less tax is paid because the company is registered in Bermuda. Oil spills have caused water pollution, with two massive spills at Bodo in Ogoniland in the Niger Delta; 42,000 residents took out a lawsuit against Shell; contamination in Ogoniland is 1,000 times higher than elsewhere; gas flaring continues, adding greenhouse gases and causing respiratory problems. Oil wealth has provoked armed conflict and groups such as the Niger Delta Avengers, costing lives and government revenue.
  • The general case against TNCs. Profits leave the country and benefit shareholders, often in HICs; wages are lower than in the TNC's home country; environmental damage is caused without responsibility for cleaning it up; a TNC can withdraw if circumstances change; powerful TNCs can exert pressure on governments; and raw materials are exported before being refined, which reduces the profit retained in LICs and NEEs.

Nigeria's changing relationships and its aid

  • Trade. Nigeria was originally part of the British Empire, so most political and economic links ran to the UK and other Empire members. Since independence in 1960 it has kept strong links with the UK but now conducts most of its trade with some of the world's largest economies — the EU, USA and India — and links with China are growing. Oil became its main commodity export after independence, but it still imports manufactured goods such as chemicals and machinery.
  • Political links. Nigeria plays a leading political role in Africa, is a member of OPEC and ECOWAS, and has been a major contributor to UN peacekeeping since the 1960s.
  • Why aid is needed. 60% of the population — about 100 million people — live on less than US$1 a day, and over 50% live in poverty.
  • How much. Nigeria receives 6% of the aid given to African countries, which represents only 0.8% of its annual GNI — so aid is a small part of its economy compared with oil.
  • Named examples. Christian Aid — 360,000 people received life-saving health care and 1,000 local people were trained. The UK Department for International Development funded a health and HIV programme. The IMF approved £3.4 billion post-Covid for economic redevelopment.

Nigeria — the environmental cost, and the gains in quality of life

  • Deforestation. 96% of Nigeria's forests have been destroyed through logging, agriculture and urban expansion, removing habitat and the carbon sink.
  • Mining and oil. Around 40 oil spills; contamination in Ogoniland is 1,000 times higher than elsewhere; 42,000 residents took out a lawsuit against Shell; oil spills have destroyed aquatic ecosystems and ruined fishermen's livelihoods.   • Waste. 10,000 tonnes a day; lack of sanitation and inadequate waste disposal cause land and water pollution; electronic waste is imported from Europe and dismantled unsafely.
  • Urban growth. Rapid urban growth has caused urban sprawl, swallowing up countryside, while emissions from cars and industry contribute to climate change.
  • The gains. Between 2015 and 2019 life expectancy increased by 9 years. Between 1990 and 2019 access to safe water rose from 46% to 85%. Manufacturing and services now provide better-paid, more secure employment than subsistence agriculture.

  • The future challenges. Overcoming socio-economic inequality; tackling government corruption so wealth reaches everyone; addressing soil erosion, water pollution, desertification and oil spills; maximising oil revenue and investing it in other sectors; handling ethnic and religious conflict sensitively; providing basic sanitation for all; and further investment in healthcare and education.

De-industrialisation and globalisation in the UK

Spec 3.2.2.5 · Revision Mat — UK Changing Economy; 2022 Q02.3, 2024 Q02.8

  • De-industrialisation — the reduction of industrial activity or capacity, especially heavy industry and manufacturing.
  • What declined — coal mining, shipbuilding and steel manufacturing, as raw materials ran out and markets were lost.
  • Three causes — mechanisation replacing workers, exhaustion of raw materials, and competition from NEEs with lower wages.
More detail

De-industrialisation is the reduction of industrial activity or capacity in a region or economy, especially of heavy industry or manufacturing industry. AQA's own definition adds the causes: "due to exhaustion of raw materials, loss of markets and competition from NEEs".

How the UK's employment structure changed in the 20th century. A decrease in the number of people working in manufacturing, as mechanisation — the introduction of robots and automated plant — replaced jobs, and as competition from abroad increased. A dramatic increase in the service sector, through the introduction of public services, the growth of financial services, and an increase in leisure time and disposable income. And since the 1980s, the research and development sector has become increasingly important.

What de-industrialisation involved in practice: the closure of coal mines and shipyards; a negative multiplier effect, as the smaller businesses that supplied and supported heavy industry closed too, affecting thousands more people; huge job losses and rising unemployment in North East England as factories and industrial sites closed; and a skills problem — many of those employed in heavy industries struggled to find new jobs with the skills they had.

Globalisation's six effects on the UK, from the school's revision mat:

  • Migration — migrants fill jobs where the UK has a shortage of skilled workers, such as healthcare and construction.
  • Less manufacturing — fewer goods are made in the UK because they can be imported more cheaply from countries such as China where wages are lower.
  • Inequality — the gap between the best-paid and lowest-paid jobs is increasing.
  • Outsourcing — jobs are moved to other countries where wages are lower.
  • Economic growth — in most years the UK economy grows by 1–2%, mainly through trade with other countries.
  • Foreign investment — foreign companies invest in the UK, bringing new ways of working and new technology; and cheaper goods and services follow from more competitive wages and production.

The environmental legacy of heavy industry: coal mining created spoil heaps, vast mounds of waste removed during extraction; burning coal to generate electricity caused considerable air pollution in cities across the UK; and toxic waste materials from heavy industry polluted land and water supplies.

  • The negative multiplier — suppliers and support businesses closed too, so job losses spread; the North East was hit hardest.
  • Globalisation cuts both ways — cheaper goods, foreign investment and 1–2% growth a year, against outsourcing and widening inequality.
  • Skills mismatch — workers from heavy industry struggled to find new jobs with the skills they had.

  • What it is. A 2.5 km² limestone quarry in Somerset — a working extractive industry in a rural area.
  • Economic value. It contributes £15 million a year to the local economy.
  • Environmental impacts. Quarrying removes the landscape itself and generates dust, noise and lorry traffic; this is the reason the spec pairs it with "how modern industrial development can be more environmentally sustainable".

The post-industrial economy — the figures

  • Services. 80% of the UK economy comes from the tertiary sector; 1.7% comes from research.
  • Information technology. 1.3 million people are employed in IT; home-working and self-employment have grown because of the internet; the UK is viewed as one of the top IT countries in the world and receives overseas investment as a result; technological developments have driven specialist manufacturing, services and research; information can be accessed anywhere on mobile devices. The turnover of digital technology companies grew by 4.5% between 2016 and 2017 against 1.7% growth in UK GDP — 2.6 times faster than the overall economy; London-based technology businesses raised £9 billion between 2015 and 2018; and the UK accounts for 5% of all high-growth technology workers employed globally.
  • Science parks. Rural change, infrastructure, the north–south divide and the UK in the wider world

Spec 3.2.2.5 · Revision Mat — UK Changing Economy; Case Study Summary Booklet quiz facts

  • Rural change — growth areas gain services and jobs; the Outer Hebrides has lost 50% of its population since 1901.
  • Infrastructure — road, rail, ports and airports; A303 £2bn, Crossrail £19bn, Liverpool2 £400m and 5,000 jobs.
  • The north–south divide — longer life expectancy, higher incomes and house prices in the south-east; higher unemployment in the north.
  • Closing it — devolution, HS2, northern business parks, 43 Enterprise Zones, and LEPs — one created 11,000 jobs.
More detail

Rural areas with population growth (North Somerset, Cambridgeshire): an increased population leads to higher demand for goods and services, which helps secure the future of rural shops, schools and businesses; it provides balance to rural–urban migration, particularly as young people move away in search of better opportunities; new people are more likely to invest in new local businesses; and new developments provide jobs.

Rural areas with population decline (the Outer Hebrides, Mid Wales): the population of the Outer Hebrides has fallen by 50% since 1901. The 2025 mark scheme sets out the mechanism: younger people leave in search of opportunities, leaving a higher proportion of older residents, which closes schools and makes the community less varied, and creates social care and health pressures; economically, the cost of providing services rises because smaller populations reduce economies of scale, and a reduced, less varied population makes businesses less viable — which is why the figure described rural shops as a "lifeline".

Transport and infrastructure. The four aspects being developed are the road network, rail network, ports and airports. The widening project on the A303 will cost £2 billion; Crossrail cost £19 billion to build; Liverpool2 cost £400 million and created 5,000 jobs; the new runway at Heathrow is expected to cost £19 billion, boost the economy by £61 billion and create 75,000 new jobs.

The north–south divide is the term used to describe the social, economic and cultural disparities between London and the south-east of England and the rest of the UK. People living in the south-east typically have a longer life expectancy, higher income and better standard of living; house prices are higher because demand is higher; and rates of unemployment are higher in the north as regions continue to adjust to de-industrialisation. AQA's own definition adds "clear differences in health conditions, house prices, earnings, and political influence".

Strategies to reduce it: devolution of powers to regional authorities; HS2 and other transport links; business and science parks in the north of England; 43 Enterprise Zones across the UK offering tax breaks and simplified planning; and Local Enterprise Partnerships — one received £1 billion of investment and created 11,000 jobs.

The UK in the wider world. Links run through trade, culture, transport and electronic communication, and through two institutions. The European Union was formed to reduce trade barriers and increase cooperation, with free movement of workers between member states — the UK's departure changed the terms of that relationship. The Commonwealth is a voluntary association of 53 independent and equal sovereign states, mostly former territories of the British Empire, home to 2.2 billion citizens, united by language, history, culture and shared values rather than legal obligation. The 2022 6-marker — "suggest how changing economic and political links may affect the UK's place in the wider world" — set the two against each other explicitly: does leaving the EU mean taking back control of money, laws and borders, or can the Commonwealth provide the trading future instead?

  • Rural decline areas — the Outer Hebrides has lost 50% of its population since 1901, closing schools and shops.
  • Heathrow's third runway — £19bn to build, a £61bn economic boost and 75,000 new jobs.
  • Closing it — devolution, HS2, northern business parks, 43 Enterprise Zones, and LEPs — one created 11,000 jobs from £1bn.

The 9-mark frame

Section B's 9-marker is nearly always one of two shapes: "evaluate the impacts of X on a host country / on quality of life" (Nigeria) or "explain this statement about the UK economy" (transport, regional difference, post-industrial change). Both take the same structure, and the school's rule is to evaluate inside every paragraph rather than saving it for the end.

  1. One-sentence intro naming the case study and your line. "Nigeria, the largest economy in Africa and the world's 13th largest oil producer, has seen economic development improve quality of life substantially — life expectancy rose nine years between 2015 and 2019 — but at a severe environmental cost." Name the place in the first sentence; the examiners' recurring complaint is a "geography of anywhere".
  2. Paragraph 1 — the positive case, P.E.E.L. Point, Explanation of the mechanism, Evidence (the figure), Link back to the question. Then evaluate it: how large, how widely shared, how durable. "Shell awards around 90% of contracts to Nigerian companies, which means the money stays in the national economy and creates the multiplier effect, although the company is registered in Bermuda so the tax benefit is smaller than it appears."
  3. Paragraph 2 — the negative case, same shape. For a TNC or quality-of-life question this is the environmental and distributional cost: Bodo, Ogoniland's 1,000-times contamination, the 42,000 claimants, gas flaring, 96% of the forests. Say which of the two paragraphs is stronger, and why.
  4. Paragraph 3 — the complication. The paragraph that reaches Level 3, because it shows the two sides are connected rather than opposed: oil revenue funded the water supply that rose from 46% to 85%, and the same industry caused the spills. Or, for a UK question: transport investment raises productivity in the places it reaches, which is exactly why it can widen the north–south divide.
  5. A conclusion that decides. Use the ladder — definitely / mostly / partly / slightly / not at all — rank the factors, and tie it to the exact words of the question. For Nigeria, the Malaysia and Singapore comparison is the strongest closing evidence there is.

Sentence starters that earn the AO3 marks:

  • "Figure 10 shows that…, which means…, and therefore…"
  • "This is a significant advantage because…, as a result…"
  • "However, this benefit is limited because…, whereas the cost…"
  • "The two are connected: the same oil revenue that funded… also caused…"
  • "The most significant factor is…, because it affects… whereas… affects only…"
  • "Overall, I mostly agree, because… outweighs…"

03Understanding it

U1

Why can an HIC have a higher death rate than an LIC?

Show answer

Because the death rate is a crude number of deaths per 1,000 people, and it depends on the age structure of the population, not on how good healthcare is.

An HIC in Stage 4 or 5 has a very large elderly population — Japan has 28% over 65 — and elderly people die at a higher rate, so the national figure rises even though life expectancy is the highest in the world. An LIC in Stage 2 has a very young population — Mexico had 45% under 15 in 2020 — and young people die at a low rate, so the national figure stays low.

The exam point: this is why death rate alone is a poor measure of development, and it is exactly the "give a reason the measure is inaccurate" answer the 2023 report says was rare. Infant mortality is a far better health indicator, because it isolates the group most sensitive to healthcare quality — Nigeria's 94 per 1,000 against the UK's 5.

U2

Why is falling birth rate in Stage 3 an economic benefit rather than a problem?

Show answer

Because of what happens to the dependency ratio. In Stage 2 the death rate fell while births stayed high, so a very large cohort of children was born. In Stage 3 those children reach working age at the same time as the birth rate falls, so the country has an unusually large working-age population relative to dependents — the demographic dividend.

Three consequences follow. More workers means more output and more tax revenue. Fewer children per family means women can enter paid employment, enlarging the workforce again and raising household income. And with fewer dependents to support, households and governments can save and invest rather than spend everything on subsistence.

The complication worth adding: the dividend is temporary. The same large cohort eventually becomes a large elderly cohort, which is how Stage 4 turns into Stage 5 — Japan's 28% over 65 is the dividend coming due.

U3

Nigeria produces 2.2% of the world's oil and has the largest economy in Africa. Why is 60% of its population still in poverty?

Show answer

Three mechanisms, and the best answers use all three.

Over-dependence on one commodity. Oil is 98% of export earnings but only 14% of GDP, so national income rises and falls with the world oil price and very little of the economy actually produces oil. Oil extraction is capital-intensive, so it generates revenue without generating mass employment.

Where the profits go. TNC profits leave the country to shareholders, often in HICs; Shell is registered in Bermuda so pays less Nigerian tax; and raw materials are exported before being refined, so the value added in processing accrues abroad.

Corruption and regional inequality. The school's material names corruption as the single biggest obstacle — aid is "lost or not given to the right people", and the stated future priority is "tackling government corruption to ensure wealth reaches everyone". Wealth is also geographically concentrated: US$3,617 GDP per head in the south against US$292 in the poorest region, with urban areas taking the greater share of public services.

The clinching comparison: thirty years ago Nigeria was at a similar stage of development to Malaysia and Singapore, which have moved far ahead. Same starting point, different outcomes — so the problem is not a lack of resources.

U4

Debt relief and aid both transfer money to poorer countries. Why do geographers treat them as different strategies?

Show answer

Because of the direction of the money and who controls it.

Aid is a new transfer in: money, goods or services given by a donor government or by the World Bank or IMF. The donor usually decides what it funds, which is where the problems come from — donors may demand political influence over who benefits, aid can be diverted by corruption, and long-term reliance can create dependence. Nigeria's aid is also small relative to its economy: 6% of all African aid, but only 0.8% of Nigeria's GNI.

Debt relief stops an existing flow out: it cancels money already owed, so repayments that were leaving the country stay in it. $75 billion was written off across the 19 HIPCs. The recipient government then decides how to spend it, which is why the mat links it directly to "road building and health care".

The exam point: the 2023 report says candidates "conflat[ed] it with loans". Debt relief is the opposite of a loan. Say the word "cancel" and you have the mark.

U5

If tourism gives Jamaica 35% of its GDP, why has it not closed the development gap?

Show answer

Because of distribution, leakage and vulnerability.

Distribution. The benefits cluster geographically: wealthy areas such as Montego Bay have gained most, and "pockets of poverty still exist". Infrastructure that tourists use came first — ports and airports — and roads for residents came much later, so the sequence of investment favoured the industry over the population.

Leakage. Much tourist spending goes to foreign-owned hotels, airlines and cruise lines rather than staying in Jamaica. The counter-example is local farmers supplying hotels, which is exactly why that detail is worth quoting — it is the mechanism that reduces leakage.

Vulnerability. Tourism depends on disposable income in other countries. Arrivals still rose in 2009, but much more slowly, because the recession cut the money visitors had to spend — a single-industry economy inherits other countries' downturns.

The balanced judgement: tourism has reduced the gap substantially — $2 billion a year, 200,000 jobs, over 50% of foreign exchange earnings and genuine infrastructure such as the Logwood water treatment plant — but it has made Jamaica prosperous unevenly and precariously. "Partly" is the honest answer.

U6

Why did de-industrialisation cause so much more unemployment than the number of industrial jobs lost?

Show answer

Because of the negative multiplier effect. Heavy industry did not exist in isolation: a shipyard or coal mine bought from local suppliers, used local hauliers and engineers, and its wages were spent in local shops. When it closed, "many smaller businesses that supplied and supported heavy industries closed, a knock-on effect affecting thousands of people".

The second mechanism is the skills mismatch. Workers in coal, steel and shipbuilding had specialised manual skills with no equivalent in the growing sectors, and the school's material is explicit: "many of those employed in heavy industries struggled to find new jobs with the skills they have." The growth was in services, finance and later IT, often in a different region.

And it was geographically concentrated: "North East England has suffered huge job losses and a rise in unemployment as factories and industrial sites closed", which is one root of the north–south divide.

Use it like this: the same mechanism, in reverse, is what makes the positive multiplier the key idea in every development question — which is why the Docklands' 120,000 jobs and Nigeria's manufacturing growth both work through tax and local spending rather than wages alone.

U7

Transport investment is meant to spread prosperity. Why might it widen the north–south divide instead?

Show answer

Because transport improvements benefit the place that is already most productive. The Heathrow third runway would cost £19 billion, boost the economy by £61 billion and create 75,000 jobs — almost all of it in the south-east. Crossrail cost £19 billion and serves London alone. So the investment with the strongest business case tends to be the investment in the richest region, which is self-reinforcing.

A faster link can also work against the poorer end. If a city is two hours from London rather than four, a firm may serve it from London rather than opening an office there — so the link extends London's market instead of building a northern one. Businesses still locate where the skilled workers and universities already are.

The counter-case, which you need for balance: Liverpool2 cost £400 million and created 5,000 jobs in the north-west; there are 43 Enterprise Zones across the UK; one Local Enterprise Partnership turned £1 billion of investment into 11,000 jobs; and devolution moves decisions closer to the regions.

The judgement: infrastructure determines which places have an economic future, which is exactly what the 2024 statement claimed — but whether it narrows or widens the divide depends on where it is built, so the honest answer is that it does both.

U8

A science park and a limestone quarry are both "modern industrial development". Which is more environmentally sustainable?

Show answer

Less obvious than it looks, which is why the 2023 6-marker on this discriminated so well.

The science park looks sustainable — clean, quiet, no extraction, high-value output, and Nissan's comparable plant has 10 wind turbines (6.6 MW), 19,000 photovoltaic panels and a 22.4% cut in CO₂ since 2005. But the examiners' report says Level 3 was reached by students who considered "the carbon footprints of construction and employee transportation". Science parks sit on the edge of university cities on greenfield land with good road access, so they are built on undeveloped land and generate car commuting — Southampton Science Park is next to the M3 for exactly that reason.

The quarry looks unsustainable — Torr Quarry removes 2.5 km² of limestone permanently, with dust, noise and lorry traffic. But it is restored as it goes: 80 hectares landscaped, with the Somerset Wildlife Trust creating biodiverse-rich landscapes on the worked ground, and it returns £15 million a year to the local economy, which is part of the sustainability test too.

The judgement: both are partly sustainable, and for opposite reasons — the quarry's impact is severe but localised and actively reversed, while the science park's impact is diffuse and mostly ignored. Saying that, rather than picking a side, is what the command word wants.

04Exam drill

What each command word actually wants

Section B is Question 02 on Paper 2 — compulsory, around 30 marks, and the home of the paper's maths. The AQA command words have fixed meanings, taken here from the school's command word table and exam technique guide.

  • Describe — say what you see. Trends, extremities, data, anomalies. No reasons.
  • Explain — say why or how. Causes and processes, developed with because, this means that, as a result. Make explicit reference to the figure if there is one.
  • Suggest — a reasonable explanation you have worked out from the figure plus your own knowledge; a broad range of valid answers is accepted, so long as each is developed.
  • Outline — a brief but developed point; on a 2-marker, point + development, and on a 3-marker, a three-step chain.
  • Discuss — present key points about different sides of an argument. Open with one side, make multiple points to support it, make counter-arguments, finish with a clear conclusion that links back to the question. It does not have to be balanced.
  • Assess — a judgement of the size or significance of the problem. Which factor is most significant? How large a problem is this? State which factors are most to least important in the conclusion and justify the choice.
  • Evaluate — judge from evidence, weighing up both sides, and rank the arguments from most to least important or convincing. Use however and on the other hand.
  • To what extent — the same as evaluate, but the conclusion must land on the ladder: definitely, mostly, partly, slightly, not at all.
  • Maths. 10% of the paper's marks are mathematical. Mean, median, interquartile range, range, percentage change, best-fit lines, plotting and graph completion all appear. Read the rounding instruction — "to one decimal place", "to the nearest whole percentage" — because the 2022 and 2023 reports both say the commonest error was not rounding. Show your working: if the answer is wrong but the method is right you still get a mark.

Twelve questions in the real spread: two short, four 4-markers, four 6-markers and two 9-markers. The 6- and 9-mark answers are written as plans, because AQA marks them in levels — treat the mark points as a coverage target rather than a tick list.

D1

Outline the limitations of one social measure of development, such as access to clean water. (2)

Show answer

Access to clean water is recorded as a single national percentage, so it is an average that hides the difference between urban and rural areas and between rich and poor 1

It also says nothing about the quality or reliability of that water, or about the rest of development — Nigeria's figure rose from 46% to 85% between 1990 and 2019, yet urban areas still take a far greater share of public services and 60% of the population remains in poverty 1

The 2023 report's exact complaint: candidates said what the measure was not rather than giving a reason it is inaccurate. Give a mechanism — averages hide inequality — not an absence.

D2

Outline one or more reasons for the decline of traditional industries in the UK. (3)

Show answer

Mechanisation replaced workers: the introduction of robots and automated plant meant the same output needed far fewer people, so manufacturing employment fell 1

Raw materials were exhausted and markets were lost — the coal and iron ore that coal mining, steel and shipbuilding relied on became harder and more expensive to extract 1

Competition from NEEs undercut UK producers, because goods can be manufactured and imported far more cheaply from countries such as China where wages are lower, so UK factories and shipyards closed — with a negative multiplier effect as their suppliers closed too 1

D3

Explain how Fairtrade can reduce the development gap. (4)

Show answer

Fairtrade guarantees producers in LICs a fair, stable price for what they grow — usually cocoa, coffee or cotton — rather than leaving them exposed to a volatile world market price set elsewhere 1

A higher and more predictable income means farmers can plan: they can afford to send children to school, buy better seed and equipment, and survive a poor harvest without selling land, so the household escapes the poverty trap 1

Fairtrade also pays a premium that is invested in the local community, funding shared projects such as a well, a school or a clinic, which benefits people who are not themselves farmers 1

And it requires fair wages for the workforce, reducing exploitation; the scheme works best where farmers organise collectively — 90% of small coffee farmers in eastern Uganda have joined the Gumutindo Co-operative, which gives them the scale to negotiate and to export directly 1

D4

Suggest how debt relief can help reduce the development gap. (4)

Show answer

Many countries borrowed heavily in the 1970s and 1980s for large-scale development projects and then fell into debt repaying the loans at high rates of interest; debt relief cancels the money owed 1

This frees money that was leaving the country as interest payments — across the 19 Heavily Indebted Poor Countries, $75 billion of debt was written off 1

The government can then redirect that spending into development projects it chooses itself, such as road building, health care and education, rather than into servicing a loan 1

Those investments raise productivity and incomes, which increases tax revenue and allows further spending — so debt relief can start a positive cycle rather than a one-off transfer, and unlike aid it carries no donor conditions and creates no new dependence 1

✗ "Debt relief gives countries money to spend on development." — the 2023 report says students had "a poor understanding of debt relief, often conflating it with loans".
✓ Use the word cancel. Debt relief removes an existing outflow; it does not add an inflow.

D5

Explain how manufacturing industry can stimulate economic development in a LIC/NEE you have studied. (4)

Show answer

In Nigeria, manufacturing now accounts for 27% of the economy and is its fastest growing sector, and it provides better-paid, more secure employment than the subsistence agriculture it replaced — employment in agriculture has fallen by 40% 1

Higher wages are spent locally, so shops, transport and services grow alongside the factories: this is the multiplier effect, and it spreads the benefit to people who do not work in manufacturing 1

Companies and their workers pay tax, which gives the government revenue it can spend on schools, clinics, water and roads — Nigeria's access to safe water rose from 46% to 85% between 1990 and 2019 1

Manufacturing also keeps the value added inside Nigeria. At present oil is 98% of export earnings but only 14% of GDP because crude is exported before refining; processing raw materials domestically retains that profit and reduces the need to import manufactured chemicals and machinery 1

D6

Suggest how international migration can be a consequence of uneven development. (4)

Show answer

Uneven development means opportunity is concentrated: 34% of global wealth is held in North America, while in LICs wages are low, well-paid jobs are scarce, healthcare is limited and schooling is poor — so people move from where development is low towards where it is high 1

The pull is specific and measurable — higher wages, reliable healthcare and education, and security. London's migrant streams came from the former British Empire and then Eastern Europe, and 37% of Londoners were born outside the UK 1

Uneven development also produces the conflict and environmental stress that force migration rather than merely encouraging it: 590,000 people have fled Syria, where 80% of the remaining population live below the poverty line and unemployment is 55% 1

The movement then feeds back into the gap. Migrants are typically young, healthy and often better educated, so source countries lose the workers they most need — the brain drain — although remittances sent home and the skills of returning migrants work in the opposite direction 1

D7

"Tourism is an effective way of reducing the development gap." Do you agree? Use a named example. (6)

Show answer

Plan: the case for with Jamaica's figures, the case against with Jamaica's own limitations, then a judgement. Level marked; a one-sided answer caps at Level 2 because the command word asks whether you agree.

Tourism contributed 35% of Jamaica's GDP in 2019, bringing 4 million visitors and about $2 billion a year — over 50% of the country's total foreign exchange earnings, which is the currency it needs to buy imports 1

It is also the country's main source of employment, with over 200,000 people employed directly and indirectly; wages are spent locally and taxes are paid to the government, which allows more public spending — the multiplier effect 1

That spending produced infrastructure residents use as well as tourists: new port and cruise liner facilities at Falmouth and a new water treatment plant at Logwood, which improves health as well as the visitor experience. The Negril Marine Nature Park also gives conservation an economic value, so the environment is protected rather than exploited 1

However, the benefits are unevenly distributed: wealthy areas such as Montego Bay have gained most, pockets of poverty still exist, and infrastructure such as roads came much later because the authorities prioritised port and airport investment — so the industry was served before the population 1

Tourism is also fragile, because it depends on disposable income in other countries: although visitor arrivals continued to rise in 2009, the growth was well below previous years because the recession cut what visitors could spend. A country that earns a third of its GDP from tourism inherits other countries' downturns 1

Judgement: I partly agree. The scale is unarguable — expenditure rose from US$128.7 million in 1975 to US$2.6 billion in 2016 — and the jobs, tax revenue and infrastructure genuinely narrow the gap. But tourism concentrates the gains geographically and leaves the economy exposed, so it is effective as part of a strategy rather than as a substitute for industrial development and investment 1

D8

"Transnational corporations (TNCs) bring more disadvantages than advantages to a host country." Do you agree? Explain your answer. (6)

Show answer

Plan: name the TNC and the host country in the first line, advantages with figures, disadvantages with figures, judgement that ranks them. Level marked. The 2023 report: "the best answers used Nigeria and had good place specific detail linked to Shell".

Shell, an Anglo-Dutch oil TNC, has operated in Nigeria since 1958 following the discovery of oil in the Niger Delta, and it has brought substantial advantages: direct employment for tens of thousands of Nigerians and many more indirectly, with around 90% of its contracts awarded to Nigerian companies 1

That matters because local contracts and secure wages raise disposable income, which is spent locally and generates the multiplier effect, while Shell's tax contributions give the government revenue it could not otherwise raise — Nigeria's access to safe water rose from 46% to 85% between 1990 and 2019 1

Shell has also invested directly in human capital: $250,000 spent on healthcare and 3,000 scholarships to local schools, which raises the skill level of the workforce and so the country's longer-term capacity to develop 1

However, the financial benefit is smaller than it appears, because the company is registered in Bermuda and therefore pays less Nigerian tax; more generally, TNC profits leave the host country to shareholders in HICs, wages are lower than in the TNC's home country, and crude is exported before refining so the value added accrues abroad 1

The environmental cost falls entirely on Nigeria. Two massive oil spills at Bodo in Ogoniland destroyed aquatic ecosystems and fishermen's livelihoods, contamination in Ogoniland is 1,000 times higher than elsewhere, 42,000 residents took out a lawsuit against Shell, and gas flaring continues, adding greenhouse gases and causing respiratory illness. Oil wealth has also provoked armed groups such as the Niger Delta Avengers 1

Judgement: I mostly agree. The advantages are real but substitutable — another employer could provide jobs and contracts — whereas the disadvantages are permanent and concentrated: a destroyed delta ecosystem cannot be rebuilt, and a TNC can withdraw at any time, taking the jobs and leaving the pollution 1

D9

Suggest how the UK is moving towards a post-industrial economy. (6)

Show answer

Plan: define the shift, then take the three drivers — services, IT, and science parks and research — each with figures. The command word is suggest, so explanation is enough; no counter-argument required, but a limitation adds depth.

A post-industrial economy is one where most employment is in service industries rather than manufacturing, and the UK has made that transition: 80% of the economy now comes from the tertiary sector, against a 20th-century structure dominated by coal, steel and shipbuilding 1

The service sector grew because of the introduction of public services, the expansion of financial services, and rising leisure time and disposable income, while manufacturing shrank as mechanisation replaced workers and cheaper imports arrived from NEEs such as China 1

Information technology is the clearest marker of the shift: 1.3 million people are employed in IT, home-working and self-employment have grown because of the internet, and the UK is regarded as one of the world's leading IT countries, which attracts overseas investment 1

The growth rate shows how far the balance has tipped — the turnover of digital technology companies grew 4.5% between 2016 and 2017 against 1.7% growth in UK GDP, 2.6 times faster than the overall economy; London technology businesses raised £9 billion between 2015 and 2018; and the UK accounts for 5% of all high-growth technology workers employed globally 1

Research and development has become a sector in its own right since the 1980s, delivered through science parks: located on the edge of university cities with good transport links and attractive environments, employing graduates and tapping directly into university research. They employ around 75,000 people in the UK — Southampton Science Park opened in 1986 beside the M3, and 21% of the population of South Cambridgeshire works in hi-tech industries 1

Even manufacturing that survives has become post-industrial in character — high-technology, research-led and increasingly clean, as at the Nissan plant with its 10 wind turbines, 19,000 photovoltaic panels and a 22.4% cut in CO₂ since 2005. The transition is not costless, though: de-industrialisation left regional unemployment concentrated in North East England and widened the north–south divide 1

D10

Suggest how population decline can lead to social and economic changes in the rural landscape in the UK. (6)

Show answer

Plan: social changes, then economic changes, each as a chain. The mark scheme caps you at Level 2 if you cover only one of the two, so do both explicitly.

Younger people leave rural areas in search of work, training and services, so the population that remains is increasingly elderly — the Outer Hebrides has lost 50% of its population since 1901 1

Socially, fewer children means school rolls fall until schools close, which removes a focus of community life and makes the area still less attractive to young families — a self-reinforcing decline 1

An ageing population also increases demand for health and social care at exactly the point when the working-age population able to provide it is shrinking, and the community becomes less varied and less vibrant 1

Economically, a smaller population makes businesses less viable, because there are not enough customers to sustain them: village shops, pubs and post offices close, and rural bus services are withdrawn, which the figures describe as the loss of a "lifeline" for people without cars 1

The per-head cost of providing public services rises, because a smaller population reduces economies of scale — so local and national government face increasing costs to keep the same provision, and may ask whether it can be maintained at all 1

The landscape itself changes as a result: farms are amalgamated or abandoned, buildings become derelict or are converted to second homes, and services concentrate in larger settlements. The contrast with rural areas of population growth, such as North Somerset or Cambridgeshire, is instructive — there, higher demand secures the future of shops and schools, new residents invest in local businesses, and new developments create jobs 1

D11

Using a case study of a LIC/NEE, evaluate the effects of economic development on the population's quality of life. (9)

Show answer

Plan — intro naming Nigeria and the line; the gains; the costs; the complication that links them; judgement. Level marked: AO1 place knowledge, AO2 understanding, AO3 evaluation, 3 marks each. Add 3 SPaG marks.

Intro. Nigeria, the largest economy in Africa and the world's 13th largest oil producer, has seen economic development improve measurable quality of life substantially since independence in 1960 — but very unevenly, and at a serious environmental cost 1

The gains in health and services. Between 2015 and 2019 life expectancy rose by 9 years, and between 1990 and 2019 access to safe water rose from 46% to 85% — improvements funded ultimately by oil, tax and TNC investment 1

The gains in employment. The industrial structure has shifted away from subsistence agriculture, where employment has fallen 40%, towards manufacturing at 27% of the economy — now the fastest growing sector — and services at 53%. These jobs are better paid and more secure, and the wages spent locally generate a multiplier effect that benefits people outside the new industries 1

Evaluating the gains. They are real but shallow: 60% of the population still live in poverty and around 100 million people live on less than US$1 a day, and infant mortality remains 94 per 1,000 against the UK's 5, with life expectancy at 54 against 81. Nigeria ranks 164th on HDI against the UK's 13th 1

The environmental cost. 96% of Nigeria's forests have been destroyed through logging, agriculture and urban expansion; around 40 oil spills have destroyed aquatic ecosystems, with Ogoniland contaminated at 1,000 times the level found elsewhere and 42,000 residents suing Shell; gas flaring and vehicle emissions leave 94% of the population exposed to high air pollution 1

Why that reduces quality of life directly. Polluted water and dead fisheries remove both food and livelihood from delta communities; air pollution causes respiratory illness; and 10,000 tonnes of waste a day, plus electronic waste imported from Europe, contaminates land and water near the poorest settlements — so the same development that raised water access damaged water quality 1

The distributional problem. The gains are concentrated where the economy is: GDP per head is US$3,617 in the south against US$292 in the poorest region, and urban areas take a greater share of public services. Corruption diverts revenue and aid — Nigeria receives 6% of all African aid, yet that is only 0.8% of its GNI — and over-dependence on oil, 98% of export earnings but only 14% of GDP, leaves the whole economy exposed to the world price 1

Ranking the factors. The most significant limitation is not the environmental damage, severe as it is, but the governance of the revenue: thirty years ago Nigeria was at a similar stage of development to Malaysia and Singapore, both of which have moved far ahead from a comparable starting point and without comparable oil wealth 1

Conclusion. Economic development has improved quality of life in Nigeria only partly: the nine-year gain in life expectancy and the near-doubling of safe water access are genuine, but with 60% still in poverty, 94% breathing polluted air and the Malaysia comparison showing what was achievable, development has delivered far less quality of life than the country's resources should have allowed 1

D12

"The economic future of different parts of the UK will be affected by improvements and developments in transport and infrastructure."

Explain this statement. (9)

Show answer

Plan — intro defining the four infrastructure strands; how improvements create economic futures; the regional problem; the counter-strategies; judgement. Level marked, plus 3 SPaG marks.

Intro. The UK is developing four strands of infrastructure — the road network, the rail network, ports and airports — and because investment in each is geographically fixed, it determines which parts of the country have an economic future and which do not 1

How transport creates economic growth. Better links cut the cost and time of moving goods and people, so firms can reach wider markets and recruit from a larger labour pool; the construction itself creates jobs, and the permanent jobs that follow generate tax revenue and local spending through the multiplier effect 1

Rail. Crossrail cost £19 billion and halved journey times to Heathrow, raised property values 25% around its stations and brought 1.5 million more people within 45 minutes of central London — which expands the capital's effective labour market without anyone moving house 1

Ports and airports. Liverpool2 cost £400 million and created 5,000 jobs in the north-west, giving the region direct access to global container shipping; the proposed third runway at Heathrow would cost £19 billion but is forecast to boost the economy by £61 billion and create 75,000 jobs 1

Roads. The £2 billion widening of the A303 shows the same logic applied to the south-west, where a single congested route has constrained access for decades 1

Why this affects different parts unequally. The investments with the strongest business case tend to be in the most productive region, so Crossrail and Heathrow concentrate £38 billion of spending in the south-east. That reinforces the north–south divide, where the south-east already has longer life expectancy, higher incomes, higher house prices and lower unemployment 1

The counter-mechanism worth naming. A faster link can even work against the poorer end: if a northern city is two hours from London rather than four, a firm may choose to serve it from London rather than open an office there, so the link extends the capital's market instead of building a northern one 1

The strategies working the other way. Devolution moves economic decisions closer to the regions; HS2 was justified on northern connectivity; there are 43 Enterprise Zones across the UK offering tax breaks and simplified planning; and one Local Enterprise Partnership turned £1 billion of investment into 11,000 jobs. Rural infrastructure matters at a smaller scale too — the withdrawal of rural bus services is itself an infrastructure decision that accelerates population decline in places such as the Outer Hebrides 1

Conclusion. The statement is largely correct, but it understates the mechanism: infrastructure does not simply affect regional economic futures, it allocates them. Because investment follows existing productivity, transport improvements will continue to widen the north–south divide unless they are deliberately directed, which is exactly why Liverpool2, HS2, devolution and the Enterprise Zones exist 1
