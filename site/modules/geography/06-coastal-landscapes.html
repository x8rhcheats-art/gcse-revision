Waves — types, characteristics and what controls their size

Spec 3.1.3.2 · Coasts Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms; 2019 Q3.1

  • Waves defined — ripples in the sea caused by the transfer of energy from the wind blowing over the surface.
  • Two types — constructive and destructive. Constructive build a beach; destructive remove it.
  • Constructive waves — low height, long wavelength, low frequency, strong swash, weak backwash: net sediment gain.
  • Destructive waves — tall, short wavelength, high frequency, weak swash, strong backwash: net sediment loss.
  • Three controls on size — fetch (the distance the wave has travelled), wind speed and wind duration.
  • The UK's prevailing wind is from the south-west — which is why the south and west coasts have the biggest fetch.

✗ "Destructive waves are bigger so they destroy the beach." — true but unexplained, and it misses the mechanism.
✓ "Destructive waves are tall with a short wavelength and high frequency, so they plunge rather than spill. The backwash is stronger than the swash, which means more material is dragged back down the beach than is carried up it, so the beach loses sediment overall."

More detail

AQA's definition: waves are "ripples in the sea caused by the transfer of energy from the wind blowing over the surface of the sea. The largest waves are formed when winds are very strong, blow for lengthy periods and cross large expanses of water." That sentence contains all three controls on wave size, which is why it is worth learning whole.

From the school mat, the three factors in its own words: fetch (distance travelled); the speed of the wind blowing over the sea or ocean; and wind duration — "the longer the wind blows over the sea/ocean the larger the wave".

Weathering and mass movement at the coast

Spec 3.1.3.2 · Coasts Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms; 2023 Q3.2

  • Weathering is in situ — rock breaks down where it sits; erosion involves a moving force removing it.
  • Mechanical weathering — physical break-up with no chemical change; freeze-thaw is the example.
  • Freeze-thaw — water enters cracks, freezes, and expands by 9%, prising the rock apart.
  • Chemical weathering — decomposition by a chemical change within the rock; sea water attacks limestone and chalk.
  • Four types of mass movement — rockfall, landslide, mud slide and slumping.
  • Slumping needs saturation — a whole segment of cliff slides along a saturated shear plane, which is why clay cliffs slump.
More detail

AQA's definitions, which is what the 1-markers are marked against:

  • Mechanical weathering — "weathering processes that cause physical disintegration or break up of exposed rock without any change in the chemical composition of the rock, for instance freeze thaw."
  • Chemical weathering — "the decomposition (or rotting) of rock caused by a chemical change within that rock; sea water can cause chemical weathering of cliffs."
  • Mass movement — "the downhill movement of weathered material under the force of gravity. The speed can vary considerably."
  • Sliding — "occurs after periods of heavy rain when loose surface material becomes saturated and the extra weight causes the material to become unstable and move rapidly downhill, sometimes in an almost fluid state."
  • Slumping — "rapid mass movement which involves a whole segment of the cliff moving down-slope along a saturated shear-plane or line of weakness."

Freeze-thaw, step by step: water enters cracks in the rock face; it freezes and expands by 9%; this causes the crack to become larger; subsequent freezing and thawing enlarges the crack, causing rock to break away; large blocks can be shattered by repeated freeze-thaw weathering. The school mat gives that sequence for the glacial module, and it is identical at the coast.

Why slumping matters for the exam. The 2022 paper set five short questions on the Holderness coast, which "is made up of soft boulder clays (till) left behind after the ice sheets melted", and asked why the rate of erosion is so high. The answer runs through slumping: boulder clay is weak and permeable, so after heavy rain it becomes saturated, a whole segment slides down a shear plane, and the collapsed material is then removed by the waves, exposing a fresh cliff face. The photograph of Aldbrough in that paper showed exactly that — recent slumping and cliff collapse.

Erosion, transportation and deposition

Spec 3.1.3.2 · Coasts Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms; 2019 Q3.4; 2023 Q3.1; 2025 Q3.1

  • Five erosion processes — hydraulic power, abrasion (corrasion), attrition, solution (corrosion).
  • Hydraulic power — breaking waves compress pockets of air in cracks; the pressure widens the crack and breaks off rock.
  • Abrasion — the wearing away of cliffs by sediment flung at them by breaking waves.
  • Attrition — transported rocks bump into each other and break into smaller, rounder pieces; it does not erode the coast.
  • Four transport processes — traction, saltation, suspension and solution; plus longshore drift along the coast.
  • Deposition — material is dropped when the sea loses energy, so sheltered places and river mouths accumulate sediment.

✗ "Attrition erodes the cliff." — attrition wears down the sediment, not the coast. The 2019 MC tested exactly this.
✓ Attrition is why sediment gets smaller and rounder with distance along a coast, which is the answer to "suggest one reason for the difference in sediment size between location X and location Y".

More detail

Erosion is defined by AQA as "the wearing away and removal of material by a moving force, such as a breaking wave" — so a moving force is what separates it from weathering. The processes:

  • Hydraulic power — "the process by which breaking waves compress pockets of air in cracks in a cliff. The pressure may cause the crack to widen, breaking off rock." The 2025 paper asked for the term describing "the force of waves crashing into the cliffs"; the 2023 paper listed it as the correct process of erosion.
  • Abrasion, also called corrasion — "the wearing away of cliffs by sediment flung by breaking waves."
  • Attrition — "erosion caused when rocks and boulders transported by waves bump into each other and break up into smaller pieces."
  • Solution, also called corrosion — the dissolving of soluble rock such as limestone and chalk by sea water.

Transportation is simply "the movement of eroded material". The four processes from the mat are traction (large material rolled along the sea bed), saltation (smaller material bounced along), suspension (fine material carried within the water) and solution (dissolved material carried in the water).

Longshore drift is the one the exam cares about most. AQA: "the zigzag movement of sediment along a shore caused by waves going up the beach at an oblique angle (swash) and returning at right angles (backwash). This results in the gradual movement of beach materials along the coast." The mat's shorter version: "the zig-zag movement of material along the coast by the sea." Because waves approach at an angle determined by the prevailing wind — from the south-west in the UK — drift has a consistent direction along any stretch of coast, which is the fact that explains spits, groynes and the whole down-drift problem in coastal management.

Deposition — "occurs when material being transported by the sea is dropped due to the sea losing energy." The mat gives three reasons coastal deposition occurs: where the coastline changes direction, where the power of the waves is reduced (for example where it meets a river), and in sheltered areas such as bays where wave energy is low.

How geological structure and rock type shape the coast

Spec 3.1.3.2 · Coasts Revision Workbook; AQA List of Key Terms; 2022 Q3.4

  • Discordant coastline — bands of hard and soft rock run at right angles to the coast, producing headlands and bays.
  • Concordant coastline — bands run parallel to the coast, producing a straighter coastline with coves.
  • Headland — a rocky promontory of rock resistant to erosion.
  • Bay — a curve of less resistant rock eroded back between two headlands.
  • Rock type controls the rate — resistant rock gives steep cliffs; weak rock erodes fast into gentle cliffs.
  • Erosional landforms cluster on headlands — caves, arches and stacks need resistant rock to stand up in.
More detail

AQA's combined definition: headlands and bays — "a rocky coastal promontory made of rock that is resistant to erosion; headlands lie between bays of less resistant rock where the land has been eroded back by the sea."

Formation of headlands and bays, which was the 2020 6-marker: where a coastline is discordant, alternating bands of more and less resistant rock meet the sea at right angles. Hydraulic power, abrasion and solution erode the less resistant rock — clays and sands — much faster, so it retreats inland to form a bay. The more resistant rock — limestone or chalk — is left protruding as a headland. Once formed, the bay is sheltered because the headlands absorb the wave energy, so constructive waves deposit sediment there to form a beach, while the exposed headland now takes the full force of destructive waves and develops caves, arches and stacks.

The 2022 paper gave Holderness and asked, in 1 mark, "suggest why there is a headland at Flamborough Head". The answer is in the key: Flamborough Head is chalk, while the rest of Holderness is soft boulder clay (till) — so the chalk is far more resistant to erosion and has been left protruding while the clay has retreated.

The school's Coasts mock asked the mirror question off an OS map: "suggest one reason why this coastline has suitable conditions for the formation of erosional landforms such as arches, stacks and stumps." Its mark scheme accepts: the existence of a wave-cut platform or caves suggests more resistant rock in the area such as chalk; steep cliffs indicate more resistant rock; Flamborough is a headland, and headlands form where there is more resistant rock and typically feature landforms of erosion.

Cliffs and wave-cut platforms

Spec 3.1.3.2 · Coasts Revision Mat; Coasts Mock mark scheme Q1.6; 2022 Q3.6 (4 marks)

  • Wave-cut platform defined — a rocky, level shelf at or around sea level representing the base of old, retreated cliffs.
  • Step 1 — the sea attacks a weakness at the base of the cliff, for example a joint in chalk.
  • Step 2 — hydraulic action and abrasion cut a wave-cut notch into that weakness.
  • Step 3 — weathering weakens the top of the cliff while the notch grows.
  • Step 4 — the overhang becomes unstable and collapses under gravity; the cliff retreats inland.
  • Step 5 — the collapsed material is eroded and transported away, leaving the platform; the process repeats.

Wave-cut platform — the sequence the mark scheme wants

The school's Coasts mock mark scheme gives this as its example answer, and the 2022 paper asked for the same thing with diagrams for 4 marks. Level 2 ("clear") needs linked statements showing the sequence and the processes; Level 1 is simple ideas with a partial sequence and little reference to process.

  1. Processes of coastal erosion and weathering shape cliffs. Less resistant (soft) rock erodes quickly, forming gently sloping cliffs; more resistant (hard) rock forms steep cliffs. Wave-cut platforms form where cliffs are made of more resistant rock.
  2. A wave-cut platform is a wide, gently sloping surface found at the base of the cliff, extending into the sea.
  3. The sea attacks a weakness at the base of the cliff — for example a joint in chalk.
  4. A wave-cut notch is created by erosional processes such as hydraulic action (breaking waves compress air in the crack, and the pressure widens it) and abrasion (sediment flung at the cliff by the waves grinds it away).
  5. Weathering — chemical, biological and freeze-thaw — weakens the top of the cliff at the same time.
  6. As the notch becomes larger the cliff above becomes unstable and collapses as the result of gravity.
  7. The cliff retreats inland.
  8. The material from the collapsed cliff face is eroded and transported away, leaving a wave-cut platform.
  9. The process repeats over time, so the platform widens as the cliff retreats further.

✗ "The waves erode the bottom of the cliff and it falls down, leaving a platform." — the sequence is there but no process is named, which is a Level 1 answer.
✓ Name and explain each process as you go: "Hydraulic action — breaking waves compress pockets of air in cracks at the base of the cliff, and the pressure widens the crack — and abrasion, where sediment flung by the waves grinds the rock away, together cut a wave-cut notch."

More detail

The school's feedback lesson identifies precisely why candidates lose these marks: "many students were capped at level 1 here because they did not explain the processes. Hydraulic action — what is it? How does it actually erode the rock? Abrasion — what is it? How does it actually erode the rock?"

So for a 4-mark landform question, the structure from the exam technique guide is: step by step (beginning to end, clear sequence), processes named and explained, explanation of how that process actually creates the landform with nothing implied, and key words — wave-cut notch, mass movement, hydraulic action, abrasion. And if the question says "use one or more diagrams", the guide is blunt: "don't just label — annotate", and "if the question relates to a landform, explain the sequence in the order it occurs."

The school's mock also asked candidates to identify landform Z on a photograph: the answer was the wave-cut notch. Learn that word; it is the hinge of the whole sequence.

Where to point: Kimmeridge Bay in Dorset, in Kimmeridge Clay and Portland Limestone, is the class's wave-cut platform example.

Caves, arches, stacks and stumps

Spec 3.1.3.2 · Coasts Revision Mat; Y10 feedback lesson model answer; 2025 Q3.5 (4 marks)

  • Step 1 — waves attack a weakness or crack in a headland of resistant rock.
  • Step 2 — hydraulic action, abrasion and solution enlarge the crack into a cave.
  • Step 3 — continued erosion cuts the cave right through the headland to form an arch.
  • Step 4 — the arch is weathered at the top and eroded at the base, so it collapses under gravity, leaving a stack.
  • Step 5 — further erosion reduces the stack to a smaller, lower stump.
  • The named example is Old Harry Rocks — arches, caves and stacks in chalk at Ballard Point, Dorset.

Stack formation — the school's own 4/4 model answer

From the Y10 end-of-year feedback lesson, written against "Explain how a stack is formed in a coastal landscape. Use one or more diagrams. [4]". Learn the shape of it: each process is defined as it is used.

"Hydraulic action is when the waves force air inside cracks in the headland (hard rock), widening the cracks into caves. The waves also throw rocks against the headland which further erode the rock (abrasion). Together these processes erode the crack into a cave, and eventually the cave breaks through to form an arch. The arch is weathered at the top and eroded at the base, which weakens it, and it eventually collapses as it cannot support its own weight. This leaves a stack (a vertical column of rock)."

The mat's version of the same sequence: "Waves erode / attack a weakness (crack) in a headland. Hydraulic action, abrasion and corrosion enlarge the crack forming a cave. Continued erosion causes an arch to form. This collapses due to weathering and gravity to leave a sea stack."

More detail

AQA's definitions for each stage, which are what the 1-mark versions are marked against:

  • Cave — "a large hole in the cliff caused by waves forcing their way into cracks in the cliff face."
  • Arch — "a wave-eroded passage through a small headland. This begins as a cave formed in the headland, which is gradually widened and deepened until it cuts through."
  • Stack — "an isolated pillar of rock left when the top of an arch has collapsed. Over time further erosion reduces the stack to a smaller, lower stump."
  • Cliff — "a steep high rock face formed by weathering and erosion along the coastline."

Why it has to be a headland. Caves, arches and stacks only survive in resistant rock. On soft boulder clay the cliff simply slumps and retreats; chalk and limestone are strong enough to hold an overhang, which is why Old Harry Rocks is in chalk and why the 2019 6-marker used a Pembrokeshire photograph and the 2023 paper used Duncansby Head. If a question gives you a photograph of a stack, say in your first sentence that the rock is resistant and the feature is on a headland — it is the difference between describing and explaining.

Beaches and sand dunes

Spec 3.1.3.2 · Coasts Revision Workbook; AQA List of Key Terms; 2025 Q3.2–3.4

  • Beach defined — the zone of deposited material from the low water line to the limit of storm waves.
  • Sandy beaches — built by constructive waves with a strong swash; gently sloping and wide.
  • Pebble beaches — built by destructive waves; steeper, with larger material left at the top of the beach.
  • Sand dune defined — a coastal sand hill above the high tide mark, shaped by wind action, covered with grasses and shrubs.
  • Dune succession — embryo → fore → yellow → grey → mature dune, with vegetation increasing inland.
  • Marram grass is the pioneer — it stabilises the sand, which lets the next stage of vegetation colonise.

✗ "The mature dune has more plants than the embryo dune." — one mark out of two, and no data.
✓ Use the figures, as the school's feedback lesson insists: "the embryo dune only has 25% vegetation cover with 2 plant species, compared to the mature dune which has 100% vegetation cover with 22 species" — and add that the mature dune has fully grown trees whereas the embryo dune has only grass.

More detail

AQA: a beach is "the zone of deposited material that extends from the low water line to the limit of storm waves. The beach or shore can be divided in the foreshore and the backshore." A sand dune is a "coastal sand hill above the high tide mark, shaped by wind action, covered with grasses and shrubs."

Dune formation. Sand is deposited on a wide, gently sloping beach by constructive waves, then dries at low tide. The prevailing wind blows the dry sand inland until it is trapped by an obstacle — driftwood, shingle or a plant. Pioneer species, above all marram grass, colonise the deposit: they tolerate salt spray and burial, and their long roots bind the sand. That stabilises the dune, so humus accumulates, the soil improves and less tolerant species can move in, so the dunes become progressively more vegetated and more stable inland — embryo dune, fore dune, yellow dune, grey dune, mature dune, with soil pH falling as the shell content of the sand is leached out.

The 2025 paper turned that succession into three marks of data work: calculate the median soil pH from a row of seven values, describe changes in vegetation from embryo to mature dune using the table, and plot a point on a scattergraph of percentage vegetation cover against number of plant species. The school's feedback lesson notes the only problems were accuracy — "draw lines with a ruler, use a pencil to shade according to the key" — and failing to quote the data.

Where to point: Studland in Dorset for both the beach (Studland Bay) and the sand dunes.

Spits and bars

Spec 3.1.3.2 · Coasts Revision Mat; 2023 Q3.5 (4 marks); 2024 Q3.4

  • Spit defined — a finger of sediment extending from the shore out to sea, often at a river mouth.
  • Longshore drift builds it — material is moved along the coast until the coastline changes direction.
  • Deposition where energy drops — at a change of direction, or where a river reduces wave power, material accumulates.
  • The curved end — opposing winds and currents hook the tip of the spit landwards.
  • Bar defined — where a spit grows right across a bay, enclosing it to create a lagoon.
  • Salt marsh behind — the sheltered water behind a spit silts up and is colonised, forming salt marsh.

Spit formation — the mat's sequence, with the detail added

"Longshore drift moves material along a coastline. Where the coastline changes direction or the power of the waves is reduced (e.g. it meets a river) material is deposited. The sediment deposited builds up over time to form a spit."

  1. The prevailing wind drives waves onto the beach at an oblique angle, so the swash carries sediment up the beach at that angle while the backwash returns it at right angles — the zigzag movement of longshore drift.
  2. Where the coastline changes direction, or where a river mouth reduces wave energy, the sea loses the energy to carry the sediment and deposits it.
  3. Deposition continues, so the sediment builds up above sea level and extends out from the shore as a finger — a spit.
  4. Opposing winds and currents, and occasional storm waves from a second direction, curve the end of the spit landwards to give the characteristic hooked end.
  5. The water behind the spit is sheltered, so fine sediment settles, vegetation colonises it and salt marsh develops.
  6. If longshore drift carries the spit all the way across a bay, it becomes a bar, and the water trapped behind it forms a lagoon.
More detail

AQA: a spit is "a depositional landform formed when a finger of sediment extends from the shore out to sea, often at a river mouth. It usually has a curved end because of opposing winds and currents." A bar: "where a spit grows across a bay, a bay bar can eventually enclose the bay to create a lagoon. Bars can also form offshore due to the action of breaking waves."

The 2024 paper gave an OS map of Dawlish Warren, Devon — the class's own spit example — and asked candidates to describe the size and shape of the spit for 2 marks. That is a map-skill answer, not a formation answer: measure the length using the scale bar and the grid squares, and describe the shape as long, narrow and curving landwards at its tip across the mouth of the River Exe.

The 2019 paper used a spit to test attrition: sediment size at location X near the proximal (attached) end averaged 11.2 cm, and at location Y near the distal (seaward) end was much smaller. The reason is that sediment is transported further by longshore drift to reach Y, so attrition has had longer to break it into smaller, rounder pieces — and the sea has less energy at the far end, so only smaller material can still be carried.

Where to point: Dawlish Warren (spit, at the mouth of the River Exe), Slapton Ley near Torcross (bar), and the Isle of Portland joined to the mainland by Chesil Beach (tombolo).

Why Dorset works as one example. The coast is discordant around Swanage: bands of chalk, clay and limestone meet the sea at an angle, so resistant Purbeck Limestone and chalk stand out as Durlston Head and Ballard Point while the clays and sands between them have been eroded into Swanage Bay. That single geological fact explains the headland, the bay, the beach in the bay, and Old Harry Rocks on the headland — four landforms from one cause, which is exactly what the 6-mark "explain how coastal landscapes are formed by different processes" question rewards.

The Holderness coast turns up repeatedly in the real papers as the contrast, so it is worth a line: it is "made up of soft boulder clays (till) left behind after the ice sheets melted", so it erodes at several metres a year with slumping and cliff collapse — and the chalk at Flamborough Head at its northern end is the only part resistant enough to form a headland, while Spurn Head at its southern end is a spit built from the eroded material.

Hard engineering — sea walls, rock armour, gabions and groynes

Spec 3.1.3.2 · AQA List of Key Terms; Case Study Summary Booklet 2026 cost table; 2023 and 2025 6-markers

  • Hard engineering defined — using concrete and large artificial structures to defend land against natural erosion processes.
  • Sea wall — a concrete wall that reflects wave energy; the most effective and the most expensive, at £10,000/m.
  • Rock armour (rip-rap) — large boulders dumped on the beach that absorb wave energy; £2000/m.

More detail

AQA's definitions: hard engineering is "the use of concrete and large artificial structures by civil engineers to defend land against natural erosion processes." A sea wall is "a concrete wall which aims to prevent erosion of the coast by providing a barrier which reflects wave energy." Rock armour is "large boulders dumped on the beach as part of the coastal defences." A gabion is "steel wire mesh filled with boulders used in coastal defences." The down-drift problem, with named evidence. The school's Coasts mock mark scheme supplies two examples worth memorising. At Hornsea, "groynes trap sediment being transported by longshore drift, building up beaches, protecting the sea front. At Mappleton, the scheme "involved two types of hard engineering — placing rock armour along the base of the cliff and building two rock groynes. Soft engineering — beach nourishment, reprofiling and dune regeneration

Spec 3.1.3.2 · AQA List of Key Terms; Coasts Mock mark scheme; 2021 6-marker (per the school's booklet)

  • Soft engineering defined — managing erosion by working with natural processes to restore beaches and coastal ecosystems.
  • Beach nourishment — adding new sand or shingle artificially; £500/m, and the beach is a natural defence.
  • Beach reprofiling — moving material from the lower to the upper beach, usually after winter storms.
  • Dune regeneration — rebuilding dunes and replanting marram grass, with boardwalks; cheapest at £20/m.
  • The big advantage — it does not interfere with coastal processes elsewhere, so no down-drift problem.
  • The big disadvantage — it requires constant maintenance as the new material is washed away again.
More detail

AQA's definitions: soft engineering is "managing erosion by working with natural processes to help restore beaches and coastal ecosystems." Beach nourishment is "the addition of new material to a beach artificially, through the dumping of large amounts of sand or shingle." Beach reprofiling is "changing the profile or shape of the beach. It usually refers to the direct transfer of material from the lower to the upper beach or, occasionally, the transfer of sand down the dune face from crest to toe." Dune regeneration is "action taken to build up dunes and increase vegetation to strengthen the dunes and prevent excessive coastal retreat. This includes the re-planting of marram grass to stabilise the dunes, as well as planting trees and providing boardwalks."

The school's mat defines the difference in one sentence worth quoting: "Hard engineering coastal management involves building artificial structures which try to control natural processes whereas soft engineering involves taking a more sustainable and natural approach to managing the coast."

The mock mark scheme's evaluation of soft engineering, which is the best balanced paragraph available: "beach management… replaces beach or cliff material that has been removed by erosion or longshore drift. The main advantage is that beaches are a natural defence against erosion and coastal flooding. It is a relatively cheap option but requires constant maintenance to replace the beach material as it is washed away. However, it does not have a negative impact on coastal processes further along the coast." And: "soft engineering works with nature rather than against it, blends in with the environment and can improve it… doesn't interfere with processes elsewhere and affect other areas; is sustainable." Against that: "areas can just be left at the mercy of the sea, more gentle intervention may not be effective, people can lose homes, livelihoods."

Dune regeneration works because of succession. Marram grass tolerates salt spray and burial and binds the sand with long roots, so replanting it restarts the stabilisation that lets other species colonise. Boardwalks matter because trampling is what destroys the vegetation in the first place — the same logic as footpath repair in a National Park.

Managed retreat — coastal realignment

Spec 3.1.3.2 · AQA List of Key Terms; Case Study Summary Booklet 2026; 2024 Q3.1

  • Managed retreat defined — allowing the sea to flood or erode an area of low-value land, as nature taking its course.
  • Realignment — the shoreline is deliberately set back, and sea walls or embankments are breached or abandoned.
  • It creates natural defences — beaches and salt marshes develop, which absorb wave energy for free.
  • Salt marsh is an environmental gain — valuable habitat, and a buffer against coastal flooding.
  • It is cheap up front — but people must be compensated for lost buildings and farmland.
  • The named example — the Medmerry managed retreat scheme in West Sussex cost £28m.

More detail

AQA's full definition: managed retreat is "allowing cliff erosion to occur as nature taking its course: erosion in some areas, deposition in others. Benefits include less money spent and the creation of natural environments. It may involve setting back or realigning the shoreline and allowing the sea to flood areas that were previously protected by embankments and seawalls."

The 2024 paper made it a 1-mark multiple choice, and the correct option was "allowing the sea to flood or erode an area of low-value land". The distractors are worth knowing because they are definitions of other things: "building up dunes and increasing vegetation" is dune regeneration; "using concrete or artificial structures to defend against natural processes" is hard engineering; "working with natural processes to restore beaches" is soft engineering.

The mock mark scheme's balance: "Managed retreat. Areas of the coast are allowed to erode and flood naturally. Usually this will be areas considered to be of low value. The advantages are that it encourages the development of beaches (a natural defence) and salt marshes (important for the environment) and cost may be low. Managed retreat is a cheap option, but people will need to be compensated for loss of buildings and farmland."

Medmerry is the school's named scheme for this, and the figure to quote is £28m — the cost of the managed retreat scheme, from the school's quick-fire list. The school's 6-and-9-marker booklet records that the 2021 6-marker — "Discuss the costs and benefits of soft engineering strategies in protecting coastlines" — was answered on Medmerry.

The four shoreline management options, which frame all of this: do nothing (let natural processes take their course and deal with the consequences); hold the line (maintain the existing defences); advance the line (build new defences further out to sea); managed retreat (allow the shoreline to move inland in a controlled way).

Brighton — a coastal management scheme in the UK

  • Where — River Adur to Brighton Marina, a 10 km stretch of coastline.
  • Why manage it — a high-value built-up area: residential, tourism and leisure, hotels and restaurants.
  • The money at stake — the value of tourism is £5bn a year, and the scheme protects the A259 coastal road.
  • Hard engineering — wooden and concrete groynes and a sea wall; timber groynes cost £75,000 per 100 m.

Brighton — reasons, strategy, effects and conflicts

The school's named UK coastal management scheme. The spec asks for exactly three things, so learn it in three blocks — and note that John's class also did their Brighton fieldwork here, titled "Evaluate the success of groynes as a method of coastal management on Brighton beach", measuring drop height on the groynes at 2 m intervals.

Reasons for management. The managed stretch runs from the River Adur to Brighton Marina — 10 km of coastline. It is a high-value built-up area with residential areas, tourism and leisure activities (Brighton Pier, the i360, Brighton Marina), hotels and restaurants. High-value seafront properties, for example near Hove, are at risk of coastal erosion, and the beach itself attracts tourists. The value of tourism is £5bn a year.

Management strategy. Hard engineering — wooden and concrete groynes and a sea wall, which creates a promenade, plus a breakwater and harbour wall at the Marina. Timber groynes cost £75,000 per 100 metres (= £750/m). Soft engineering — beach nourishment and reprofiling close to Brighton Pier, which happens around May each year. The management also protects the A259 coastal road. There is cliff stabilisation at Saltdean and Rottingdean. And there is a new strategy to widen the beach, costing £11m: rock armour and a new sea wall, longer groynes and beach nourishment.

Resulting effects and conflicts. There is potential conflict between locals, who want the area protected from coastal erosion, and tourists, who may find hard engineering unsightly. The maintenance of the management, and its cost, are ongoing problems. And because groynes trap sediment moving by longshore drift, the beach is wide at Brighton but the coast down-drift receives less new material.

The cost table to quote (Case Study Summary Booklet): sea wall £10,000/m · rock armour £2000/m · groynes £750/m · gabions £500/m · beach nourishment £500/m · dune regeneration £20/m.

More detail

Why Brighton answers every version of the management question. The spec asks for reasons, strategy, effects and conflicts; the exam asks it as "explain the effects of the scheme" (2024, 4 marks), "discuss the costs and benefits of hard engineering" (2023, 6 marks), "assess the effectiveness of strategies used to protect coastlines against erosion" (2022, 6 marks), and "'coastal management schemes move the problem of erosion from one location to another' — do you agree?" (school mock, 6 marks). Brighton supplies all of them because it combines hard engineering, soft engineering, cliff stabilisation and a costed new scheme on one 10 km stretch.

The cost–benefit argument, in the shape the 6-marker wants. The benefit is proportionate to the value protected: £5bn a year of tourism, the A259, and high-value seafront property at Hove justify spending £11m on widening the beach, because the cost of doing nothing would be far higher. The cost is that the defences need permanent maintenance — beach nourishment is repeated every May — and that groynes transfer the problem down-drift. The conflict is aesthetic and economic at once: the same hard engineering that protects the tourist economy can make the seafront less attractive to the tourists it exists for.

The school's 6/6 model answer for "Discuss the effectiveness of using hard engineering strategies to manage the coastline" is the best template, and its moves are worth copying: it names a strategy visible in the figure, judges its effectiveness with a degree word ("largely effective"), explains the mechanism ("groynes block the sediment as it moves down the coast, making the beach wider, meaning it can protect the coastline from erosion as waves break on the beach which absorbs the energy"), adds a secondary benefit ("a larger beach is also beneficial for tourism"), concedes a cost ("do require maintenance"), and quotes a figure. Then it does the same for gabions and for sea walls, and reaches a comparative judgement — "sea walls are the most effective option for reducing erosion… however, they can cost up to £25,000 per meter."

The feedback lesson's instructions for that question are the checklist: discuss at least two strategies; refer to the figure; comment on how effective they are at protecting the coast from erosion, not only on cost and appearance; and make a judgement — fully, largely, somewhat, or not at all.

Why does a coastline end up with headlands and bays rather than eroding back evenly?

Show answer

Because the rock is not uniform. On a discordant coastline, alternating bands of resistant and less resistant rock meet the sea at right angles. The weak rock — clays and sands at Swanage Bay — is eroded by hydraulic power, abrasion and solution far faster, so it retreats inland and becomes a bay. The resistant rock — Purbeck Limestone at Durlston Head, chalk at Ballard Point — is left protruding as a headland.

Then the shape feeds back on the process. The headlands now take the full force of destructive waves, so they develop caves, arches and stacks; the bays are sheltered because the headlands absorb the energy, so constructive waves deposit sediment there and a beach forms. The difference widens itself.

The exam point: rock type is the explanation for almost every "suggest why" on a coastal map. Flamborough Head is a headland because it is chalk and the rest of Holderness is boulder clay.

U2

Why can caves, arches and stacks only form in resistant rock?

Show answer

Because every stage of the sequence requires the rock to hold itself up over a void. A cave is a hole with rock above it; an arch is a bridge; a stack is a free-standing column. Chalk and limestone are strong enough in compression to do that for a long time, which is why Old Harry Rocks exists in chalk.

Weak rock behaves completely differently. Holderness is soft boulder clay, so when it is undercut it does not hold an overhang — it saturates after heavy rain and a whole segment slumps down a shear plane. The cliff simply retreats, and no arch ever gets the chance to form.

The exam point: if you are given a photograph of a stack, your first sentence should establish that it is on a headland of resistant rock. That is the "explain" the question wants; the sequence alone is description.

U3

Why do spits curve at the end?

Show answer

Because longshore drift is not the only thing acting on the tip. Drift from the prevailing wind direction builds the spit outwards from the point where the coastline changes direction. But the exposed end also receives waves generated by winds from other directions, and is affected by tidal currents and, at a river mouth, by the river's own flow. Those opposing forces push the accumulating sediment landwards, hooking the distal end — which is why AQA's definition says "it usually has a curved end because of opposing winds and currents".

The same logic explains the salt marsh. Behind the spit the water is sheltered, so energy is low, fine sediment settles out and vegetation colonises it.

The exam point: the hook and the salt marsh are two easy extra marks on a 4-mark spit question, and almost nobody includes them.

U4

Why does sediment get smaller the further along a spit you measure?

Show answer

Two reasons, and the best answers give both. First, attrition: as longshore drift carries material along the coast, the rocks and pebbles repeatedly bump into one another and break into smaller, rounder pieces, so material that has travelled further has been worn down more. Second, energy: the sea has less energy at the distal end of the spit, so it can no longer carry the largest material, which has already been deposited further back.

That is why the 2019 data showed a mean of 11.2 cm at location X near the shore and much smaller sizes at location Y at the far end.

The exam point: "suggest one reason for the difference" is only 1 mark, so pick attrition — but know both, because the 2-mark version wants a developed chain.

U5

Why do groynes cause a problem somewhere else?

Show answer

Because the sediment they trap was on its way somewhere. Longshore drift is a continuous conveyor: beaches down-drift are maintained by material arriving from up-drift. A groyne interrupts that supply, so the beach grows on its up-drift side and the beaches beyond it are, in the mark scheme's phrase, "starved of sediment". Since the beach is what absorbs wave energy, its loss means waves now reach the cliff base — at Hornsea, "even neap tides reach the base of the boulder clay cliffs increasing the rate of erosion".

Mappleton is the cleanest evidence: rock armour and two rock groynes mean Mappleton and its cliffs are no longer at great risk, but erosion south of Mappleton has increased.

The exam point: this is the answer to the school mock's question, "'coastal management schemes move the problem of erosion from one location to another' — do you agree?" Yes for groynes, much less so for a sea wall, and not at all for managed retreat.

U6

Why spend £10,000 a metre on a sea wall at Brighton but allow Medmerry to flood?

Show answer

Because the value of what is protected differs enormously, and that is the whole logic of coastal management. At Brighton the defended stretch runs 10 km from the River Adur to the Marina through a high-value built-up area: hotels, restaurants, high-value seafront property near Hove, Brighton Pier, the i360, the Marina, the A259 coastal road, and tourism worth £5bn a year. Spending £11m on widening the beach is trivially justified against that.

Managed retreat is applied to land AQA explicitly calls "low-value" — farmland and open coast — where the cost of holding the line exceeds the value of what is behind it. And it buys something hard engineering cannot: beaches and salt marshes that absorb wave energy for free and improve habitat.

The exam point: "which strategy is best" has no answer without "best for what coast". Say that, and the judgement writes itself.

U7

Why is soft engineering cheaper per metre but not necessarily cheaper overall?

Show answer

Because you pay again every year. Beach nourishment costs about £500 a metre against £10,000 for a sea wall, but the sand it adds is removed by exactly the processes that removed the original sand, so it "requires constant maintenance to replace the beach material as it is washed away". Brighton renourishes near the Pier around every May. A sea wall costs twenty times as much per metre but lasts 30–50 years.

So the comparison is a whole-life one. Soft engineering wins on environmental grounds — it works with nature, blends in, and does not interfere with processes further along the coast — and on up-front cost, but not automatically on total cost over fifty years on a high-energy frontage.

The exam point: comparing cost per metre with lifespan is exactly the kind of "detailed" reasoning that reaches Level 3.

Because they named the processes without explaining them. The feedback is blunt: "many students were capped at level 1 here because they did not explain the processes. Hydraulic action — what is it? How does it actually erode the rock? Abrasion — what is it? How does it actually erode the rock?"

The 4/4 model answer defines each one in passing: "Hydraulic action is when the waves force air inside cracks in the headland (hard rock), widening the cracks into caves. The waves also throw rocks against the headland which further erode the rock (abrasion)." That is the same length and scores double.

D1

Compare two features of destructive and constructive waves. (2)

Show answer

Destructive waves are taller and have a shorter wavelength and higher frequency than constructive waves, which are low with a long wavelength and a low frequency 1

Constructive waves have a strong swash and a weak backwash, so they deposit material and the beach gains sediment, whereas destructive waves have a weak swash and a strong backwash, so they drag material offshore and the beach loses sediment 1

"Whereas" is carrying the second mark. Two separate descriptions are not a comparison.

D2

Describe the changes in vegetation from the embryo dune to the mature dune. (2)

Show answer

Vegetation cover and the number of plant species both increase inland: the embryo dune has only 25% vegetation cover with 2 plant species, compared with the mature dune, which has 100% cover with 22 species 1

The type of vegetation also changes: the embryo dune has only pioneer grasses such as marram grass, whereas the mature dune has fully grown trees and shrubs 1

D3

Explain how a wave-cut platform is formed as a cliff is eroded. Use one or more diagrams to support your answer. (4)

Show answer

Where a cliff is made of more resistant rock, the sea attacks a weakness at its base — for example a joint in chalk — and erodes a wave-cut notch. The processes are hydraulic power, where breaking waves compress pockets of air in the crack so the pressure widens it, and abrasion, where sediment flung at the cliff by the waves grinds the rock away 1

At the same time weathering — freeze-thaw, where water in cracks freezes and expands by 9%, as well as chemical and biological weathering — weakens the top of the cliff 1

As the notch becomes larger the rock above it is left overhanging and unstable, so it eventually collapses under gravity, and the cliff retreats inland 1

The material from the collapsed cliff face is eroded and transported away by the waves, leaving behind a wide, gently sloping rocky shelf at the base of the cliff extending into the sea — the wave-cut platform. The process then repeats, so the platform widens as the cliff retreats further 1

Annotate the diagram, don't just label it: show the notch, the overhang, the collapse and the platform in sequence, with the process named at each stage.

D4

Explain how a stack is formed in a coastal landscape. Use one or more labelled diagrams. (4)

Show answer

Waves attack a weakness such as a crack in a headland of resistant rock, for example the chalk at Old Harry Rocks in Dorset. Hydraulic action — where the waves force air inside the cracks, and the pressure widens them — enlarges the crack 1

The waves also throw rocks against the headland, which further erodes it (abrasion), and sea water dissolves soluble rock (solution). Together these processes erode the crack into a cave 1

Continued erosion deepens and widens the cave until it breaks right through the headland to form an arch 1

The arch is weathered at the top and eroded at the base, which weakens it, and it eventually collapses because it cannot support its own weight. This leaves a stack — a vertical column of rock isolated from the headland — which further erosion will reduce to a lower stump 1

✓ Define each process as you use it — that is the difference between Level 1 and 4/4, according to the school's own feedback.

D5

Explain how spits and bars form along the coast as a result of deposition. (4)

Show answer

The prevailing wind drives waves onto the beach at an oblique angle, so the swash carries sediment up the beach at that angle and the backwash returns it at right angles — the zigzag movement of longshore drift, which moves material steadily along the coast 1

Where the coastline changes direction, or where it meets a river mouth and the power of the waves is reduced, the sea loses the energy to carry the sediment and deposits it 1

Deposition continues, so the sediment builds up above sea level and extends out from the shore as a finger — a spit, such as Dawlish Warren at the mouth of the River Exe. Opposing winds and currents curve its end landwards, and the sheltered water behind it silts up to form salt marsh 1

If longshore drift carries the spit all the way across a bay it joins the far side and becomes a bar, trapping the water behind it as a lagoon — for example Slapton Ley at Torcross 1

D6

You have studied a coastal management scheme in the UK. Explain the effects of the scheme. (4)

Show answer

I studied the scheme at Brighton, which protects 10 km of coastline from the River Adur to Brighton Marina. The wooden and concrete groynes trap sediment moving by longshore drift, so the beach has widened; a wider beach means waves break further out and their energy is absorbed before reaching the sea wall and the cliffs behind 1

As a result the high-value built-up area behind the defences is protected — hotels, restaurants, high-value seafront properties near Hove, Brighton Pier, the i360 and the Marina — along with the A259 coastal road, safeguarding tourism worth £5bn a year 1

There are negative effects too. The defences require continuous maintenance and expenditure: beach nourishment and reprofiling near Brighton Pier is repeated around every May, and a new £11m scheme of rock armour, a new sea wall, longer groynes and nourishment is needed to widen the beach further 1

The groynes also interrupt longshore drift, so the coast down-drift receives less new beach material and is more exposed to erosion — and there is conflict between locals, who want protection from erosion, and tourists, who may find hard engineering unsightly on a seafront whose appeal is the view 1

D7

Discuss the costs and benefits of hard engineering strategies for coastal management. (6)

Show answer

Level marked: Basic 1–2, Clear 3–4, Detailed 5–6. At least two strategies, reference to the figure, a judgement with a degree word, and figures for Level 3. Plan:

Groynes. Largely effective on a drift-dominated coast: they trap sediment as it moves along the coast, widening the beach, which means waves break on the beach and their energy is absorbed before reaching the cliffs. A wider beach also benefits tourism. At about £750 per metre — Brighton's timber groynes cost £75,000 per 100 m — they are cost-effective 1

The groyne cost. But by trapping sediment a groyne deprives the area down-drift of new beach material. At Mappleton, rock armour and two rock groynes removed the risk to the village but increased erosion to the south; at Hornsea, down-drift beaches are so starved of sediment that even neap tides reach the base of the boulder clay cliffs 1

Sea walls. The most effective option for reducing erosion: a concrete barrier that reflects wave energy, durable for 30–50 years, and it creates a promenade used by locals and tourists. It also does not impede the movement of sediment along the coast, unlike a groyne 1

The sea wall cost. At around £10,000 per metre it is by far the most expensive option, it needs maintenance, reflected energy can scour the beach in front of it, and it can look obtrusive 1

Rock armour and gabions. Rock armour at £2000/m absorbs wave energy and lets a beach build up behind it, but the boulders are imported so look out of place and can shift in storms. Gabions — wire cages of rocks that absorb energy as water passes through the gaps — cost only £500/m, so they are a good choice for protecting something important but unvisited, such as a road or a power station, though they are not visually appealing 1

Judgement. Hard engineering is largely effective where the value protected is high — at Brighton it defends £5bn of annual tourism and the A259 for an £11m scheme — but its costs are real: it must be maintained indefinitely, and groynes transfer the erosion problem down the coast, which is why soft engineering and managed retreat are preferred on lower-value frontages 1

D8

Discuss the costs and benefits of soft engineering strategies in protecting coastlines. (6)

Show answer

Level marked. The 2021 question, which the school answered on Medmerry. Plan:

What it is. Soft engineering manages erosion by working with natural processes to restore beaches and coastal ecosystems, rather than building artificial structures to control them 1

Beach nourishment and reprofiling — the benefit. Adding sand or shingle artificially replaces material removed by erosion or longshore drift, and a beach is a natural defence against both erosion and coastal flooding, because it absorbs wave energy. At around £500 per metre it is relatively cheap, and it blends into the environment — Brighton nourishes and reprofiles near the Pier each May 1

The cost. It requires constant maintenance to replace material as it is washed away, so the low price per metre is paid repeatedly; and on a high-energy frontage gentle intervention may simply not be effective 1

Dune regeneration. Rebuilding dunes and replanting marram grass stabilises the sand, allowing other vegetation to colonise and the dune to strengthen, with boardwalks preventing the trampling that destroyed it. At about £20 per metre it is the cheapest strategy on the spec — but it only works where dunes exist and it takes years 1

Managed retreat. Allowing the sea to flood or erode low-value land encourages the development of beaches and, importantly, salt marshes, which are valuable habitat and absorb wave energy for free. The Medmerry scheme cost £28m, so set-up is not free, and people must be compensated for the loss of buildings and farmland 1

Judgement. The decisive advantage of soft engineering is that it works with nature rather than against it and does not interfere with coastal processes elsewhere, so unlike groynes it does not move the problem down the coast — which makes it sustainable. But it is only appropriate where the land behind is of lower value, because people can lose homes and livelihoods, and it cannot defend a £5bn seafront such as Brighton's on its own 1

D9

Explain how different coastal landforms are created by erosion. (6)

Show answer

Level marked. "Different landforms" means at least two or three, each with its own sequence. If a figure is given, use it as the structure — the exam guide suggests splitting a photograph into quadrants and finding a point in each. Plan:

The processes. Four processes of erosion operate: hydraulic power, where breaking waves compress pockets of air in cracks and the pressure widens them; abrasion, where sediment flung by the waves grinds the rock away; attrition, which wears the sediment itself smaller and rounder; and solution, where sea water dissolves soluble rock such as chalk and limestone 1

Headlands and bays. On a discordant coastline, bands of hard and soft rock meet the sea at right angles. The less resistant rock erodes faster and retreats to form a bay — Swanage Bay, in clays and sands — while the resistant rock is left protruding as a headland, such as Durlston Head in Purbeck Limestone 1

Cliffs and wave-cut platforms. The sea attacks a weakness at the base of a resistant cliff, and hydraulic power and abrasion cut a wave-cut notch; weathering weakens the cliff top; the overhang collapses under gravity and the cliff retreats, leaving a gently sloping rocky shelf — the wave-cut platform at Kimmeridge Bay 1

Caves, arches and stacks. On the exposed headland, waves attack a crack; hydraulic action, abrasion and solution enlarge it into a cave; continued erosion cuts the cave through the headland to form an arch; the arch is weathered above and eroded below until it cannot support its own weight and collapses, leaving a stack — Old Harry Rocks, in chalk at Ballard Point 1

Why rock type runs through all of it. Resistant rock holds steep cliffs and overhangs, so it produces platforms, arches and stacks; weak rock such as the boulder clay of Holderness slumps and retreats instead, so it produces rapidly eroding cliffs and no arches at all 1

Linking sentence. The landforms are therefore not independent: the headland created by differential erosion is what concentrates wave energy and allows the cave–arch–stack sequence to develop, while the sheltered bay beside it becomes a site of deposition 1

D10

"Coastal management schemes move the problem of erosion from one location to another." Do you agree? Using an example, explain your answer. (6)

Show answer

Level marked. The school's own mock question. Its mark scheme states there is no "correct" view and both sides can be credited if supported with evidence, but a named example is required for Level 3. Plan:

Agree — the mechanism. Longshore drift is a continuous conveyor: beaches are maintained by sediment arriving from up-drift. Hard engineering that interrupts it therefore protects one place at the expense of another — the mark scheme's phrase is that down-drift areas are "starved of sediment" 1

Agree — the named evidence. At Mappleton the scheme placed rock armour along the base of the cliff and built two rock groynes; Mappleton and its cliffs are no longer at great risk, but the groynes stopped beach material moving south and erosion south of Mappleton has increased. At Hornsea, groynes build up the beach protecting the sea front, but down-drift even neap tides now reach the base of the boulder clay cliffs 1

Disagree — not all strategies do this. A sea wall reflects wave energy without trapping sediment, and the school mat notes that sea walls do not impede the movement of sediment along the coast. I partly agree: the statement is true of groynes, which demonstrably transfer erosion down-drift, but it is not true of soft engineering or managed retreat, which work with coastal processes instead of interrupting them. Stretch — 9-mark shape. "Hard engineering is the only realistic way to protect a developed coastline." To what extent do you agree? (9)

Show answer

Introduction — the coast and the stakes. At Brighton, 10 km from the River Adur to Brighton Marina is managed because it is a high-value built-up area: hotels, restaurants, high-value seafront property near Hove, Brighton Pier, the i360, the Marina, the A259 coastal road and tourism worth £5bn a year 1

Paragraph 1 — the case for hard engineering. Sea walls reflect wave energy, last 30–50 years and create promenades; groynes trap drift and widen the beach so waves break offshore; rock armour absorbs energy and lets a beach build behind it. On a high-energy developed frontage these are the only options that reliably hold the line 1

The costs are justified by the value protected: an £11m beach-widening scheme against £5bn a year of tourism is a strong ratio, and at £750 per metre groynes are cheap relative to the property behind them 1

Paragraph 2 — the case against. Hard engineering transfers the problem: groynes starve the coast down-drift of sediment, so at Mappleton erosion increased to the south and at Hornsea neap tides now reach the boulder clay cliffs. It also requires indefinite maintenance, and a sea wall at £10,000 per metre is unaffordable on most of the coast 1

It can also damage the asset it protects: there is conflict at Brighton between locals who want protection and tourists who find hard engineering unsightly — so on a coast whose value is its appearance, concrete has a hidden cost 1

Paragraph 3 — the alternatives, and where they work. Soft engineering works with natural processes, blends into the environment and, crucially, does not interfere with processes further along the coast. Beach nourishment at £500 per metre and dune regeneration at £20 per metre are far cheaper, and Brighton already uses nourishment and reprofiling near the Pier each May 1

The realistic answer is not one or the other but a combination matched to the value of the land: hold the line with hard engineering where assets are concentrated, and work with natural processes where they are not. Conclusion that decides. I agree only partly. Hard engineering is necessary on a developed, high-energy frontage, because nothing else holds the line against destructive waves — but it is not sufficient, since it must be combined with nourishment to offset the sediment it traps, and it is not "the only realistic way" for the coast as a whole, where managed retreat is both cheaper and environmentally better 1

D12

Stretch — 9-mark shape. Assess the extent to which rock type and structure, rather than coastal processes, determine the landforms of a stretch of coastline. (9)

Show answer

A "to what extent is X rather than Y" question: you must argue both and then decide. Use Dorset and Holderness as the paired evidence. Plan:

Introduction. The spec itself says distinctive coastal landforms are the result of rock type, structure and physical processes, so the question is really about which dominates 1

Paragraph 1 — the case for rock type and structure. On the discordant Dorset coast, resistant Purbeck Limestone forms Durlston Head and chalk forms Ballard Point, while the clays and sands between them have been eroded back into Swanage Bay. The same waves act on all of it, so only the rock can explain the difference 1

Rock type also decides which landforms are even possible: chalk is strong enough to hold an arch and then a stack, which is why Old Harry Rocks exists, whereas the soft boulder clay of Holderness slumps along a saturated shear plane instead and no arch ever forms 1

Structure decides the plan of the coast: discordant coastlines give headlands and bays, concordant ones give a straighter coast with coves — so the large-scale shape is geological before any process acts 1

Paragraph 2 — the case for processes. Processes do the work: hydraulic power compresses air in cracks, abrasion grinds the rock, solution dissolves chalk and limestone, and without them no landform forms at all, however weak the rock 1

Processes also determine the rate, and rates are set by wave energy, which depends on fetch, wind speed and wind duration. Holderness retreats rapidly because weak rock meets high-energy North Sea waves; the same clay in a sheltered bay would not 1

And depositional landforms are almost purely process-driven: a spit such as Dawlish Warren, a bar such as Slapton Ley, Chesil Beach as a tombolo, and the Studland dunes all exist because of longshore drift, the change in coastline direction and the wind, not because of the rock beneath them 1

Paragraph 3 — the interaction. The two are not separable, because each shapes the other. Differential erosion creates the headland; the headland then concentrates wave energy on itself and shelters the bay beside it; so the bay receives deposition and a beach while the headland develops caves, arches and stacks. Rock type sets the template and processes execute it, but the result then redirects the processes 1

Conclusion that decides. Rock type and structure determine which landforms appear and where, and are therefore the more fundamental control on an erosional coastline such as Dorset. But processes determine how fast and are the dominant control on depositional landforms, which owe nothing to the local geology — so the statement holds to a great extent for erosional landforms and only slightly for depositional ones 1
