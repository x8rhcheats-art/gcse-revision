Global atmospheric circulation — the engine

Spec 3.1.1.3 "General atmospheric circulation model: pressure belts and surface winds" · Tropical Storms Revision Mat; AQA Revision Notes; AQA List of Key Terms

  • Definition — the worldwide system of winds which transports heat from tropical to polar latitudes, through a troposphere 15 km deep.
  • Three cells per hemisphere — Hadley, Ferrel and Polar. Learn them in that order, equator outwards.
  • Rising air = low pressure — at the Equator and at 60°; cloud, rain and storms.
  • Sinking air = high pressure — at 30° and at the poles; cloudless skies, low rainfall, deserts.
  • Surface winds blow from high to low — trade winds towards the Equator, westerlies towards the poles.
  • The Coriolis effect bends them — the Earth's rotation curves winds right in the northern hemisphere, left in the southern.

✗ "Sinking air causes low pressure." — the 2023 examiner's report: "a significant proportion thought that sinking air causes low pressure, despite the evidence provided in the resource."
✓ Rising air leaves less air pressing down, so it creates low pressure; sinking air piles up and creates high pressure. Rising = wet, sinking = dry.

✗ Writing "prevailing winds" or "polar winds" where the answer is trade winds — the other error the 2023 report names on that cloze question.

More detail

The sequence AQA wants, equator outwards:

  1. The Equator receives the most intense solar heating, because the Sun is overhead and the energy is concentrated on the smallest area.
  2. The air there is heated, becomes less dense and rises, creating a belt of low pressure. As it rises it cools and condenses, forming cloud and heavy rainfall — which is why tropical rainforests sit on the Equator.
  3. The rising air reaches the top of the troposphere, stops rising and spreads north and south, losing its remaining heat and moisture.
  4. At 30° north and south this cooler, denser, now-dry air sinks, creating a belt of high pressure with cloudless skies and very low rainfall — which is why the hot deserts sit on the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn.
  5. At the surface the air splits: some flows back towards the Equator as the trade winds (completing the Hadley cell), and some flows polewards as the westerlies (starting the Ferrel cell).
  6. At 60° the warm westerlies meet cold polar air; the warmer air is forced up, creating low pressure and the unsettled weather the UK lives under.
  7. At the poles, cold dense air sinks, creating high pressure, and flows outwards as the polar winds (completing the Polar cell).

Ocean currents move heat too: thermohaline circulation carries warm surface water polewards and cold dense salty water back along the ocean floor. The pressure belts also shift north in our summer and south in our winter, following the overhead Sun.

Where tropical storms happen, and the conditions they need

Spec 3.1.1.3 "Global distribution of tropical storms; relationship with general atmospheric circulation" · AQA Revision Notes; AQA GCSE textbook; 2019 and 2023 question papers

  • Between 5° and 30° north and south of the Equator — the 2019 cloze answer; 2023's multiple choice answer was "between the Tropics".
  • Sea surface above 27 °C to a depth of 100 m — the warm sea is the energy supply, so the storm needs a deep warm layer, not a warm skin.
  • Not on the Equator itself — there is no Coriolis effect there, so the storm cannot spin and hold together.
  • Over the ocean, in summer and autumn — that is when sea temperatures are high enough.
  • Low wind shear — winds must not change much with height, or the towering storm clouds are torn apart.
  • They track west — carried by the prevailing trade winds, then curve polewards: north in the northern hemisphere, south in the southern.

✗ "Tropical storms happen in hot countries."
✓ Give the latitude band and the reason: "between 5° and 30° north and south of the Equator, because the sea there exceeds 27 °C to a depth of 100 m, which supplies the heat energy that makes the air rise." The 2019 question was worth 2 marks for exactly two such reasons.

More detail

Why South America almost never gets one: the sea on its west coast is too cold. That single comparison proves the 27 °C condition is doing real work rather than being a memorised number.

Three names, one thing. In the USA and the Caribbean they are hurricanes; in South-East Asia and Australia, cyclones; in Japan and the Philippines, typhoons. Haiyan is a typhoon because it hit the Philippines.

The Coriolis effect is the deflection of wind by the Earth's rotation. The Earth is far wider at the Equator than at the poles, so it must spin faster there; that difference in speed bends any wind crossing the surface. Hurricanes in the northern hemisphere bend to the right, so the clouds swirl anticlockwise; southern-hemisphere cyclones swirl clockwise. On the Equator itself the effect is zero, so storms neither form there nor cross it.

Named distribution you can quote from the 2019 Figure 3: the East Pacific (Jun–Oct), the Caribbean (Aug–Oct), South Asia (Oct–Nov), East Asia (May–Dec), the South-West Pacific (Jan–Mar), Madagascar (Dec–Mar) and West Australia (Jan–Mar).

How a tropical storm forms — the seven-step sequence

Spec 3.1.1.3 "Causes of tropical storms and the sequence of their formation and development" · AQA GCSE textbook

  • 1. Heating — air is heated above the surface of a warm tropical ocean and rises rapidly under low pressure.
  • 2. Drawing in — the rising air draws up more air and large volumes of moisture, causing strong winds.
  • 3. Spin — the Coriolis effect makes the air spiral upwards around a calm central eye.
  • 4. Condensation — rising air cools and condenses into towering cumulonimbus clouds and torrential rain; the latent heat released powers the storm.
  • 5. The eye — cold air sinks down the centre, so there is no cloud: drier and much calmer.
  • 6. Travel, 7. Decay — it crosses the ocean in the prevailing wind; on reaching land it loses its heat and moisture source and weakens.

✗ "The storm dies when it hits land because of friction." — half the answer.
✓ Friction slows it, but the real reason is that the warm ocean is the fuel: once over land there is no more evaporating water to release latent heat, so the engine stops. Average lifespan is one to two weeks.

The structure and features of a tropical storm

Spec 3.1.1.3 "The structure and features of a tropical storm" · AQA GCSE textbook; Tropical Storms Revision Mat; 2022 mark scheme Q01.4

  • The eye — 16–48 km across, calm, cloudless, warmest and with the lowest air pressure of all.
  • The eye wall — the ring of towering cumulonimbus immediately around it; the strongest winds and heaviest rain in the whole storm.
  • Rain bands — spiralling arms of cloud and rain extending outwards; the whole storm is 240–320 km across.
  • Height — the cloud tops may be more than 13 km up; the storm moves forward at only 16–24 km/h.
  • Rotation — anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere, clockwise in the southern.
  • The passage over you — wind and rain build, then sudden calm and sun in the eye, then wind and rain return violently from the other direction.

✗ Reading features off a satellite photo that cannot actually be seen in it. The 2022 report: "there was no credit for features that couldn't be deduced from the image such as the pattern or speed of winds, the torrential rain or the direction of movement."
✓ From a satellite image you may credit: circular shape, spiralling bands of cloud, anticlockwise rotation, a central cloudless eye, the eye wall, and a measured width using the scale.

Effects — and how a storm kills

Spec 3.1.1.3 "Primary and secondary effects of tropical storms" · AQA GCSE textbook; Tropical Storms Revision Mat

  • Three hazards — strong winds (at least 119 km/h), torrential rain (up to 500 mm in 24 hours), and storm surges.
  • Storm surge — the sea driven up onto the coast by the wind, up to five metres; it causes most of the deaths.
  • Primary — buildings destroyed, people killed and injured, power lines down, crops wiped out, boats destroyed.
  • Secondary — landslides, contaminated water and disease, lost livelihoods, disrupted aid, looting, long-term unemployment.
  • Aid is hampered — flooded and debris-blocked roads mean help arrives slowest exactly where it is needed most.
  • Saffir–Simpson scale — categories 1 to 5, based on wind speed since the 2012 update; Category 5 is above 252 km/h.

Typhoon Haiyan — the Philippines, 3–11 November 2013

The spec requires "a named example of a tropical storm to show its effects and responses". This is John's class's example.

  • Where — the Philippines, worst hit at the city of Tacloban (population 220,000). Palau, Micronesia, Vietnam, Taiwan, Hong Kong and South China were also affected.
  • Physical — Category 5, 170 mph winds, a 5 m storm surge with waves up to 15 m (45 ft), and over 400 mm of rain. One of the strongest storms ever recorded.
  • Formation — sea temperature above 27 °C, forming between 5° and 15° north of the Equator.
  • Primary effects — 6,300 killed (some sources over 7,000), most drowned by the storm surge; 220,000 left homeless; 90% of Tacloban destroyed; 30,000 fishing boats destroyed; Tacloban airport badly damaged; crops and power lines destroyed.
  • Secondary effects — 6 million people lost their source of income and 14 million were affected; road, power, ferry and airport services disrupted, power out for a month in places; shortages of water, food and shelter led to widespread disease; looting and violence broke out in Tacloban; flooding contaminated 71,000 hectares of farmland and an oil tanker spill devastated a mangrove habitat.
  • Immediate responses — international aid from the UK (water and temporary shelters, shelter kits); the Philippines Red Cross delivered canned food, rice, sugar, salt and cooking oil; the US aircraft carrier USS George Washington used helicopters for search and rescue; over 1,200 evacuation centres opened; French, Belgian and Israeli field hospitals.
  • Long-term responses — cash-for-work programmes paid people to clear debris and rebuild; Oxfam funded replacement fishing boats; thousands of homes rebuilt away from flood-risk areas; new cyclone shelters built; roads, bridges and airport rebuilt; rice and fishing restarted quickly but coconut farming takes five years for trees to bear fruit. Economic cost of recovery: $5.8 billion.

Immediate and long-term responses to tropical storms

Spec 3.1.1.3 "Immediate and long-term responses to tropical storms" · Tropical Storms Revision Mat; AQA GCSE textbook; 2024 question paper

  • Immediate — the reaction as the storm hits and in the days afterwards: evacuation, emergency shelters, search and rescue, food, water and medical aid.
  • Evacuation is the headline immediate response — to higher ground away from the surge, or to emergency storm shelters, because the storm was predicted.
  • Aid can be hindered — blocked or flooded roads and damaged airports mean help arrives slowest in the most remote and worst-hit places.
  • Long-term — reactions over months and years: rebuilding homes, infrastructure and businesses, and restoring livelihoods.
  • Long-term is also protection for next time — rebuilding away from flood-risk land, new cyclone shelters, storm-proof construction, better early warning systems.
  • HICs fund their own; LICs depend on aid — which is why in an LIC the long-term stage is the one most likely to stall.

✗ Listing responses without saying when they happened. A "response" with no timescale cannot be sorted into the two categories the question asks about.
✓ Anchor each one: "Immediately, the USS George Washington flew search-and-rescue missions and 1,200 evacuation centres opened. In the long term, cash-for-work programmes paid people to clear debris and Oxfam funded replacement fishing boats."

Reducing the effects — monitoring, prediction, protection, planning

Spec 3.1.1.3 "How monitoring, prediction, protection and planning can reduce the effects" · Tropical Storms Revision Mat; 2025 and 2022 mark schemes

  • Monitoring — satellites, radar and aircraft track the storm; computer models turn that data into a predicted path.
  • Prediction — unlike earthquakes, storms can be predicted; the USA's National Hurricane Centre in Florida tracks every Atlantic and East Pacific storm.
  • Warning buys time — days of notice let people evacuate, board up and stock supplies, which is why deaths fall even when damage does not.
  • Protection — reinforced windows, doors and roofs; hurricane straps; houses on stilts; sea walls; storm drains; concrete cyclone shelters.
  • Planning — evacuation plans and routes, emergency supply kits, drills, education, exclusion zones, restricting building in risk areas.
  • The limit — a storm can change course, so an evacuation may be wasted or sent the wrong way; and in LICs there may be nowhere safe to evacuate to.

✗ "Planning reduces the impact because people are prepared." — the 2022 report: "Generalised or vague answers about the desirability of planning were not credited."
✓ One specific strategy, developed: "Evacuation centres are designated in advance (1), so people know a safe place to go, which minimises loss of life and injury (1)." Note that where the question says "one way", only one strategy is credited — listing more wastes time.

How climate change might affect tropical storms

Spec 3.1.1.3 "How climate change might affect the distribution, frequency and intensity of tropical storms" · AQA GCSE textbook; AQA Revision Notes; Tropical Storms Revision Mat

  • Answer in three parts — the spec names distribution, frequency and intensity, and so does the mark scheme.
  • Distribution: may widen — sea surface temperatures in the tropics have risen 0.25–0.5 °C, so more ocean exceeds 27 °C; the South Atlantic and sub-tropics may become affected.
  • Frequency: same or lower — the overall number is expected to stay the same or decrease.
  • Intensity: higher — warmer oceans mean more energy, so Category 4 and 5 storms increase while categories 1–3 decrease.
  • Uncertainty is part of the answer — accurate wind-speed monitoring is only a few decades old, so older data is less reliable.
  • Risk is rising anyway — population growth and building in coastal locations have increased the potential loss even without climate change.

✗ "Climate change means there will be more hurricanes." — the single most common error, and the textbook says the opposite.
✓ "More severe rather than more frequent: the overall number is expected to stay the same or fall, but the proportion that reach Categories 4 and 5 is expected to rise, because warmer oceans supply more energy."

Weather hazards in the UK

Spec 3.1.1.3 "An overview of types of weather hazard experienced in the UK" · UK Weather Hazards Revision Mat; AQA GCSE textbook; AQA List of Key Terms

  • Extreme weather defined — weather significantly different from the average or usual pattern, especially severe or unseasonal.
  • Four types — storm events, flooding, droughts and heatwaves, and extremes of cold weather.
  • They come from four directions — severe winter weather from the east, hot and dry from the south, heavy snow and bitter cold from the north, heavy rain and strong winds from the west.
  • Depressions — low-pressure systems off the Atlantic bring the heavy rain and strong winds; when they stop coming, cold conditions take over.
  • Drought, UK definition — fifteen consecutive days with less than 0.2 mm of rain on any one day.
  • There are benefits too — more tourism and staycations, and a wider variety of crops such as grapes for wine.

Records worth quoting. The longest UK drought on record ran 18 months across 1975–76, when Cheltenham exceeded 32 °C for seven successive days. The 2003 drought brought 38 °C at Faversham, Kent, and across Europe was linked with 20,000 deaths. In 2022 the UK recorded 40.3 °C and the driest nine-month period since 1916, with the South East receiving just 74% of its long-term average rainfall from November to July. The winters of 1946–47, 1962–63 and 1978–79 were exceptionally cold; in January 2014 over 17,000 trains were cancelled because of freezing conditions.

The Beast from the East, March 2018 — useful as a second UK example. A stratospheric warming event disturbed the jet stream, allowing cold winds from Russia to reach the UK; the air mass picked up moisture over the North Sea and produced heavy snowfall. It cost the UK £1 billion per day as transport was disrupted and businesses and schools closed. Red weather warnings were issued, Devon and Cornwall police declared a major incident, the RAF helped relief efforts in Lincolnshire and the military rescued vehicles on the M62.

An extreme weather event in the UK — the Somerset Levels

Spec 3.1.1.3 "An example of a recent extreme weather event in the UK: causes; social, economic and environmental impacts; how management strategies can reduce risk" · Case Study Summary Booklet; AQA Revision Notes

  • Three causes, only two of them weather — Atlantic storms, storm surges up the Bristol Channel, and twenty years without dredging.
  • Social — 600 houses flooded, 16 farms evacuated, months in temporary accommodation, villages cut off, power lost.
  • Economic — £150 million overall; 80 roads closed costing the local economy £15 million; the Bristol-to-Taunton railway severed.
  • Environmental — 14,000 acres underwater for 3–4 weeks, contaminated with sewage, oil and chemicals; stagnant water had to be reoxygenated.
  • Management targets the human cause — a £20 million Flood Action Plan whose centrepiece is dredging, plus raised roads and banks and upgraded pumps.
  • The big-ticket option — a planned £80 million tidal barrier at Bridgwater to protect 11,500 homes.

The Somerset Levels floods — December 2013 to February 2014

The spec requires one recent UK extreme weather event covering causes, social, economic and environmental impacts, and management. This is John's class's example.

  • Where — north-east of Taunton, bordered by the Bristol Channel, flat low-lying farmland drained by rivers flowing into the Severn Estuary.
  • Causes — a quick succession of Atlantic storms brought 100 mm above average rainfall (the wettest January since records began); high tides and storm surges pushed water up the Bristol Channel, preventing river water from reaching the sea; and the drains and rivers had not been dredged for 20 years, so they were full of sediment and held far less water.
  • Social — 600 houses flooded, 16 farms evacuated, residents in temporary accommodation for several months, several villages cut off, power supplies cut.
  • Economic — £150 million overall, of which £20 million was residential; 80 roads closed, costing the local economy £15 million; the Bristol-to-Taunton railway cut.
  • Environmental — 14,000 acres of farmland damaged and underwater for 3–4 weeks; over 1,000 livestock evacuated; floodwaters contaminated with sewage, oil and chemicals; a huge volume of debris to clear; stagnant water had to be reoxygenated before being returned to the rivers.
  • Management — a £20 million Flood Action Plan: 8 km of the River Parrett and River Tone dredged at a cost of £6 million, then a programme of annual dredging; road levels raised; riverbanks raised and strengthened; old diesel pumping stations upgraded; and a planned £80 million tidal barrier at Bridgwater to protect 11,500 homes.

Is UK weather becoming more extreme?

Spec 3.1.1.3 "Evidence that weather is becoming more extreme in the UK" · AQA GCSE textbook; UK Weather Hazards Revision Mat; 2022 mark scheme Q01.8

  • Temperature — UK temperatures have risen by about 1 °C since 1980, and more records have been broken recently than ever before.
  • Rainfall — more winter rain has fallen in heavy events since the 1980s, increasing the frequency and magnitude of river flooding.
  • Named events — Somerset Levels 2014, Beast from the East 2018, Storms Ciara and Dennis 2020, Cumbria 2009, Shrewsbury 2020, the 2022 heatwave at 40.3 °C.
  • The mechanism — a warmer Atlantic gives rain-bearing depressions more energy and moisture.
  • The counter-argument you must include — all weather is subject to great variability, and a handful of recent events is not a trend.
  • Predictions — warmer, wetter winters, more depressions reaching all parts of the UK, and greater risk of extreme flooding and high winds.

✗ Agreeing with the statement and then listing the impacts of extreme weather. The 2022 report: "Others restricted their comments to the general impacts of extreme weather… rather than on evidence supporting the idea that the UK climate is becoming more extreme."
✓ The question is about evidence of a trend. Quote the trend data (1 °C since 1980; heavier winter rainfall since the 1980s), name the events, then acknowledge natural variability — and make a judgement.

✗ Straying into tropical storms and tornadoes. Keep it in the UK.

The 9-mark frame — weather hazards

Section A's 9-marker is +3 SPaG and is level-marked. For this topic it has been "Discuss: both immediate and long-term responses are needed" (2024) and "Assess the extent to which tropical storms have effects on people and the environment" (2018). Both need Typhoon Haiyan with figures and evaluation inside every paragraph.

  • Intro — one sentence naming the example and your line. "In November 2013 a Category 5 super typhoon, Haiyan, struck the Philippines with 170 mph winds, felt most in Tacloban city; its effects on people were more severe than those on the environment, though both were considerable."
  • Paragraph 1 — effects on people, evaluated. "6,300 were killed and 220,000 left homeless, and 90% of Tacloban was destroyed. More significantly, 6 million people lost their source of income, so the secondary effect outlasted the primary one by years."
  • Paragraph 2 — the economy as the hinge. "30,000 fishing boats were destroyed — a primary economic effect which had a more significant longer-term impact, as jobs and one of the main sources of protein disappeared together. Recovery cost $5.8 billion."
  • Paragraph 3 — the environment, the one most candidates skip. "Impacts on the environment are often overlooked but should not be understated: widespread flooding contaminated 71,000 hectares of farmland and an oil tanker spill devastated a fragile mangrove habitat. However, mangroves regenerate, whereas the 6,300 deaths are permanent."
  • Conclusion that decides in two sentences. "Overall, the extent of the impact on people was greater, because the media-reported death toll understates the 6 million livelihoods lost. The environmental damage was severe but recoverable, which is why the human cost should rank first."

03Understanding it

U1

Why are there rainforests on the Equator and deserts on the Tropics, when both are hot?

Show answer

Because of what the air is doing vertically, not how hot it is.

At the Equator the Sun's energy is concentrated on the smallest area, so air is heated, becomes less dense and rises. Rising air creates low pressure, and as it rises it cools, condenses and forms cloud — hence torrential daily rainfall and tropical rainforest.

That air travels polewards in the upper atmosphere, losing its heat and moisture. At 30° north and south it is cool, dense and dry, so it sinks, creating high pressure. Sinking air warms and can hold more moisture, so cloud cannot form — hence cloudless skies, almost no rainfall and hot deserts.

The exam point: this single mechanism links Section A to the Living World. "Rising = wet, sinking = dry" answers questions in both topics.

U2

A tropical storm needs sea above 27 °C. Why does that temperature also explain where, when and how violently storms happen?

Show answer

Because the warm sea is the fuel. Water evaporating from a 27 °C ocean carries huge amounts of energy; when that vapour rises, cools and condenses into cumulonimbus cloud, the latent heat released powers the storm. No warm sea, no energy.

Where: only within about 5°–30° of the Equator is the ocean that warm, and warm to a depth of 100 m rather than just at the surface — which is why South America's cold west coast almost never gets storms.

When: only in summer and autumn, when the sea has had months to warm.

How violently: the warmer the water, the more energy available, which is why the Saffir–Simpson category tracks sea temperature — and why climate change is expected to increase the intensity of storms.

And why they die: the moment a storm crosses the coast, the fuel supply stops.

U3

If the eye of the storm is calm and sunny, why is it the most dangerous moment for people on the ground?

Show answer

Because people think it is over. In the eye, cold air sinks, so there is no cloud, the Sun appears, the wind drops to nothing and air pressure is at its lowest. The storm is 240–320 km across and moving at only 16–24 km/h, so that calm can last an hour or more.

Then the far side of the eye wall arrives — the strongest winds and heaviest rain in the entire storm — and because the storm rotates, those winds now come from the opposite direction, hitting structures already weakened from the other side.

Use it like this: this is why planning and education count as management strategies in their own right. Knowing to stay inside through the eye is a decision that saves lives with no money spent.

U4

Climate change makes the oceans warmer. Why doesn't that simply mean more tropical storms?

Show answer

Because the number of storms and the strength of storms are controlled by different things. Storm formation needs several conditions at once — warm deep water, low wind shear, enough distance from the Equator for the Coriolis effect — and warming the ocean improves only one of them, while potentially increasing wind shear.

So the forecast splits three ways: frequency is expected to stay the same or decrease; intensity is expected to rise, with more Category 4 and 5 storms and fewer in categories 1–3; and distribution may widen slightly, since tropical sea surface temperatures have risen 0.25–0.5 °C so more ocean clears the 27 °C threshold — the South Atlantic and sub-tropics are the candidates.

The honest caveat, which earns AO3: accurate wind-speed monitoring is only a few decades old, so comparing with older records is unreliable. And the risk to people has already risen anyway, because of population growth and building in coastal locations — a trend that has nothing to do with the climate.

U5

Why can tropical storms be predicted when earthquakes cannot, and what difference does that make?

Show answer

A tropical storm is visible for days before it arrives. Satellites, radar and reconnaissance aircraft monitor it over open ocean; computer models turn that data into a predicted track. The USA's National Hurricane Centre in Florida tracks every Atlantic and East Pacific storm. An earthquake happens in seconds, underground, with no reliable precursor.

The difference it makes: warning converts into evacuation, and evacuation saves lives. That is why Haiyan killed 6,300 while 1,200 evacuation centres sheltered many more, and why Bangladesh's concrete cyclone shelters and early warning systems have cut cyclone death tolls so sharply.

The limit, which is the evaluation: prediction tells you a storm is coming, not what it will do. A cyclone can change course after people have been moved; Cyclone Amphan's forecast track in 2020 covered a wide "area most likely to be affected". And prediction is worthless if there is nowhere safe to go, which is a wealth question rather than a science one.

U6

Why was the Somerset Levels flood partly a human-made disaster?

Show answer

Three causes combined, and only two of them were weather.

Meteorological: a quick succession of Atlantic depressions delivered 100 mm above average rainfall in the wettest January since records began; and high tides and storm surges pushed water up the Bristol Channel, so the rivers could not discharge and spilled over their banks instead.

Human: the rivers and drainage ditches had not been dredged for 20 years, so they were choked with sediment and their channel capacity was far below what it had been. The same rainfall on a dredged system would have produced a smaller flood.

Why this matters for the mark scheme: it explains why the management response is dominated by dredging — 8 km of the Parrett and Tone for £6 million, then a programme of annual dredging. The management fixes the human cause, because that is the only one that can be fixed.

U7

Both the Somerset Levels and Typhoon Haiyan were water disasters. Why do their impact lists look so different?

Show answer

Magnitude: a Category 5 typhoon with a 5 m storm surge is an entirely different force from a winter flood on flat farmland.

Wealth and preparation: the UK is an HIC with warnings, insurance, emergency services and a £20 million action plan ready afterwards. Somerset lost no lives; its impacts are measured in £150 million, 600 flooded houses and 14,000 acres of damaged farmland. Haiyan killed 6,300 and left 6 million without an income.

Warning time and escape: in Somerset people were evacuated to temporary accommodation and villages used boats to reach the shops. In Tacloban, 90% of the city was destroyed and the evacuation centres themselves flooded.

Recovery: Somerset's railway and roads reopened within weeks and the government funded the plan. In the Philippines, coconut farming — a major livelihood — takes five years to bear fruit again.

Use it like this: the pair is the cleanest evidence that wealth converts a human disaster into a financial one — the same argument the tectonics 9-marker turns on.

U8

Is UK weather actually becoming more extreme, or do we just notice it more?

Show answer

Evidence that it is: UK temperatures have risen by about 1 °C since 1980; more winter rain has fallen in heavy events since the 1980s, which has increased the frequency and magnitude of river flooding; more records have been broken recently than ever before, including 40.3 °C in 2022; and the mechanism is plausible — a warmer Atlantic gives rain-bearing depressions more energy and moisture. The Met Office and the Hadley Centre say recent storms may already carry "human fingerprints".

Evidence that we may be over-reading it: all weather is subject to great natural variability, and the UK has always had extreme events — the 1975–76 drought and the winters of 1946–47 and 1962–63 were worse than anything recent. A handful of recent events is not a trend; you need long-term records. And high temperatures alone do not cause drought, which also requires a rainfall deficit, so not every hot year is an extreme one.

The judgement worth writing: partly agree. The temperature and heavy winter rainfall trends are supported by long records and have a physical explanation, so the evidence there is strong. The evidence for more frequent storms and droughts is weaker, because variability is high and the record is short.

04Exam drill

What each command word actually wants

Weather questions carry more of Section A's skills marks than any other topic — measuring a curved distance on a map, completing a bar, reading a satellite image, calculating a mean or a median. Those are free marks if you practise them and lost marks if you do not.

  • Calculate. Show the working. A median between two values caught out a large minority in 2023; a mean caught out others in 2024 and 2025. Read whether the answer wants one decimal place.
  • Measure. The spec includes measuring curved line distances at a variety of scales. In 2022 answers ranged from 0.002 km to 250,000 km because students measured straight lines or misread the scale.
  • Identify two features / give two reasons. Two separate points. Only what can actually be deduced from the source counts.
  • Outline (2). One point plus one development. Where the question says "one way", a list of several earns nothing extra.
  • Suggest. Apply your own understanding to the source — do not copy the key or the labels back.
  • Explain (4 or 6). Level-marked. Develop with "this means that…", "this leads to…", "as a result…". No judgement needed.
  • Do you agree? / To what extent? (6). Two or three paragraphs, evidence and explanation, plus a supported judgement. Use the figure and your own material.
  • Discuss / Assess (9 + 3 SPaG). Both sides, a named example with figures, evaluation inside each paragraph, and a conclusion that ranks. Always write something — a genuine attempt earns SPaG marks even at zero content.

Twelve questions in the real AQA spread: two short, four 4-markers, four 6-markers and two 9-markers. The 6- and 9-mark answers are plans — AQA marks those in levels, so the mark points are a coverage target rather than a tick list.

D1

Outline one reason why the number of deaths caused by tropical storms varies. (2)

Show answer

The wealth or development level of the country affected varies 1, which means HICs can afford early warning systems, storm-proof buildings and organised evacuation, so far fewer people are exposed when the storm arrives 1

Also accept, each as point + development: the strength of the storm (a Category 5 produces a far larger storm surge, which is what drowns most victims); the preparedness of the area (drills and shelters); and population density on the coast (more people in the surge zone).

The 2023 report: "Some students mentioned 2 separate points but with no development so could only achieve 1 mark." One reason, developed — not two reasons, bare.

D2

State three types of extreme weather experienced in the UK. (3)

Show answer

Storm events — heavy rain and strong winds from Atlantic depressions 1

Flooding 1

Droughts and heatwaves — also accept extremes of cold weather, heavy snow, or thunderstorms 1

Three distinct, focused points. No development needed at 3 marks.

D3

Explain how the effects of tropical storms can be reduced. (4)

Show answer

Monitoring and prediction: scientists use data from satellites, radar and aircraft to track a storm over the ocean, and computer models calculate its predicted path 1; this indicates where and when it will make landfall, which gives people days of warning to evacuate and to protect their homes and businesses — the USA's National Hurricane Centre in Florida tracks every Atlantic storm this way 1

Protection: buildings can be fitted with reinforced windows, doors and roofs and hurricane straps, built on stilts above the surge level, and defended by sea walls and storm drains 1; these reduce the damage done by winds of over 119 km/h and by a storm surge of up to five metres, so fewer homes are destroyed and fewer people are injured or made homeless 1

The mark scheme allows full marks for a developed response on any one strategy. Two developed strategies is the safer route, but four unconnected sentences naming all four strategies is Level 1.

D4

Explain why there is low pressure at the Equator and high pressure at 30° north and south. (4)

Show answer

The Equator receives the most intense solar heating because the Sun is overhead and its energy is concentrated on the smallest area 1

The air there is heated, becomes less dense and rises; rising air leaves less air pressing down on the surface, which creates a belt of low pressure, and as the air rises it cools and condenses to form cloud and heavy rainfall 1

That air travels polewards at the top of the troposphere, losing its remaining heat and moisture along the way 1

At about 30° north and south this cool, dense, dry air sinks back towards the surface; sinking air piles up and presses down, creating a belt of high pressure with cloudless skies and very low rainfall — which is why the hot deserts are found there 1

D5

Explain how the structure of a tropical storm produces its most destructive effects. (4)

Show answer

Around the eye is the eye wall, a ring of towering cumulonimbus cloud where air is rising most rapidly 1; this is where the strongest winds — at least 119 km/h and up to 170 mph in Haiyan — and the heaviest rainfall occur, so it destroys buildings, power lines and crops as it passes 1

The extremely low pressure in the eye, combined with winds pushing seawater towards the coast, raises sea level as a storm surge of up to five metres 1; the surge floods low-lying coastal land and is the cause of death for the majority of victims — in Tacloban it drowned most of the 6,300 people Haiyan killed 1

D6

Suggest how climate change might affect the distribution, frequency and intensity of tropical storms. (4)

Show answer

Distribution. Sea surface temperatures in the tropics have already risen by 0.25–0.5 °C, so more ocean exceeds the 27 °C threshold needed for formation 1; this means storms may in future affect areas that rarely see them, such as the South Atlantic and parts of the sub-tropics, though the main regions are not expected to change significantly 1

Frequency and intensity. The overall frequency of tropical storms is expected to stay the same or even decrease 1; however, intensity is expected to increase, with more Category 4 and 5 storms and fewer in categories 1–3, because warmer oceans supply more heat energy to the storm — although there is considerable uncertainty, since accurate wind-speed monitoring is only a few decades old 1

D7

Suggest how extreme weather in the UK can have social and economic impacts. (6)

Show answer

Level-marked. Separate the two categories explicitly — the 2023 report notes that a minority confuse economic with environmental. Use a named UK event.

Social — homes and health. Flooding forces people out of their homes: in the Somerset Levels in winter 2013–14, 600 houses were flooded and 16 farms evacuated, with residents in temporary accommodation for several months 1; this means lost possessions, disrupted schooling and, because floodwater was contaminated with sewage, oil and chemicals, a real risk to health 1

Social — isolation and vulnerability. Several Somerset villages were cut off and power supplies were lost, so people could not reach work, school or medical care 1; in the opposite extreme, heatwaves leave elderly people vulnerable to heat exhaustion and death — the 2003 European heatwave was linked with 20,000 deaths 1

Economic. The Somerset floods cost around £150 million overall, with 80 roads closed costing the local economy a further £15 million, and the Bristol-to-Taunton railway severed 1; as a result businesses lost trade, 14,000 acres of farmland was unusable for weeks and over 1,000 livestock had to be moved, while nationally the Beast from the East of 2018 cost the UK about £1 billion per day as transport failed and businesses and schools closed 1

D8

"UK weather is becoming more extreme." Do you agree? (6)

Show answer

Level-marked, and the command is "do you agree", so the whole answer is an evaluation. Fully agree, partly agree or disagree — but decide.

Evidence for — temperature. UK temperatures have increased by about 1 °C since 1980, and more records have been broken recently than ever before, with 40.3 °C recorded in 2022 and the driest nine-month period since 1916 1; the South East received just 74% of its long-term average rainfall from November to July that year, triggering hosepipe bans and health alerts, which is evidence of a trend rather than a single bad summer 1

Evidence for — rainfall and storms. More winter rain has fallen in heavy events since the 1980s, which has increased the frequency and magnitude of river flooding, and the suggested cause is increased warming of the Atlantic giving rain-bearing depressions more energy and moisture 1; named events support this — Somerset Levels 2014, Cumbria 2009, Storms Ciara and Dennis in 2020, and the Beast from the East in 2018 1

Evidence against. All weather is subject to great natural variability, and the UK has always had extremes: the longest drought on record was 1975–76, and the winters of 1946–47 and 1962–63 were colder than anything since 1; a handful of recent events is not a trend, and high temperatures alone do not create drought, so long-term records rather than memorable events are what the judgement must rest on 1

Judgement. Partly agree. The temperature and heavy-winter-rainfall trends are supported by century-long Met Office records and have a plausible physical mechanism, so on those measures UK weather is becoming more extreme; the evidence for more frequent storms and droughts is weaker because variability is high.

D9

To what extent can protection and prediction strategies reduce the effects of tropical storms? (6)

Show answer

Level-marked, and "to what extent" demands evaluation throughout. The 2023 report praised answers that discussed "the problems with using prediction where the tropical cyclone can change course".

Prediction works, and saves lives. Satellites, radar and aircraft monitor a storm for days over open ocean and computer models calculate its predicted path, so warnings can be broadcast in advance 1; this converts directly into evacuation — over 1,200 evacuation centres were opened before Haiyan, and Bangladesh's early warning systems have cut cyclone death tolls sharply 1

Protection works against the physical forces. Concrete cyclone shelters raised above surge level, reinforced roofs and hurricane straps, houses on stilts and sea walls all reduce damage from winds and from a five-metre surge 1; this means fewer buildings collapse and fewer people drown, which matters because the surge causes most tropical storm deaths 1

The limits — this is the AO3. A storm can change course after an evacuation has begun, so the warning may send people the wrong way, and forecast tracks cover a wide "area most likely to be affected" rather than a point 1; more fundamentally, both strategies cost money, so their effectiveness tracks wealth — Haiyan still killed 6,300 and destroyed 90% of Tacloban despite being predicted, because an LIC could not build enough shelters or rehouse people away from the coast beforehand 1

Judgement. They reduce the death toll substantially but the damage only partly, and their effectiveness depends on a country's wealth and on people acting on the warning — so to a considerable extent in an HIC, and far less in an LIC.

D10

Suggest why some tropical storms have severe primary and secondary effects. (6)

Show answer

Level-marked. Three reasons, each exemplified. Use Haiyan as the worked case.

The magnitude of the storm itself. A Category 5 storm has winds over 252 km/h; Haiyan's reached 170 mph, with a five-metre surge and waves up to 15 m, and over 400 mm of rain 1; this means the eye wall destroys almost everything in its path — 90% of Tacloban was flattened — so the primary effects are catastrophic before any other factor is considered 1

Where it makes landfall. A storm that crosses a densely populated, low-lying coastline causes far more harm than one that stays at sea: Tacloban is a city of 220,000 on the coast, so the surge reached the maximum number of people 1; the same storm over open ocean would have had almost no effects at all 1

Wealth, preparation and what the damage destroys. In an LIC, buildings are weaker, warnings reach fewer people and there is nowhere safe to evacuate to, so primary effects are larger 1; the secondary effects then compound, because the storm destroys the very things needed to recover — 30,000 fishing boats meant 6 million people lost their income, flooded roads and a damaged airport delayed aid, and shortages of water, food and shelter caused widespread disease and then looting and violence 1

D11

"Both immediate and long-term responses are needed after a tropical storm."

Discuss this statement. Use an example of a tropical storm you have studied. (9)

Show answer

Written as a plan, level-marked. "Discuss" means present both sides, make multiple points on each, then finish with a clear conclusion that links back. A named example with figures is required for Level 3.

Intro. In November 2013 Typhoon Haiyan, a Category 5 storm with 170 mph winds, struck the Philippines and devastated Tacloban; its aftermath shows that immediate and long-term responses do different jobs, and that neither alone is sufficient 1

Immediate responses — keeping people alive. International aid arrived quickly: the UK sent water, shelter kits and temporary shelters, the Philippines Red Cross delivered canned food, rice, sugar, salt and cooking oil, and French, Belgian and Israeli field hospitals treated the injured 1

Search and rescue needed military capacity: the US aircraft carrier USS George Washington flew helicopter missions, which mattered because roads were blocked and Tacloban airport was badly damaged 1

Over 1,200 evacuation centres housed people whose homes were among the 90% of Tacloban destroyed; without this, far more than 6,300 would have died in the following weeks from exposure and from the disease spreading through contaminated water 1

Long-term responses — rebuilding the means of living. Cash-for-work programmes paid people to clear debris and rebuild, which restored both the city and an income at the same time 1

Oxfam funded replacement fishing boats, directly addressing the primary loss of 30,000 boats that had cost 6 million people their source of income — something no amount of emergency food could fix 1

Thousands of homes were rebuilt away from flood-risk areas and new cyclone shelters were built, which reduces the effects of the next storm rather than only this one 1

Evaluation — why neither alone works. Immediate responses alone would have left 6 million people without a livelihood and the city rebuilt in the same unsafe place; long-term responses alone are meaningless if people die in the first week, and recovery is slow regardless — coconut trees take five years to bear fruit and total recovery cost $5.8 billion 1

Conclusion. I agree with the statement: immediate responses determine how many people survive, while long-term responses determine whether survival is worth anything and whether the next storm is less deadly — but if one had to be ranked first, the immediate response matters more, because long-term rebuilding is only possible for the people the first week saves 1

D12

Assess the extent to which tropical storms have effects on people and the environment. Use an example you have studied. (9)

Show answer

Written as a plan, level-marked. "Assess the extent" requires a judgement of how large the effects are and which are greater, ranked in the conclusion. The school's full-marks model answer runs people first, then environment, evaluating throughout.

Intro naming the example and the line. In November 2013 Typhoon Haiyan, a Category 5 storm, struck the Philippines with 170 mph winds and a five-metre surge; the extent of its effects on people and the environment was severe in both cases, but the effects on people were greater and longer-lasting 1

Effects on people — the immediate, headline scale. 6,300 people were killed, most drowned by the storm surge, 220,000 were left homeless and 90% of Tacloban was destroyed 1

The media inevitably focus on the death toll, which influences our perspective on the scale of a disaster — but the deeper effect was economic: 30,000 fishing boats were destroyed and 6 million people lost their source of income 1

Secondary effects compounded this: power was cut for over a month, food and water shortages led to widespread disease, and looting and violence broke out in Tacloban, so the impact on people continued long after the wind stopped 1

Recovery cost $5.8 billion and will affect people for years to come, since coconut farming takes five years to restart 1

Effects on the environment — often overlooked, but real. Widespread flooding from over 400 mm of rain contaminated 71,000 hectares of farmland with salt water and debris 1

An oil tanker spill devastated a wide area of environmentally significant and fragile mangrove habitat — which matters doubly, because mangroves are themselves a natural defence against storm surges 1

Evaluation — what determines the extent. The extent to which people and the environment are affected is determined by the magnitude of the storm, the wealth of the country, population density and the level of preparation; Haiyan scored badly on all four, which is why its effects were at the extreme end rather than typical of tropical storms generally 1

Conclusion that ranks. Overall the effects on people were the greater, because 6 million lost livelihoods outweigh habitat damage that will regenerate, and the 6,300 deaths are irreversible in a way the mangroves are not — though the environmental effects should not be understated, since contaminated farmland prolonged the food shortage that harmed people in turn 1
