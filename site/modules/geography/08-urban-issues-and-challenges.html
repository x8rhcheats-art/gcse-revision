01How it actually works

Learn which of Rio and London is carrying the 9-marker in a given year by reading the last question first.

Global urbanisation — the process, the pattern and the numbers

Spec 3.2.1.1 · Urban Issues and Challenges revision, slides 3–4; Urban Revision Mat

  • Urbanisation is a proportion, not a total — the rising percentage of a country's population living in towns and cities.
  • Over 55% of the world now lives in urban areas — and 90% of the increase will be in Africa and Asia.
  • HICs urbanised first — during the Industrial Revolution; North America is now 82% urban, HICs at least 75%.
  • Growth is fastest in LICs and NEEs — India, China and Nigeria alone account for 37% of urban growth 2014–2050.
  • HIC rates are slowing — some are counter-urbanising, with people leaving crowded cities for the countryside.
  • Two factors only — the rate of urbanisation is set by migration and natural increase, and examiners want both named.

✗ "Urbanisation is when cities get bigger." — that is urban growth, and the 1-marker in 2025 was marked on the exact wording.
✓ "Urbanisation is the increase in the proportion of a country's population living in towns and cities." A rural population can still be rising while urbanisation happens.

More detail

Urbanisation is the process by which an increasing percentage of a country's population comes to live in towns and cities. Rapid urbanisation is a feature of many LICs and NEEs. Keep it separate from urban growth, which is the absolute increase in the size of a city — examiners reward the precise term.

The global picture, in the figures the school's slides give: 55%+ of the world's population lives in urban areas (UN, 2023); there were 33 megacities worldwide in 2023 and 50 are expected by 2050; and 90% of the increase in the world's urban population will be in Africa and Asia.

Why urbanisation happened first in HICs. It began during the industrial revolution. People were pulled to urban areas from rural areas to work in factories, and they were pushed as developments in technology led to mechanisation on farms.

The pattern in HICs today. HICs such as the USA, most of Europe, Australia and Japan are highly urbanised, with at least 75% of the population living in urban areas. North America is the most urbanised region, with 82% of its population in urban areas. The rate of urbanisation in HICs is slowing down, and counter-urbanisation is occurring in some HICs as people move from overcrowded urban areas to more attractive rural environments.

Four reasons the school gives for the counter-urbanisation trend: (1) car ownership has risen over the last 40 years so people are more mobile, and information technology means more people can work from home; (2) urban areas are becoming increasingly unpleasant places to live, through pollution, crime and traffic congestion; (3) more people move when they retire; (4) new business parks on greenfield sites at the edge of cities mean people no longer have to travel to the city centre, so they prefer to live on the outskirts near where they work.

Why rates are higher in LICs and NEEs. As LICs develop, people are pushed from the countryside as mechanisation leads to fewer jobs. People are pulled to cities by the growth of jobs in urban industries, and are also attracted by higher living standards, education and better healthcare. India, China and Nigeria are expected to account for 37% of the growth in the world's urban population between 2014 and 2050.

Why cities grow — push, pull and natural increase

Spec 3.2.1.1 · Urban Revision Mat (with answers); 2025 Q01.4 mark scheme

  • Two causes, always both — rural–urban migration, and natural increase (birth rate minus death rate).
  • Push factors — no well-paid work, poor transport, no sanitation, limited schooling, drought, soil erosion, poor harvests, machines replacing farm jobs.
  • Pull factors — better-paid jobs, a big market, better education and healthcare, fresh water and sanitation, family already there, better transport.
  • Migration causes the natural increase — migrants are typically 18–35, so the birth rate is high and the death rate low.
  • The exam link to make — a low median age means more people of reproductive age, so more children swell the population further.
  • HIC growth is slower — earlier industrialisation, earlier urbanisation, access to contraception, and lower birth rates.

✗ "People move to cities for a better life." — one undeveloped assertion; the 2-markers need point plus development.
✓ "People are pushed from rural areas by poor harvests (1); the lack of food and produce to sell forces them to the city, where food supply and jobs are more regular (1)."

More detail

The two factors that affect the rate of urbanisation are migration, as people move from rural to urban areas, and natural increase, as birth rates are higher than death rates in urban areas. The link between them is the one that earns the top marks: migrants are typically aged 18–35, which produces a high birth rate and a low death rate, so the city grows twice over.

Push factors (rural areas): limited opportunities for well-paid employment; poor transport infrastructure; lack of sanitation; limited opportunities to get a good education; environmental issues such as drought, soil erosion and desertification; poor harvests resulting in limited food; agricultural jobs being replaced by machinery; limited services such as fresh water, health care and electricity.

Pull factors (urban areas): employment opportunities in better-paid jobs; a large market for goods and services; improved education and healthcare provision; better services such as fresh water and sanitation; family and friends who may have already moved; better public transport provision; a higher standard of living due to higher wages and higher quality housing with services.

The 2025 mark scheme for the 4-marker on exactly this shows what "application" means. From the figure: a low median age and a weekly growth figure of many thousands. From your own understanding: the low median age means young people of reproductive age, so larger numbers of children swelling the population; the weekly figure shows rapid growth — and manipulating that figure further (dividing it to give a daily total) is specifically credited as evidence you understand the scale. The scheme also credits consequences: a youthful population provides a supply of factory workers; rapid growth creates resource and housing pressure; urban sprawl and informal settlements grow.

For the HIC version, the 2022 examiner's report says 1 mark was widespread for access to contraception, with better answers developing this to lower birth rates, and the best answers showed understanding of earlier industrialisation, urbanisation and urban–rural migration.

Megacities, world cities and primate cities

Spec 3.2.1.1 · Urban Revision Mat; Case Study Summary Booklet quiz facts

  • Megacity — an urban area with a population of at least 10 million. Learn the number, not "very big".
  • 33 in 2023, around 50 expected by 2050 — and the new ones will be overwhelmingly in Africa and Asia.
  • Most are in LICs/NEEs — in 2014, at least 21 megacities were in LICs/NEEs against just 7 in MICs.
  • World city — a city with global influence in finance, trade, culture or politics; London is an "Alpha++" world city.
  • Primate city — a city that dominates its country, usually more than twice the size of the next largest.
  • Examples to name — Tokyo, Mumbai, São Paulo, Lagos, Dhaka, Kinshasa, Manila, Jakarta.
More detail

A megacity is a city with a population of at least 10 million people. The majority of the world's megacities are located in LICs and NEEs, particularly in Asia and Africa. The school's figures: 33 megacities in 2023, rising to around 50 by 2050, with 90% of the world's increased urban population in Africa and Asia.

The 2022 Paper 2 Figure 1 was a world map of growth rates per week for named cities, which is worth knowing as a reference list of megacities and their growth: Lagos +85, Dhaka +74, Kinshasa +63, Mumbai +51, Manila +29, Jakarta +27, Mexico City +22, São Paulo +18, Rio de Janeiro +10, New York +10, Berlin +1 — the HIC/LIC contrast in one figure.

The spec also names world cities and primate cities. A world city has influence over the whole globe through its financial, cultural and political reach — London and New York are the two standard examples. A primate city dominates its own country's urban hierarchy.

Rio de Janeiro — the facts and figures

  • Location. South-east Brazil, on the Atlantic coast, grown up around Guanabara Bay, a large natural bay. Brazil is a Newly Emerging Economy and the world's 12th largest economy.
  • Population. Greater Rio de Janeiro area 13 million; around 1.5 million people live in favelas, which the school's booklet puts at 24% of the population across roughly 1,000 favelas. Population is mostly under 30.
  • Inequality. Gini coefficient around 0.53 — very high.

Rio — the opportunities urban growth created

Spec 3.2.1.2 · Case Study Summary Booklet 2026; Urban revision slides

  • Health — free Unified Health care, more doctors per 1,000 than Brazil as a whole (3.44 v 2.81).
  • Education — over 400 secondary schools and 6 universities; literacy 97% against a national 93%.
  • Water — the Guandu plant is the largest water treatment works in the world and supplies most of Rio's water.
  • Energy — 99% of homes have electricity; the Simplício HEP scheme added 30% of Rio's supply in 2013.
  • Formal jobs — tertiary work in retail and public services raises disposable income and tax revenue, driving the multiplier effect.
  • Industry and port — Ternium Steelworks employs favela residents; the port exports coffee, sugar and iron ore.

Rio de Janeiro — opportunities, with the figures

  • Healthcare. Unified Health provides free health care across the city, with 3 public and 6 private hospitals. Rio has 3.44 doctors per 1,000 people against Brazil's 2.81. Travelling health care clinics in Santa Maria bring staff to people's homes; Family Health Teams reach around 3,000 people.
  • Education. Over 400 secondary schools and 6 universities; education compulsory to 14. Literacy is 97% in Rio against a national average of 93%. A private university has been set up in Rocinha; local government grants support families with children at school; the Schools of Tomorrow programme runs practical skills-based courses in poor, violent areas; the NGO Alfazendo prepares children in Cidade de Deus for college exams.
  • Water. The Guandu treatment plant is the largest in the world and supplies the great majority of Rio's fresh water. 7 new water treatment plants were built and over 300 km of water pipes laid between 1998 and 2014.
  • Energy. 99% of homes have electricity. 60 km of new power cables installed; 2 new nuclear reactors; the Simplício hydro plant, built at a cost of $2 billion and opened in 2013, supplies 30% of Rio's energy; 150 solar panels in the Santa Marta favela; the NGO Revolusolar has installed panels in Babilônia.
  • Economy. Growth in the formal economy, especially tertiary jobs in retail and public services, raises disposable income and tax revenue and triggers the multiplier effect. Oil has been discovered offshore. The Ternium Steelworks in the west of the city employs favela residents. The port is Brazil's third largest, handling coffee, sugar and iron ore exports.
  • Transport. The metro has been expanded under Guanabara Bay; the Olympics brought metro Line 4 connecting Barra da Tijuca to the city centre and the VLT light rail through the regenerated Porto Maravilha district.

Rio — the challenges urban growth created

Spec 3.2.1.2 · Case Study Summary Booklet 2026; Urban revision slides

  • Housing — around 1,000 favelas, built on steep hillsides, so landslide and flood risk is high.
  • Health gap — favela residents live about 13 years less; infant mortality 21/1000 in Cidade de Deus against 9/1000 in Barra.
  • Education gap — only 50% continue beyond 14; Rio das Pedras has 50,000 people and 2 schools.
  • Water and sanitation — 12% of favela homes lack running water; 35% of sewage runs in open sewers.
  • Crime and unemployment — murder rate 20 per 1,000 in favelas; 20% favela unemployment; 3.5 million in the informal economy.
  • Environment — most congested city in South America, 5,000 traffic-pollution deaths a year; Guanabara Bay severely polluted; under 2% of waste recycled.

Rio de Janeiro — challenges, with the figures

  • Managing growth. Around 1,000 favelas in the Greater Rio area, holding roughly 1.5 million people — the booklet puts it at 24% of Rio's population. Many are built on steep hillsides, so landslide risk is high in the heavy rains of April–October.
  • Health. Only 70% of slums have access to health cover, and the city has only 6 hospitals, mainly in affluent areas. Infant mortality is 21 per 1,000 in Cidade de Deus against 9 per 1,000 in Barra. In Rocinha, 6,000 people have at least one serious health problem, and there are two health clinics for about 100,000 people. Favela residents live on average 13 years less than people in wealthier parts of Rio.
  • Education. Only 50% of children continue education beyond 14. Rio das Pedras has a population of 50,000 and only 2 schools.
  • Water and sanitation. 12% of Rio's slums have no access to running water; a third of the supply is lost through leaky pipes, fraud and illegal access; about 35% of the city's sewage is carried in open sewers, often through the favelas, into Guanabara Bay.
  • Energy. Large numbers of favela dwellers illegally tap the grid, which is dangerous, and blackouts still occur.
  • Crime. Favelas are controlled by armed gangs — the Red Command in Jacarezinho. The murder rate is 20 per 1,000 in the favelas. Pacifying Police Units (UPPs) were established from 2013 to reclaim favelas from drug dealers, but have been criticised for focusing on favelas near Olympic sites, and pacification collapsed in many favelas after 2016.
  • Unemployment and the informal economy. 20% unemployment in the favelas. 3.5 million people work in the informal economy on about £75 a month — unregulated, often dangerous, no sick pay, and no tax paid, which starves the city of the revenue it needs for infrastructure.
  • Traffic and air pollution. Rio is the most congested city in South America; car ownership rose 40% in ten years; air pollution causes 5,000 deaths a year.
  • Water pollution. 150 tonnes of pollution pours into Guanabara Bay per day; 55 rivers flowing into the bay are classed as heavily polluted; over 50 tonnes of industrial waste is discharged daily; the Petrobras oil refinery has regular oil spills. 12 new sewage works have been built and ships are fined for discharging fuel.
  • Waste disposal. Rio produces around 3.5 million tonnes of waste a year and less than 2% is recycled. Most goes to the Seropédica landfill, 70 km from the city, where landfill gas is used to generate electricity or converted into fuel for waste collection vehicles. Collection is hardest in the favelas because the streets are too narrow for lorries.
  • The Olympic cost. The 2016 Games cost $13.1 billion and displaced around 77,000 residents, many forcibly evicted from Vila Autódromo; gentrification in Porto Maravilha drove up rents; several venues fell into disrepair.

✓ Name the type and give the figure: air pollution (5,000 deaths a year from congestion), water pollution (150 tonnes a day into Guanabara Bay, 55 heavily polluted rivers), waste (under 2% recycled, Seropédica landfill 70 km away).

Urban planning for the urban poor — the Favela-Bairro Project

Spec 3.2.1.2, named example · Case Study Summary Booklet 2026, p. 13

  • A site-and-service scheme — upgrade the favelas where they stand rather than demolish them.
  • 1995–2009 — cost the city government $1 billion and helped 250,000 people across 100+ favelas.
  • Legal land ownership — the key move: with title, 100% mortgages and low-interest loans, residents invest in their own homes.
  • Infrastructure — 12 new sewage works, 60 km of new power cables, paved roads, drainage, and postcodes.
  • Self-help — people were given tools and training to rebuild in brick; education programmes fought unemployment.
  • The cable car — a 3.5 km cable car across Complexo do Alemão, so the hillside is connected to the city.

The Favela-Bairro Project, Rio de Janeiro

  • The problem. 1,000 favelas in the Greater Rio area. In non-improved favelas, 30% had no electricity and 50% no sewage connections. No legitimate housing, so no incentive to build properly; poor employment prospects and a lack of education; many houses poorly constructed from scrap.
  • What it was. A site and service scheme running 1995–2009, costing the government $1 billion and helping 250,000 people. It covered over 100 favelas by 2005. The principle was to upgrade existing favelas rather than demolish them.
  • Services installed. 12 new sewage works; 60 km of new power cables; houses built with electricity and sanitation; new roads, drainage and elevated pavements; the Simplício hydro plant ($2 billion, 30% of Rio's energy) came in the same period.
  • Legal rights. People were given legal ownership of the land they live on, 100% mortgages, low-interest loans to improve their homes, and postcodes — some were allowed to buy their homes outright. This is the mechanism that matters: with legal title, residents invest.
  • Social provision. Community centres, nurseries and health clinics; job training and skills programmes; "fighting unemployment with education"; free childcare for teenage parents so they can return to education.
  • Self-help schemes. People were given tools and training to improve their own homes and rebuild in brick.
  • Transport. A 3.5 km cable car across Complexo do Alemão, linking a hillside favela to the rest of the city.
  • Outcomes — the good. Quality of life improved for about 250,000 residents; infant mortality fell; access to clean water improved; residents invested in their homes once they had legal rights.
  • Outcomes — the limits. Improvements were uneven and some favelas were left out; funding was cut after 2008 so the long-term sustainability is questioned; pacification displaced some residents; and some of the new pavements actually increased flood risk on the steep slopes.

✗ "The Favela-Bairro Project improved quality of life for the urban poor." — the 2022 examiner's report says the majority "just stat[ed] that quality of life was improved without saying how".
✓ Give the mechanism: "Residents were given legal ownership of their land, which meant they could get a 100% mortgage and a low-interest loan; because the house was now theirs, they invested in rebuilding it in brick, which made it safer on the steep slope and raised its value."

London — location, importance and the impact of migration

Spec 3.2.1.3, Case Study Four · Case Study Summary Booklet 2026, p. 14; HIC City Revision Mat

  • Where — south-east England on the River Thames; 32 boroughs plus the City of London; around 9 million people.
  • Locally — average earnings about £6,000 a year higher than the UK average.
  • Nationally — capital city, the UK's biggest city and biggest financial centre; 22% of UK GNI.
  • Internationally — one of the world's two leading financial centres; 6 major airports; 30 million tourists a year.
  • Migration since WWII — the former British Empire (India, Pakistan, Nigeria, Kenya, Jamaica), then Eastern Europe after 2007.
  • The result — 37% of Londoners were born outside the UK, and a population spike in the 20–40 age bands.

London — the facts and figures

  • Location and size. South-east England on the River Thames; 32 boroughs plus the City of London; around 9 million people in Greater London; an Alpha++ world city.
  • Local importance. Average earnings are about £6,000 a year higher in London.
  • National importance. Capital city of the UK; biggest city; biggest financial centre; 22% of UK GNI is generated in London; housing in London's top 10 boroughs is worth more than all the housing in Northern Ireland, Scotland and Wales combined; over 200,000 start-up companies were established in 2015.
  • International importance. With New York, one of the two most important financial centres in the world; headquarters of many large international companies; an international centre for media and communications, education (world-renowned universities), legal and medical facilities, and entertainment and tourism; 6 major airports, all connected by public transport; 30 million tourists per year.
  • Migration. Post-WWII streams were dominated by migrants from the former British Empire — India, Pakistan, Nigeria, Kenya, Jamaica — and from Eastern Europe since EU free movement in 2007. Result: 37% of London's population was born outside the UK, and there is a population spike in the 20–40 age bands.
  • Positive effects of migration. More skilled workers competing for jobs, which benefits companies recruiting; migrants contributed £20 billion to UK public finances between 2000 and 2011; London is one of the most culturally diverse cities in the UK, with migrants bringing different entertainment, clothing and foods — areas such as Chinatown in Soho were created by the Chinese diaspora.
  • Negative effects of migration. Population growth has led to housing shortages; there are social and racial tensions in some areas; pressure on schools, with a shortage of places; increased competition for jobs.
More detail

The 2025 mark scheme for "Describe the importance of a named UK city for the rest of the UK" shows the shape of the 2-marker: one mark for an initial statement, a second for developing it or adding a separate statement — "London is the UK capital (1)"; "Liverpool is an important city in the North West (1), the largest port for [trade with Ireland] (1)". The 2023 report notes that "many students struggled to access both marks with largely generic answers that could have applied to any UK city". So the second mark comes from the figure, not from a second vague adjective.

The wider UK population distribution is worth a line because the examiners sometimes set it up: upland areas such as northern Scotland are sparsely populated because they are difficult to build on, the climate is cold and wet, the land is hard to farm and natural resources are lacking. Coastal locations near estuaries have high density because harbours could be built, allowing fishing and trade. Urban areas also grew where there were reserves of iron ore and coal — Newcastle and Leeds. Density is highest in the south-east because of the favourable climate and proximity to London.

London — opportunities, with the figures

  • Cultural change, recreation and entertainment. Shoreditch — old industrial buildings reused for circus training; Old Street Roundabout became "Silicon Roundabout" for tech firms; low-income Bangladeshi families moving out as the area gentrified. The West End is famous for its shows; shopping on Oxford Street; major new developments such as Battersea Power Station.   • The Docklands. Containerisation in 1981 ended dock work, and the London Docklands Development Corporation was set up the same year to regenerate the derelict area. The result was Canary Wharf: by 2025, around 100,000 finance workers. The LDDC attracted £7.7 billion in private sector investment, with roughly 2,700 companies now operating in the area providing about 120,000 jobs in finance, retail and services.
  • Integrated transport. Demand for travel is predicted to grow 60% by 2050. The Elizabeth Line (Crossrail) halves journey times to Heathrow, raises property values by 25% around its stations, and brings 1.5 million more people within 45 minutes of central London. London has 6 main airports, all connected by public transport, plus the Underground, buses, Tramlink and the DLR.
  • Urban greening. 47% of London's area is green space; 30,000 allotments; 8.3 million trees; 223 of London's parks and green spaces hold Green Flag awards.
  • Economic. London generates 22% of UK GDP; its transport network allows the free flow of goods and services; it attracts educated graduates from around the world into research and development, finance and education, who pay taxes to the UK government.
More detail

The school's model 9-mark answer on the Docklands makes the link that pushes an answer into Level 3, and it is worth copying: investment → companies move in → jobs → income tax and corporation tax → government revenue → spending on healthcare, hospitals and education. In other words, the economic opportunity produces a social opportunity, which is exactly the kind of connected reasoning the mark scheme calls AO2.

The counter-argument in the same model answer is equally important: the dock workers were lower-skilled and could not apply for the new finance jobs in Canary Wharf, and the low-income neighbourhoods right next door — Poplar and the Isle of Dogs — have continued to suffer poverty and unemployment. "Success for some, not all" is the conclusion the examiners reward.

London — challenges, with the figures

  • Urban deprivation. 2 million people (27%) live in poverty, against about 20% for the rest of England. Tower Hamlets has the greatest poverty rate of any London borough at 56%. Newham has a 44% child poverty rate. Deprivation is increasing in the outer boroughs as affordable housing in inner London becomes harder to find.
  • Inequality in health. Life expectancy is 88 in Chelsea and 80 in Tower Hamlets — an 8-year gap. Infant mortality in Hackney is 3.4 times that of Bromley. Quality of healthcare is inconsistent across boroughs.
  • Inequality in education. GCSE attainment is 74% in Kensington and Chelsea, 1.5 times the 51% in Lewisham. The city is struggling to provide enough school places.
  • Inequality in employment. Unemployment is 4% in Chelsea and 10% in Tower Hamlets; London's unemployment rate is still higher than the UK average.
  • Housing. 170,000 homeless — about 50% of the UK total; average house price over £500,000 (2024); average central London rent over £2,000 a month; overcrowding in inner boroughs; rough sleeping up 23% since 2020. Rising housing costs are reducing living standards.
  • Dereliction, brownfield and greenfield. Areas such as Battersea Power Station were derelict until recently redeveloped; many East London buildings became derelict through de-industrialisation. In 2019 there were almost 3,000 brownfield sites in London covering 2,600 hectares — enough brownfield land remains for over a million homes. Merton has the most brownfield sites of any borough (6,809); Westminster has none. Brownfield development is expensive, which is why greenfield sites on the fringe come under pressure.
  • Waste disposal. The average London household produces 970 kg of waste per year. London sent the largest proportion of its waste to incineration of any UK region in 2021 (66%); 750,000 tonnes goes to landfill every year; only 20% is composted, and recycling rates are the lowest in the country.
  • Environment. NO₂ levels breach WHO limits in central London; the urban heat island makes the city up to 5°C warmer than its surroundings; the Thames Barrier has closed over 200 times; green space is unequally distributed between boroughs even though 47% of London is green.
  • Urban sprawl. A rising population puts pressure on the rural–urban fringe for new homes and for cheap land for businesses.

Urban sprawl, the rural–urban fringe and commuter settlements

Spec 3.2.1.3 · HIC City Revision Mat; 2025 Q01.10 mark scheme

  • Rural–urban fringe — the zone of transition between built-up area and countryside, where land uses compete.
  • Positives — rising house prices for existing residents, more services, greater cultural diversity, better transport.
  • Negatives — loss of farmland and habitats, urban sprawl, worse public transport so more cars, land never returns to countryside.
  • Commuter settlements — villages within rail or motorway reach of a city, which fill with estates of identikit housing.
  • The figure evidence in 2025 — cul-de-sac estates eating greenfield land, surrounded by farmland, station and A-road nearby, lots of cars, swales for drainage.
  • Loss of character — the "identikit" point: the settlement stops being rural without becoming urban.
More detail

The rural–urban fringe is a zone of transition between the built-up area and the countryside, where there is often competition for land use — out-of-town shopping centres and golf courses alongside farmland and motorways. Urban sprawl is the unplanned growth of urban areas into the surrounding countryside.

Positive impacts of urban sprawl on the fringe: house prices can increase for those already living there; services such as schools and healthcare increase to meet the needs of the growing population; cultural diversity could increase; transport infrastructure could be improved.

Negative impacts: public transport is worse in rural areas, so there are more cars on the road; urban sprawl itself; land is unlikely ever to return to countryside once built on; valuable farmland or recreational land is lost; natural habitats may be destroyed.

The 2025 mark scheme for the 6-marker is a model of what "suggest" allows: loss of habitats, greenfield land and farmland; loss of rural or unique character; increased air pollution and congestion from the extra cars; increasingly busy branch-line train services; and impacts on drainage from the additional tarmac — with credit specifically given to students who spotted the swales in the photograph as evidence that drainage is already a problem.

London's integrated transport strategies

The 9-mark frame

Three paragraphs plus a conclusion, and the school's rule is that evaluation inside every paragraph beats evaluation saved for the end. The structure is P.E.E.L. — Point, Explanation, Evidence, Link — and in this section the classification is almost always S.E.E.: social, economic, environmental.

  1. One-sentence intro naming the place and your line. "Rio de Janeiro, an NEE city of 13 million grown around Guanabara Bay, has created substantial social and economic opportunities through urban growth, but they are very unevenly distributed." Name the case study in the first sentence — the examiners' commonest complaint is a "geography of anywhere".
  2. Paragraph 1 — the first category, with figures. Point (the opportunity or challenge), explanation (why it follows from urban growth), evidence (the number), then evaluate — how big, who benefits, who doesn't. "Rio has over 400 secondary schools and a literacy rate of 97% against Brazil's 93%; however only 50% of children continue beyond 14, and Rio das Pedras has 50,000 people and two schools, so the opportunity exists but is not shared."
  3. Paragraph 2 — the second category, same shape. If the question says "social and economic", do one of each and compare their size. If it says "to what extent", your evaluative sentence belongs here.
  4. Paragraph 3 — the third category, or the counter-case. For a "has it worked?" question this is where the limits go: uneven coverage, funding cut after 2008, gentrification, displacement. Say which side is stronger.
  5. A conclusion that decides. Use the school's ladder — definitely / mostly / partly / slightly / not at all — and tie it to the words of the question. Two sentences, no new material.

Sentence starters that earn the AO3 marks:

  • "Figure 2 shows that… which means… and therefore…"
  • "This is a significant opportunity because… and as a result…"
  • "However, this benefit is limited to…, whereas…"
  • "The most significant challenge is…, because it causes…, which in turn…"
  • "Urban change created more economic than social opportunity, although the two are linked, since tax revenue funds…"
  • "Overall, I mostly agree with the statement, because… outweighs…"

SPaG (3 marks). Spell these right: urbanisation, Rio de Janeiro, favela, Favela-Bairro, Guanabara Bay, Rocinha, Stratford, Elizabeth Line, ULEZ, gentrification, counter-urbanisation, Seropédica. Use the specialist terms — the top band wants "a wide range of specialist terms as appropriate".

03Understanding it

U1

Why can a country's rural population be rising at the same time as it is urbanising?

Show answer

Because urbanisation is a proportion, not a total. It is the increase in the percentage of the population living in towns and cities. If the total population grows fast enough, the number of people in the countryside can still rise while the share living there falls.

The school's revision mat makes the point explicitly for the 1960–2017 data: "although the rural population continues to increase, the proportion of people living in these areas across the world is decreasing".

The exam point: this is exactly why "urbanisation is when cities get bigger" is marked wrong. Cities getting bigger is urban growth. Keeping the two apart is worth a 1-mark definition in most years and protects you on the graph-description questions.

U2

Rural–urban migration brings adults to a city, not children. So why does migration cause a birth rate increase?

Show answer

Because of who migrates. Migrants are typically aged 18–35 — people of reproductive age — so the city gains a population heavily weighted towards young adults. That produces a high birth rate and, because there are few elderly people, a low death rate. The gap between them is natural increase.

So the two causes of urban growth are not independent: migration creates the natural increase. In Rio, the population is "mostly under 30", which is the same mechanism.

How the mark scheme rewards it: the 2025 scheme credits reading a low median age off the figure and then saying it "will mean young people of reproductive age, so larger numbers of children swelling the population". That sentence is the AO2 link — the figure alone is only AO3.

U3

Rio has the largest water treatment plant in the world, yet 12% of favela homes have no running water. How can both be true?

Show answer

Because supply is not the same as access. The Guandu plant produces the water; the distribution network is what fails. About a third of Rio's supply is lost through leaky pipes, fraud and illegal access, and favelas are built informally on steep hillsides where no piped network was ever planned.

The same gap appears across every Rio service. There are six hospitals, but they are mainly in affluent areas, so Rocinha has two clinics for about 100,000 people. There are 400+ secondary schools, but only 50% of children continue beyond 14. 99% of homes have electricity, but many favela connections are illegal and dangerous.

Use it like this: this is the single most useful idea in the Rio case study, because it gives every "to what extent" question a built-in answer — the opportunity is real at city scale and absent at favela scale.

U4

Why was giving favela residents legal ownership of their land the most important thing the Favela-Bairro Project did?

Show answer

Because it changed the incentive. Before the scheme, housing was not legitimate, so there was no reason to spend money improving a house you could be evicted from — which is why so many were built from scrap. Legal title, plus 100% mortgages and low-interest loans, meant residents could borrow against the property and had a reason to invest. The outcome the booklet records is residents rebuilding in brick under self-help schemes once they had the tools, training and security.

Title also brought postcodes, which sounds trivial and is not: an address lets you register for services, receive post, open a bank account and apply for formal jobs.

The exam point: the 2022 examiner's report says most candidates "just stat[ed] that quality of life was improved without saying how". Legal title → borrowing → investment → safer brick housing is the "how", and it is what separates Level 3 from Level 2.

U5

The Docklands regeneration created 120,000 jobs. Why did it still leave Poplar and the Isle of Dogs poor?

Show answer

Because of a skills mismatch. The jobs that were lost in 1981 were dock jobs, which were manual and low-skilled; the jobs created at Canary Wharf were in finance, retail and professional services. The former dockers could not apply for them, so the new employment was filled largely by commuters and incomers.

The second mechanism is gentrification. Rising property values make an area more expensive to live in, so lower-income residents are displaced rather than lifted. Stratford shows the same pattern — house prices up over 50% since 2012.

How to deploy it: this is why the honest conclusion to every UK regeneration question is "a success for some". The school's model answer reaches Level 3 precisely by conceding that the economic opportunities were larger and better distributed than the social ones.

U6

Building on brownfield land is more expensive than building on greenfield. Why does London do it anyway?

Show answer

Because the alternative costs more in other ways. Greenfield development means urban sprawl into the rural–urban fringe: loss of farmland and habitats, land that will never return to countryside, longer commutes, and more cars because rural public transport is poor.

Brownfield development instead removes dereliction, reuses existing infrastructure, and sits where public transport already runs. London has the capacity: almost 3,000 brownfield sites covering 2,600 hectares in 2019, with enough brownfield land for over a million homes. Battersea Power Station is the showcase.

The complication worth adding: brownfield land is unevenly distributed — Merton has 6,809 sites and Westminster has none — so the places with land are not always the places with demand, which is why the fringe still comes under pressure.

U7

Why do examiners refuse to credit the word "pollution" on its own?

Show answer

Because the three kinds have different causes, different victims and different solutions, and an unclassified answer shows none of that understanding.

Air pollution in Rio comes from congestion — car ownership up 40% in ten years — and causes 5,000 deaths a year across the whole city, rich and poor. Water pollution comes from the 35% of sewage in open sewers plus industrial discharge, putting 150 tonnes a day into Guanabara Bay and threatening the tourist beaches. Waste is a collection problem — favela streets are too narrow for lorries — with under 2% recycled.

The solutions differ accordingly: a city-centre toll and metro expansion for air, 12 new sewage works and fines for ships for water, the Seropédica landfill with gas capture for waste.

The exam point: the school's slides put it bluntly — "always reference air or water pollution; examiners don't reward 'pollution' unless classified".

U8

East Village cuts water use by 50% and energy use by 30%. Is that enough to make London sustainable?

Show answer

No, and saying why is what the 2024 9-marker wanted. Conservation works at the scale it is built at. East Village is a 27-hectare development of about 2,800 homes with a purpose-built CHP plant, grey water system and automated waste collection. London has around 9 million people in largely Victorian and post-war housing stock that cannot be retrofitted as cheaply.

What conservation cannot touch: London still produces 970 kg of waste per household and incinerates 66% of it; NO₂ still breaches WHO limits; the city is still up to 5°C warmer than its surroundings; and 170,000 people are homeless, which no amount of grey water recycling addresses. Sustainability in the spec's own definition includes resources "allocated fairly" and a strong community — a social test as well as an environmental one.

The balanced judgement: water and energy conservation make cities substantially more sustainable where new building allows it, and they are necessary; but they are not sufficient without transport strategies (ULEZ cut NO₂ 44%), waste reduction and affordable housing. "Partly" is the honest word.

04Exam drill

What each command word actually wants

Section A is Question 01 on Paper 2 — 1 hour 30 minutes, 88 marks, three compulsory-plus-optional sections. The question types are marked in completely different ways, so answer each in its own shape. This is the school's exam technique guide plus what the examiners' reports say was going wrong.

  • 1 mark — state / give / name / "what is…?" One clear point, or the correct definition. The guide's warning: people "do not read the instructions clearly" and these questions "hide on a busy page". Don't lose them. For "shade one circle only", shade one.
  • 2 marks — outline / describe / give two. Usually point + development, not two bare statements: "it is rare that just a straightforward statement suffices". Use because, this means that, therefore. If it says "give two", two separate points are fine.
  • 3 marks — suggest / outline. Three linked steps in one chain, or three separate developed points. The 2025 scheme for a 3-marker shows a chain earning all three: "economic development increases wages (1) and therefore tax receipts (1) which can be spent on better sanitation (1)".
  • 4 marks — explain / suggest, usually with a figure. Two developed points, or four basic ones. Make a point and explain it, twice, with specific detail. You must refer to the figure — "Figure X shows that…" — and then use it. The report's complaint is students "merely lift text and repeat it".
  • 6 marks — explain / assess / discuss / "to what extent", level marked. Two or three developed paragraphs with evidence and explanation. If the command is discuss, assess or to what extent, you also need limitations or counter-arguments and a short judgement; if it is explain, explanation alone is enough. A named example is often required — the mark scheme caps you at Level 2 without one.
  • 9 marks (+3 SPaG) — assess / evaluate / discuss / "to what extent", level marked. A one-sentence intro naming the case study, two or three P.E.E.L. paragraphs with evaluation inside each, and a one or two sentence conclusion that decides. Classify with S.E.E. (social, economic, environmental). The three AOs carry 3 marks each: AO1 place knowledge, AO2 understanding of the process, AO3 application to this question and this figure. "Simply repeating case study information is knowledge (AO1) and cannot gain credit" in the AO2/AO3 part — so every fact needs a therefore.

Twelve questions in the real spread: two short, four 4-markers, four 6-markers and two 9-markers. The 6- and 9-mark answers are written as plans, because AQA marks them in levels rather than by ticking points — treat the mark points as a coverage target, not a tick list.

D1

What is urbanisation? (2)

Show answer

The increase in the proportion (or percentage) of a country's population living in urban areas 1

— that is, in towns and cities, which is different from urban growth, the absolute increase in a city's size 1

The 1-mark version in 2025 needed only the first line. Do not write "when cities get bigger".

D2

Outline one reason why reducing crime is a challenge in some LIC/NEE cities. (3)

Show answer

Informal settlements are extremely densely built with narrow, unplanned streets, so policing them is physically difficult 1

This allows armed gangs to control territory — in Rio the Red Command holds Jacarezinho, and the murder rate in the favelas reaches 20 per 1,000 1

Attempts to reclaim them, such as the Pacifying Police Units established from 2013, were criticised for concentrating on favelas near Olympic sites, and pacification collapsed in many favelas after 2016 1

The 2023 report: "it was a shame to see so many stereotypical and simplistic views equating crime as the inevitable result of poverty". Poverty earns the first mark; gangs, corruption and sheer density are the answers that earn the rest.

D3

Suggest how natural increase and migration affect the growth of LIC/NEE cities. (4)

Show answer

Rural–urban migration brings large numbers of people into the city: they are pushed from the countryside by mechanisation removing farm jobs, poor harvests and a lack of sanitation and schooling, and pulled by better-paid work, education and healthcare 1

Because migrants are typically aged 18–35, the city's population becomes heavily weighted towards young adults — a figure showing a low median age is direct evidence of this 1

Natural increase then compounds the migration: with so many people of reproductive age the birth rate is high, while few elderly residents keeps the death rate low, so births greatly exceed deaths 1

The combined effect is very rapid growth — in Rio the population is mostly under 30, and growth of many thousands a week means tens of thousands a month, driving urban sprawl and the expansion of informal settlements 1

Refer to the figure explicitly, then use it: manipulating a weekly growth figure into a daily total is specifically credited in the 2025 mark scheme as showing you understand the scale.

D4

Suggest how urban greening can create opportunities in UK cities. (4)

Show answer

Urban greening increases and preserves open space such as parks and gardens, which creates social opportunities for recreation and exercise — London has 47% green space, 8.3 million trees and 30,000 allotments, and 223 of its parks hold Green Flag awards 1

This improves physical and mental health, which reduces pressure on health services and means fewer days lost to illness 1

Green space also brings environmental benefits that become economic ones: trees absorb CO₂ and filter particulates, improving air quality in a city where NO₂ breaches WHO limits, and vegetation reduces the urban heat island effect, which in London makes the city up to 5°C warmer than its surroundings 1

Greening raises the attractiveness of an area, so property values and investment rise — the Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park's 100 hectares of green space helped draw the International Quarter's 25,000 jobs to Stratford 1

D5

Suggest how urban transport strategies can be used to reduce traffic congestion. (4)

Show answer

Designated cycle lanes make cycling safer and easier, and clear signing of routes with journey times lets cyclists plan a trip, so more people are encouraged to cycle to work 1

That means fewer cars on the road for short journeys, directly reducing congestion — East Village has designated cycle lanes, and London has over 800 Santander Cycles docking stations plus Lime and Forest e-bikes 1

Improving public transport capacity does the same at a larger scale: the Elizabeth Line, 118 km and 41 stations, cuts Paddington to Canary Wharf to 17 minutes and relieves the Central, District and Jubilee lines 1

Because the train is now faster and more convenient than driving, commuters switch from car to rail, so the number of vehicles on London's roads falls — reinforced by the £15-a-day Congestion Charge, which has cut central traffic by 30% since 2003 1

✗ "Trains are better for the environment." — the 2023 report penalised answers where "the congestion reduction was implicit only", and answers that drifted onto climate change and air quality.
✓ Finish the chain every time: faster → people switch → fewer cars → less congestion.

D6

Suggest how the growth of a commuter settlement has impacts on the rural–urban fringe. (4)

Show answer

New housing estates spread into the remaining greenfield land in and around the village, so farmland and natural habitats are lost permanently — once built on, land is unlikely ever to return to countryside 1

The settlement also loses its rural character: identikit estate housing and cul-de-sac street patterns replace a distinct sense of place, so the fringe becomes neither properly rural nor properly urban 1

Because rural public transport is poor, commuters drive, so car numbers rise sharply — increasing congestion and air pollution on roads built for a much smaller village, and crowding the branch-line trains into the city 1

Additional tarmac and roofs reduce infiltration and increase surface runoff, raising flood risk, which is why new estates are built with swales to manage drainage; set against this, existing residents see house prices rise and services such as schools and shops become more viable 1

D7

Explain how urban planning is improving quality of life for the urban poor. Use an LIC/NEE example you have studied. (6)

Show answer

Plan: name the scheme, give the mechanism, give the figures, then evaluate. AQA marks this in levels, so three developed points with place detail reach Level 3; a list of features without a how stays in Level 2.

The Favela-Bairro Project in Rio de Janeiro was a site and service scheme running 1995–2009, which cost the city government $1 billion and improved conditions for 250,000 people across more than 100 favelas; crucially it upgraded favelas where they stood rather than demolishing them, so communities were not broken up 1

Before the scheme, 30% of non-improved favelas had no electricity and 50% no sewage connection; the project installed 12 new sewage works, 60 km of new power cables, paved roads and drainage, which cut waterborne disease and contributed to a fall in infant mortality 1

The most important measure was giving residents legal ownership of the land they lived on, with 100% mortgages and low-interest loans 1

This improved quality of life because a legal title gives people a reason to invest in a house they can no longer be evicted from: under self-help schemes they were given tools and training and rebuilt in brick, which is safer on a steep hillside, and postcodes let them register for services and apply for formal jobs 1

Education and job training programmes tackled the cause of poverty rather than its symptoms — free childcare for teenage parents let them return to education — and a 3.5 km cable car across Complexo do Alemão connected a hillside favela to the city, cutting journey times to work 1

However, the improvements were uneven and some favelas were left out entirely; funding was reduced after 2008 so long-term sustainability is doubtful; pacification displaced some residents; and some of the new pavements actually increased flood risk on the steep slopes. Quality of life improved substantially for those included, which is the honest judgement 1

D8

To what extent has urban growth created social opportunities in a major city in an LIC or NEE? (6)

Show answer

Plan: the structure is the evaluation. Take two social services — health and education — and for each show the city-scale opportunity and then the favela-scale failure. Level marked; the 2023 report says "very few students considered the to what extent aspect", so the contrast is where the marks are.

Urban growth in Rio de Janeiro has concentrated services in the city: Unified Health provides free care, and Rio has 3.44 doctors per 1,000 people against Brazil's 2.81, so access is better than in rural Brazil, especially for children and the elderly 1

Education shows the same gain — over 400 secondary schools and 6 universities produce a literacy rate of 97% against a national 93%, and literacy raises employability and income 1

However, these opportunities are not shared. Hospitals are concentrated in affluent areas: the city has only six, and Rocinha has two clinics for about 100,000 people, so favela residents live on average 13 years less than people in wealthier districts, and infant mortality is 21 per 1,000 in Cidade de Deus against 9 in Barra 1

Education is equally unequal: only 50% of children continue beyond 14, and Rio das Pedras has 50,000 residents and two schools, so rapid growth outran school building 1

Growth has also produced responses that extend the opportunity — travelling health clinics in Santa Maria, Family Health Teams, a private university in Rocinha, the Schools of Tomorrow programme and the NGO Alfazendo in Cidade de Deus — which shows the opportunity is widening rather than fixed 1

Judgement: urban growth has created substantial social opportunities, but only partly realised them — the figures show a clear advantage over rural Brazil alongside a 13-year life expectancy gap inside the same city, so the extent depends entirely on which part of Rio you live in 1

D9

To what extent is managing environmental issues, such as waste disposal, a challenge in cities in LICs/NEEs? (6)

Show answer

Plan: classify the environmental issues, show why each is hard to manage, then show what is being managed successfully — that is the "to what extent".

Waste is a severe challenge in Rio de Janeiro: the city produces around 3.5 million tonnes a year and less than 2% is recycled, so almost everything goes to landfill 1

Collection is the specific difficulty: favela streets are too narrow for refuse lorries, so waste accumulates in the settlements where the greatest number of people live, spreading disease and blocking drainage channels on steep slopes 1

Water pollution is harder still, because it combines sources: about 35% of the city's sewage runs in open sewers, over 50 tonnes of industrial waste is discharged daily, and the Petrobras refinery spills regularly — the result is 150 tonnes entering Guanabara Bay every day and 55 feeder rivers classed as heavily polluted, threatening both wildlife and the beach tourism the economy depends on 1

Air pollution is a third strand, driven by congestion: Rio is the most congested city in South America, car ownership rose 40% in ten years, and air pollution causes 5,000 deaths a year — which affects the whole city, not only the poor 1

However, these issues are being managed with some success. The Seropédica landfill, 70 km from the city, captures landfill gas to generate electricity and fuel the collection vehicles; 12 new sewage works have been built and ships are fined for discharging fuel; a city-centre toll and the metro extension under Guanabara Bay target congestion 1

Judgement: managing environmental issues is a major challenge, and the limiting factor is not usually a lack of schemes but the cost of retrofitting infrastructure into settlements that grew informally and faster than planning could follow — which is why the recycling rate stays below 2% while the landfill gas scheme works 1

D10

Explain how features of sustainable urban living can reduce a city's environmental impact. Use a named example. (6)

Show answer

Plan: the spec names four features — water and energy conservation, waste recycling, green space, and transport. Take them in turn with East Village's figures, and link each to the environmental impact it reduces.

East Village in Stratford, the converted 2012 Olympic athletes' village of about 2,800 homes, uses rainwater harvesting, grey water recycling and water-efficient appliances to cut mains water consumption to 50% below average; this reduces abstraction from rivers and groundwater, which matters in the water-stressed south-east 1

Energy use is 30% below average, because biomass is burned in an on-site Combined Heat and Power system that generates electricity and useful heat at once rather than wasting the heat, and high insulation standards cut the demand in the first place 1

Lower energy demand means fewer fossil fuels burned in power stations, so greenhouse gas emissions and air pollutants both fall — and because the fuel is biomass rather than gas, the carbon released was recently absorbed by the plants themselves 1

Waste recycling is designed in: 99% of parkland waste is recycled, food waste is composted and automated collection removes the need for bin lorries, so less goes to landfill or incineration and there are fewer vehicle movements 1

Green space does two jobs — 10 hectares within a 27-hectare development, with green roofs, ponds and wetland areas, which absorb CO₂, reduce the urban heat island and slow surface runoff so flood risk falls 1

Transport completes it: 37 EV charging points, designated cycle lanes, 6 bus routes and Stratford International station mean residents can live without a car, and the site sits inside the Congestion Zone and ULEZ, which cut NO₂ by 44% in the original zone 1

D11

"Urban change in the UK has created more economic opportunities than social opportunities."

Evaluate this statement using a UK city you have studied. (9)

Show answer

Plan — four paragraphs. Intro naming the place and the line; economic opportunities with figures; social opportunities and their limits; the link between the two; judgement. AQA marks in levels: AO1 place knowledge, AO2 understanding, AO3 evaluation, 3 marks each. A one-sided answer caps at Level 2. Add 3 SPaG marks.

Intro. The London Docklands in east London, derelict after containerisation ended dock work in 1981, were regenerated by the London Docklands Development Corporation set up the same year; overall the change created substantially more economic than social opportunity 1

Economic opportunities. The LDDC attracted £7.7 billion of private sector investment, which funded the offices, shops and infrastructure of Canary Wharf 1

Around 2,700 companies now operate in the area providing about 120,000 jobs in finance, retail and services, and by 2025 roughly 100,000 finance workers are based at Canary Wharf, raising incomes well above the London average 1

This matters beyond the wages: businesses and high-earning employees pay large sums in tax, so government revenue rises, which boosts GDP — and workers shopping locally raise the income of other businesses, the multiplier effect 1

Social opportunities, and their limits. Some social gains followed directly — tax revenue funds healthcare, hospitals and education, and the wider change brought cultural and recreational opportunity, with 47% of London green space and 223 Green Flag parks 1

However, the social benefits were far less evenly spread. The dock workers were lower-skilled and could not apply for the new finance jobs, so they faced unemployment and, without income, homelessness 1

The low-income neighbourhoods immediately next to Canary Wharf — Poplar and the Isle of Dogs — have continued to suffer poverty and unemployment, and Tower Hamlets still has the highest poverty rate of any London borough at 56%, with life expectancy of 80 against 88 in Chelsea 1

Evaluating the link. The two are not independent: economic opportunity generates the tax that funds social provision, so the statement slightly understates the social effect. But the mechanism is indirect and slow, whereas the economic effect was immediate and measurable, and gentrification actively displaced the residents who most needed the social gain — house prices in nearby Stratford rose over 50% after 2012 1

Conclusion. I mostly agree: urban change in London created more economic than social opportunity, because the £7.7 billion of investment and 120,000 jobs were real and immediate while the social benefits reached the original community only partially and indirectly — a success for some rather than for all 1

✗ A list of everything you know about London, or a drift into "opportunities versus challenges" — both are named in the examiners' reports as the commonest failures here.
✓ Address economic and social in turn, then conclude which was greater and why. That is exactly what the report says the best answers did.

D12

"To what extent can water and energy conservation make cities more sustainable?" (9)

Show answer

Plan — intro defining sustainability and naming East Village; water conservation; energy conservation; what conservation cannot fix; judgement. Level marked, and "to what extent" means the limits must be argued, not just mentioned. Add 3 SPaG marks.

Intro. Sustainable urban living means minimal damage to the environment, resources allocated fairly and a strong community; East Village in Stratford — the converted 2012 athletes' village of about 2,800 homes on a 27-hectare site — is the clearest UK test of how far conservation alone can go 1

Water conservation. Rainwater harvesting, grey water recycling from sinks and showers for toilet flushing, and water-efficient appliances cut mains consumption to 50% below average 1

That is genuinely significant because the south-east is the driest and most densely populated part of the UK, so reduced abstraction protects rivers and groundwater, and ponds and wetlands on site slow runoff and reduce flood risk 1

Energy conservation. Energy use is 30% below average through an on-site biomass Combined Heat and Power system, which captures the heat a conventional power station wastes, plus high insulation standards that cut demand at source 1

Lower demand means fewer fossil fuels burned, so greenhouse emissions fall along with the air pollutants that damage health in a city where NO₂ breaches WHO limits — and the development targets carbon neutrality 1

The limits — scale. East Village was purpose-built; London has around 9 million people in largely Victorian and post-war stock that cannot be retrofitted with a CHP plant and a grey water network at the same cost, so the model does not simply scale up 1

The limits — what conservation cannot touch. London still produces 970 kg of waste per household a year and incinerates 66% of it; the urban heat island still makes the city up to 5°C warmer; the Thames Barrier has closed over 200 times; and 170,000 people are homeless, which no water or energy measure addresses even though fair allocation is part of the spec's own definition of sustainability 1

What else is needed. Transport and waste strategies do work that conservation cannot: ULEZ cut NO₂ by 44% in the original zone, the Congestion Charge cut central traffic 30% since 2003, and East Village's own 99% parkland recycling rate and 37 EV charging points show conservation is most effective as one part of a package 1

Conclusion. Water and energy conservation make cities partly more sustainable: the East Village figures prove the environmental gains are large and measurable where new building allows them, but they are necessary rather than sufficient, because waste, transport, air quality and above all the fair allocation of housing lie outside their reach 1
