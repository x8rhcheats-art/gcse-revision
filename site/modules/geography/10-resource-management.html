How Section C is built, in full

Section C is in two parts and the rubric matters. Question 03 is compulsory and covers the resource overview plus the UK's food, water and energy — so you can be asked about all three whichever option you sit. Then you answer one of Question 04 (Food), Question 05 (Water) or Question 06 (Energy). Your class does Energy. What a resource is, and why food, water and energy

Spec 3.2.3.1 · Resource Management Revision Mat; AQA List of Key Terms

  • A resource — something human society attaches value to because of its usefulness.
  • Resource management — the control and monitoring of resources so that they are not depleted or exhausted.
  • The three the spec names — food, water and energy, because each is needed for economic and social well-being.
More detail

A resource is something which human society attaches value to due to its usefulness. Resource management is the control and monitoring of resources so that they do not become depleted or exhausted.

The spec's framing is that all three resources are "significant for economic and social well-being", so the 1- and 3-mark questions at the start of Question 03 are usually about why, not where:

  • Food — calories are the fuel for our bodies, needed to work and to enjoy ourselves. Without them, undernutrition follows: people "do not eat enough nutrients to cover their needs for energy and growth, or to maintain a healthy immune system".
  • Water — we drink it to survive, and we also need it to wash, dispose of wastes, grow and process food, and for industrial manufacturing. Poor supply therefore affects health, education (time spent collecting water), income and dignity at once — which is what the 2022 3-marker on "social well-being" wanted.
  • Energy — needed to keep ourselves warm, construct houses, transport goods and people, process food and power machinery.

The 2025 paper opened Question 03 with a map of well-being for women, and the mark scheme's accepted reasons for a high score are worth knowing because they are generic resource geography: the country is an HIC with high standards of living; most high scorers are in northern Europe with a temperate climate and few natural hazards; they are stable democracies; and almost all are conflict free.

  • Food — calories are the fuel our bodies need in order to work and to enjoy ourselves.
  • Water — needed to drink to survive, and to wash, dispose of waste, grow and process food, and manufacture.
  • Energy — needed to keep warm, build houses, transport goods, process food and power machinery.

Global inequalities in the distribution of food, water and energy

Spec 3.2.3.1 · Resource Management Revision Mat; Energy Workbook pp. 5–7

  • Food surplus — large areas of Europe, Asia and North and South America produce more than they consume.
  • Food deficit — most of sub-Saharan Africa, plus India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Afghanistan; 50 countries face food insecurity.
  • Water shortage — Africa, the Middle East and Australia are prone to shortage and drought.
  • Energy consumption — highest in HICs; North America is the clearest cluster of very high per-capita use.
More detail

Food. Large areas of Europe, Asia and North and South America produce a food surplus. In a large proportion of countries in sub-Saharan Africa there is a food deficit, and this is also the case in some Asian countries such as India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Afghanistan. Around 50 countries face food insecurity, and more than 800 million people live every day with hunger or food insecurity.

Water. Africa, the Middle East and Australia are prone to water shortages and drought. Europe, South East Asia, northern South America and northern North America have a water surplus. A water surplus exists where supply is greater than demand; a water deficit where demand is greater than supply.

Energy. Consumption per person is highest in HICs — North America, with the USA and Canada, has some of the highest levels in the world. The key relationships the 2022 scatter-graph question tested are:

  • GNI and energy consumption are positively correlated — richer countries use more energy per person, because they own more appliances, drive more, heat and cool buildings, and run more industry.
  • Consumption and reserves do not match. The countries holding the largest fossil fuel reserves are often not the largest consumers, so energy has to be traded — which is the root of energy insecurity and of energy conflict.
  • Energy insecurity is almost universal. The workbook's framing: high levels of insecurity are experienced by both HICs and LICs/NEEs; the countries with the lowest insecurity tend to be those rich in resources; insecurity can occur at both high and low levels of consumption; and even a country with a high supply can be insecure if demand is higher still.

The Arctic is the frontier: 30% of the world's unexploited natural gas is thought to lie there, which is why the region is becoming politically contested as the ice retreats.

  • Water surplus — Europe, South East Asia, northern South America and northern North America.
  • Reserves and consumption do not match — the countries that use most energy are rarely the countries that hold the fossil fuels.

✓ Describe it properly: name continents and regions, give the general pattern, then the anomaly. "Countries with very low renewable generation are concentrated in the Middle East and North Africa, with a second cluster in eastern Europe; the exception is Norway, which is surrounded by low-renewable countries but generates almost all its electricity from hydro."

The UK's food situation

Spec 3.2.3.1 · Resource Management Revision Mat; Case Study Summary Booklet quiz facts

  • Four demand changes — high-value exotic exports, all-year seasonal food, organic produce, and a bigger carbon footprint.
  • Imports — 12% of the food eaten in the UK comes from LICs, which raises food miles and carbon.
  • Agriculture's own footprint — 12% of the UK's total greenhouse gas emissions come from farming.
  • Agribusiness — larger farms, hedgerows removed, more mechanisation and chemicals, so yields rise.
More detail
  • Imports — 12% of the food eaten in the UK comes from LICs, which raises food miles and the carbon footprint.
  • Local sourcing — cuts food miles and supports the local economy, but limits choice and seasonality.
  • Organic — demand is rising because it is seen as better for the environment and healthier, so organic area is expanding.

The UK's changing demand for food — the four strands

  • High-value exotic foods. Growing demand for high-value food exports such as Madagascan vanilla and specialist honey. These travel long distances, raising food miles — the distance covered supplying food to consumers — and so the carbon footprint of the UK diet.
  • All-year seasonal food. Growing demand for foods such as strawberries out of season, which can only be grown in the UK in summer; the rest of the year they are flown or shipped in, or grown in heated glasshouses.
  • Organic produce. Growing demand because it is seen as better for the environment and healthier. Organic produce is grown with environmentally and animal-friendly methods: artificial fertilisers are banned and farmers build fertile soil by rotating crops and using compost, manure and clover, with no synthetic additives such as pesticides and dyes. So UK farmers convert land to organic, which lowers yields per hectare but commands a higher price.
  • Agribusiness. The application of business skills to agriculture. It increases food production because farming takes place on a greater scale: farm size increases as hedgerows are removed when farms are combined, and the greater use of mechanisation and chemicals raises crop yields. The cost is habitat loss and chemical runoff.
  • The figures to quote. 12% of the food eaten in the UK comes from LICs. 12% of the UK's total greenhouse gas emissions come from agriculture. Around 50 countries face food insecurity.
  • Local sourcing. A method of food production and distribution that is local rather than national or international — food is grown and harvested close to consumers' homes and distributed over much shorter distances. Advantages: far fewer food miles and so a lower carbon footprint; fresher produce; money stays in the local economy, supporting farmers and shops; and consumers know how it was produced. Disadvantages: limited choice, strictly seasonal, and often more expensive per unit.

The UK's water situation

Spec 3.2.3.1 · Resource Management Revision Mat; 2019, 2022 and 2025 Q03.4

  • The mismatch — rainfall is greatest in the west; the greatest demand is in the driest part, the south-east.
  • So water is transferred — from areas of surplus in the west and north to the deficit south-east.
  • Rising demand — 310 litres per person a day, and demand is expected to exceed supply by 2034.
  • Pollution effects — pesticides kill wildlife, fertilisers cause eutrophication, drinking water is polluted, fishing and tourism lose money.
More detail
  • The UK has enough water — the problem is that rainfall patterns do not match demand.
  • So water is transferred — from areas of surplus in the west and north to the deficit areas of the south-east.
  • Rising demand — average UK consumption is 310 litres per person a day, and demand is expected to exceed supply by 2034.
  • Pollution causes — agricultural chemicals and pesticides, oil from boats, untreated industrial waste, transport runoff, sewage.

The UK's water — supply, demand and quality

  • Why transfer is needed. The UK receives enough water to meet its demands, but rainfall patterns do not match demand. Rainfall is greatest in the west of the country while the east receives less, and the greatest demand comes from the south-east, which is the driest area in the UK and has a water deficit. Water therefore has to be transferred from the west to the south-east.
  • Rising demand. Average daily consumption in the UK is 310 litres per person, and demand is expected to exceed supply by 2034. The drivers: a rising population increasingly concentrated in the south; more water-using appliances — UK dishwasher ownership rose from 22% in 1998 to 49% in 2018 — and an increasing trend for UK houses to have more than one bathroom.
  • Causes of water pollution. Chemicals and pesticides from agriculture; oil from boats and ships; untreated industrial waste; oil and heavy metals from transport; sewage pumped into rivers and seas.
  • Effects of water pollution. Pesticides kill wildlife; fertilisers cause eutrophication — nutrients from farmland and sewage produce algae which reduce oxygen levels in the water; drinking water is polluted; and fishermen and the tourist industry suffer economic losses.
  • The scale of the problem, from the exam figures. The 2019 paper quoted the Environment Agency naming population growth, climate change and plastic pollution as the main future threats to water quality. The 2025 paper quoted MPs: "No river in England is clean" — agriculture and water companies have caused raw sewage, microplastics and farm waste to be present in all of England's rivers, along with car tyre particles, oils and wet wipes, so people using rivers are now at risk.
  • Management strategies. Water transfer schemes, which attempt to make up for shortages by constructing systems of canals, pipes and dredging to move water from one river basin to another; groundwater management to regulate levels, pollution and use; limits on over-abstraction, which is when water is used faster than it is replaced; and water conservation — metering, efficient appliances and grey water recycling.

The UK's energy situation and its changing energy mix

Spec 3.2.3.1 · Resource Management Revision Mat; Case Study Summary Booklet quiz facts; 2023 Q03.2–03.3, 2024 Q03.1–03.3

  • The shift — away from fossil fuels and (slightly) nuclear, towards renewables.
  • The figures — 47% of UK energy was "green" in 2020; 35% of UK energy was imported in 2019.
  • Why fossil fuels fell — their environmental impact, and declining domestic supplies as North Sea reserves are depleted.
  • The costs of renewables — £22bn for a new nuclear station and £4m for a commercial wind turbine.
More detail
  • Why renewables rose — environmental benefits and the UK's legally binding carbon reduction targets.
  • Cost drives the switch — rising gas prices make renewables relatively cheaper, so investment moves.
  • The costs of renewables — £22bn for a new nuclear station, £4m for a commercial wind turbine, and high set-up costs generally.

The UK's changing energy mix — the facts

  • What the energy mix is. The range of energy sources of a region or country, both renewable and non-renewable.
  • The change. There is a shift from fossil fuels and nuclear to renewable energy sources. Nuclear energy has fallen slightly. The use of fossil fuels has decreased because of their environmental impact and declining supplies within the UK. There has been an increase in the use of renewables because of their environmental benefits and because they help the UK meet its targets to reduce carbon emissions.
  • The figures. In 2020, 47% of UK energy was "green". In 2019, 35% of UK energy was imported. The target for UK renewables by 2030 is 65%.
  • Why domestic gas supply has fallen. North Sea reserves are being depleted and the remaining fields are harder and more expensive to exploit, so the UK imports more — which exposes it to world prices and to the politics of supplier countries.
  • Why rising gas prices favour renewables. Once gas is expensive, the electricity it generates is expensive too, so wind and solar — which have high set-up costs but almost no fuel cost — become cheaper per unit by comparison; and expensive imported gas is an energy security risk, so there is a political incentive to replace it.
  • The economic issues with renewables. High set-up costs — a new nuclear power station costs around £22 billion to build and a commercial wind turbine around £4 million; tourism can decline where environments become less attractive; and low profitability is a concern.
  • The environmental issues with renewables. They are considered unattractive; wind turbines can affect wildlife, especially birds and bats; in upland areas large areas of land are flooded for reservoirs behind HEP dams; and biomass reduces biodiversity where monoculture crops replace mixed habitat.

✗ "The UK uses more renewables now because they are better for the environment." — one reason, undeveloped; the 2023 3-marker wanted the demand for different sources to change, with reasons.
✓ Three linked reasons: fossil fuels are declining domestically and their emissions drive climate change, so demand has fallen; renewables are needed to meet the UK's carbon targets — 65% by 2030 — so demand has risen; and because rising gas prices make gas-fired electricity expensive, renewables become comparatively cheaper, so investment follows.

Why global energy demand is rising, and global supply

Spec 3.2.3.4 · Energy Workbook pp. 9–10; 2024 Q06.4

  • Three drivers — economic development, population growth, and technology.
  • Economic development — industry, car ownership, appliances, heating and cooling all rise with income.
  • Population growth — more people need more homes, food, transport and goods, so total demand rises.
  • Technology also raises supply — fracking, deep-water drilling and Arctic exploration reach reserves that were previously inaccessible.
More detail

Energy consumption is increasing due to economic development, rising population and technology.

Economic development. As a country develops, industry expands and manufacturing is energy-intensive; incomes rise so people buy cars, fridges, washing machines, air conditioning and electronics; cities grow and need lighting, transport and construction; and services such as hospitals and offices need continuous power. This is why the 2022 scatter graph of GNI against energy consumption showed a clear positive relationship — the richer the country, the more energy each person uses. The 2024 3-marker asked for exactly this link.

Population growth. A larger population needs more homes to heat and light, more food to grow and process, more transport and more manufactured goods, so total national demand rises even where efficiency improves.

Technology — the positive and negative sides. Negatively, technology creates demand that did not exist: more devices per person, data centres, and the electrification of heating and transport. Positively, technology improves efficiency — LED lighting, better insulation, more efficient engines and smart controls all reduce the energy needed for the same output — and it increases supply, because fracking, deep-water drilling and polar exploration make reserves accessible that previously were not. 30% of the world's unexploited natural gas is in the Arctic.

The four factors affecting energy supply, which the spec names and the exam asks about one at a time:

  • Physical factors. Geology determines where fossil fuels and geothermal heat exist; climate determines where solar, wind and HEP work — the UK's position in the mid-latitude westerlies suits wind, and upland relief with high rainfall suits HEP; a coastline with a large tidal range suits tidal power. The 2025 3-marker asked for these directly.
  • Cost of exploitation and production. Deep, remote or small reserves cost more to extract than they are worth, so they stay in the ground until prices rise. Set-up costs for renewables are high — £22 billion for a nuclear station, £4 million for a wind turbine.
  • Technology. A reserve is only a resource if the technology exists to reach it. Fracking opened shale gas; improved turbine design made offshore wind viable.
  • Political factors. Governments decide whether to allow fracking or build nuclear; international agreements set carbon targets; and conflict interrupts supply — the 2023 examiner's report notes that most students answered this with "the current situation in Ukraine and the impact on Russian energy supplies".
  • The evidence — GNI and energy consumption per person are positively correlated across the world's countries.
  • Population growth — more people need more homes, food, transport and goods, so total demand rises even if per-head use does not.
  • Technology cuts both ways — it creates new demand (devices, data centres) and reduces it (efficiency, LEDs, insulation).

Energy insecurity and its impacts

Spec 3.2.3.4 · Energy Workbook pp. 12–13; AQA List of Key Terms; 2019 Q06.4, 2022 Q06.6, 2023 Q06.3

  • Energy insecurity — when supply cannot meet demand reliably or affordably; it is not an "energy deficit".
  • Industrial output falls — factories cannot run, so production, exports and jobs are lost.
  • Food production suffers — no power for irrigation, processing or refrigeration, so yields and food security fall.
  • Exploration of difficult environments and conflict — insecurity pushes countries into fragile areas and into disputes over supply.
More detail
  • Energy security — the uninterrupted availability of energy sources at an affordable price.
  • Prices rise and fuel poverty follows — in 2021 a measurable share of European households could not afford to heat their homes.

The impacts of energy insecurity

  • Industrial output. Manufacturing needs continuous, affordable power; blackouts and price spikes stop production, so output, exports and employment all fall and the economy shrinks.
  • Food production. Energy runs irrigation pumps, fertiliser manufacture, farm machinery, food processing and refrigeration. Energy insecurity therefore becomes food insecurity, and in LICs it can tip into undernutrition.
  • Industry and services. Hospitals, schools and water treatment all need power; in Rio, favela residents illegally tap the grid precisely because supply is unreliable, which is dangerous as well as insecure.
  • Rising prices and fuel poverty. Shortage raises prices, and households spend a greater share of income on heating and transport. The 2025 paper's figure measured the percentage of households in each country that could not afford to heat their homes in 2021 — a direct measure of insecurity in HICs as well as LICs.
  • Exploration of difficult and environmentally sensitive areas. Insecurity makes expensive, risky extraction worthwhile — deep-water drilling, tar sands, the Arctic (which holds 30% of the world's unexploited natural gas) and tropical rainforest, as at Camisea in Peru.
  • Conflict. Where supply and demand are in different countries, energy becomes political. Countries fight or threaten to fight over reserves and over the routes that carry them; a supplier can shut off a pipeline to apply pressure; disputed territory such as the Arctic or the South China Sea becomes contested because of what lies beneath it; and inside a country, extraction can provoke armed resistance — the Niger Delta is the clearest case.

✗ "Energy insecurity is when a country doesn't have enough energy." — the 2023 report says the only common error was defining energy deficit instead.
✓ "Energy insecurity is when energy availability is not enough to ensure the population enjoys good health, livelihood and earnings — the opposite of energy security, which is the uninterrupted availability of energy sources at an affordable price." Note the two halves: uninterrupted and affordable.

Strategies to increase energy supply — renewable and non-renewable

Spec 3.2.3.4 · Energy Workbook pp. 15–17; Resource Management Revision Mat; 2023 Q06.5

  • Renewables — biomass, wind, HEP, tidal, geothermal, wave, solar. Learn one advantage and one disadvantage each.
  • Reliability is the discriminator — tidal is predictable, HEP and geothermal are constant, wind and solar are intermittent.
  • Fossil fuels — still needed now, because renewables cannot yet meet demand, but finite and polluting.
  • Nuclear — reliable, low-carbon and high-output, but £22 billion a station, with waste that stays radioactive for millennia.
More detail

Non-renewable energy in the short term. Fossil fuels will continue to supply the majority of world energy for some time, because the infrastructure — power stations, pipelines, refineries, vehicle fleets — already exists, because renewables cannot yet meet total demand and are intermittent, and because fossil fuels remain cheaper in many places. Known reserves are still substantial and new technology keeps adding to them.

Non-renewable energy in the long term. They are finite, so supplies will eventually be exhausted; extraction becomes progressively more expensive as the easy reserves are used; and their emissions drive climate change, so international agreements and national targets — the UK's 65% renewables by 2030 — will force them out regardless of what remains underground.

Nuclear power. The energy released by a nuclear reaction, using fuel made from mined and processed uranium to make steam and generate electricity. It is reliable, very high output and almost carbon-free in operation, which is why the UK retains it. The concerns are the cost — around £22 billion for a new station — the radioactive waste, which remains dangerous for thousands of years and has no permanent disposal solution, the risk of accident, and the link to weapons technology.

The 2023 6-marker, "to what extent can wind and tidal power and other strategies increase energy supply?" The examiner's report is explicit about what separated the best answers: "understanding of the reliability of tides and wind power allowed appropriate discussions". So the argument is that tidal is predictable and wind is not, which is why wind needs backup capacity while tidal does not — and that the phrase "and other strategies" is an instruction to bring in nuclear, biomass, solar, HEP and fossil fuels rather than writing only about the figure.

  • Two families — renewable (not diminished by use) and non-renewable (finite, so depleted by use).
  • Fossil fuels in the short term — still needed, because renewables cannot yet meet demand and the infrastructure exists.
  • Fossil fuels in the long term — finite and polluting, so they must be replaced; the question is how fast.

More detail
  • What it is — a natural gas extraction and pipeline project in the Peruvian rainforest, in the Camisea region.
  • The economics — could save Peru US$4 billion in energy costs and earn US$34 billion over the project's 30-year life.
  • Employment — the gas industry built around it employs 1.2 million people.

The Camisea Natural Gas Pipeline Project, Peru

  • Location. The Camisea region of the Peruvian Amazon rainforest, with a pipeline carrying gas across the Andes to the coast.
  • Advantage — national energy costs. The project could save Peru US$4 billion in energy costs, because domestic gas replaces imported fuel.
  • Advantage — export earnings. Peru could make US$34 billion over the 30-year life of the project from exports, which is government revenue available for development.
  • Advantage — the cleanest fossil fuel. Natural gas produces 45% less CO₂ than the alternatives, so switching to it reduces emissions relative to coal or oil.
  • Advantage — employment. The industry employs 1.2 million people, with further employment opportunities in construction and operation.
  • Advantage — infrastructure. Improved infrastructure could benefit locals — the roads, power and services built for the project remain afterwards.
  • Disadvantage — deforestation. The deforestation associated with the pipeline destroys habitats, in one of the most biodiverse environments on Earth.
  • Disadvantage — landslides. Clearing routes for pipelines has led to landslides, because removing the forest removes the root network that binds steep Andean slopes.
  • Disadvantage — indigenous people. There is a direct impact on indigenous tribes whose land the project crosses, and locals may not have immunity to diseases brought by developers — a risk that has been lethal in previously uncontacted Amazonian groups.
  • Disadvantage — the method. Fracking is a controversial process, with concerns about groundwater contamination and induced seismicity.

Sustainable energy — conservation, and Chambamontera, Peru

  • Four conservation strands — home and workplace design, transport, reducing demand, and more efficient use of fossil fuels.
  • Chambamontera — a micro-hydro scheme in the Andes of Peru, supported by the charity Practical Action.

More detail
  • Sustainable energy supply — energy savings, efficiency measures and renewable sources, used without harming future generations.
  • Why there — isolated communities are cut off from the national grid, so local generation is the only realistic option.

Chambamontera micro-hydro scheme, Peru

  • Where. The Andes mountains, Peru — steep relief and reliable rainfall, which is exactly what micro-hydro needs.
  • Why it was introduced. Isolated communities are cut off from the main electricity power network, and extending the national grid to them would be prohibitively expensive, so there is a need for local power generation.
  • What it is. Hydroelectric power generation on a micro scale, supported by the charity Practical Action. The community had to pay some of the cost — US$51,000 in total, about $750 per family — which gives local people ownership of the scheme and a reason to maintain it.
  • Why it is sustainable. It uses renewable energy; it has a low environmental impact, because a micro-hydro scheme needs no large dam and floods no valley; it has low maintenance and running costs, so the community can keep it working without outside help — this is intermediate technology: simple, easily learned and easily maintained, serving local needs.
  • Benefits to the community. Electricity is reliable, and there is now electric street lighting, which improves safety and lets social and community life continue after dark. Light in homes extends the hours available for study, and power allows refrigeration of food and medicine.
  • Economic benefits. Electricity creates economic opportunities — small businesses can run machinery and lighting, produce can be processed and stored rather than sold immediately, and people are not spending income on kerosene or batteries.
  • Environmental benefits. No fossil fuels are burned, so there are no greenhouse gas emissions and no indoor air pollution from kerosene lamps or wood fires; no forest is cleared for fuelwood.

Energy conservation — the four strands, with AQA's own examples

  • Designing homes and workplaces. Insulating houses — cavity wall and loft insulation, double glazed windows — to keep heat in or out, reducing the need for heating or air conditioning; thermostats on radiators, computerised heating controls and smart meters; energy saving appliances; encouraging people to turn devices off rather than leave them on standby; large windows to make use of natural light; and LED lighting or lights with motion sensors in offices.
  • Transport. More fuel-efficient cars; carbon fibre cars that are lighter and more aerodynamic; electric and hybrid cars; car tax that encourages people to drive less polluting vehicles; investment in public transport, including hydrogen buses and electric trams; park and ride schemes; and clean air zones that charge vehicles to enter — London's ULEZ cut NO₂ by 44% in the original zone.
  • Reducing energy demand. Public information, pricing and regulation — appliance efficiency standards, building regulations, and the habit changes that follow from smart meters showing real-time cost.
  • Using technology to improve the efficiency of fossil fuels. Combined Heat and Power, which captures the heat a conventional power station wastes — East Village in Stratford uses a biomass CHP system and runs 30% below average energy use; more efficient turbines; and carbon capture.
  • Why each of these is sustainable, which is the half of the 2025 question most candidates skipped: less energy is needed to heat, cool and light buildings, so finite fossil fuels are used more slowly; decreased fossil fuel use means fewer greenhouse gas emissions from power stations, which contribute to climate change; less petrol and diesel means less oil extraction, which damages fragile environments and leaks during transport; fewer vehicles means fewer pollutants and better human health; schemes that cut congestion and raise public transport use reduce travel times, bringing economic benefits to business; and encouraging walking and cycling benefits health and wellbeing.

The 6-mark frame (and the 9-mark version if it appears)

Section C's extended answer is a 6-marker, not a 9-marker — there is one at the end of Question 03 and one at the end of Question 06. AQA marks them in levels, AO1 3 marks and AO2 3 marks, so the structure is tighter than a 9-marker: two or three developed paragraphs, evidence and explanation, plus discussion if the command word demands it, and no introduction.

  1. Name the example in the first clause. "The Camisea natural gas project in the Peruvian Amazon…" or "The Chambamontera micro-hydro scheme in the Andes of Peru…". The mark scheme states explicitly: max top Level 2 if the scheme is not named or cannot be inferred, and max top Level 2 if you use the wrong kind of scheme.
  2. Refer to the figure if there is one, then use it. "Figure 17 shows that…, which means…, and therefore…". The examiners' standing complaint is lifting without developing.
  3. Paragraph 1 — the first side, developed with figures. Point, explanation of the mechanism, evidence, link back to the question's words. For Camisea: the US$34 billion over 30 years, and why export revenue matters to an NEE.

03Understanding it

U1

The UK receives more than enough rainfall. So why does it need water transfer schemes?

Show answer

Because the water and the people are in different places. Rainfall is greatest in the west — the uplands of Wales, the Lake District and western Scotland, which face the prevailing south-westerly winds and force air to rise. Demand is greatest in the south-east, which is both the most densely populated region and the driest, so it runs a water deficit while the west runs a surplus.

Water transfer therefore moves water between river basins through canals, pipes and dredging — the only way to match a supply pattern set by physical geography to a demand pattern set by human geography.

The complication that earns the evaluation mark: demand is still rising. Consumption averages 310 litres per person per day, the population is increasingly concentrated in the south, dishwasher ownership rose from 22% to 49% between 1998 and 2018, and more homes have more than one bathroom. Demand is expected to exceed supply by 2034 — so transfer alone will not be enough without conservation.

U2

Why is buying local food not automatically better for the environment than importing it?

Show answer

Because food miles are only part of the carbon footprint. The other part is how the food was produced, and 12% of the UK's total greenhouse gas emissions come from agriculture — fertiliser manufacture, machinery, livestock methane and soil disturbance.

The case that matters is out-of-season produce. A UK strawberry in January has to be grown in a heated, lit glasshouse, which can emit more than a strawberry shipped from a country where it grows in the open air. The same logic applies to tomatoes and salad crops.

What local sourcing does achieve: far fewer food miles for food that is in season; fresher produce; money retained in the local economy, supporting farmers and shops; and transparency about how it was produced. The costs are limited choice, strict seasonality and often a higher price.

The exam point: this is why the 2023 and 2024 questions were phrased as "opportunities and challenges" and "creates opportunities and challenges". A one-sided answer about food miles misses half the marks.

U3

A country can hold huge energy reserves and still be energy insecure. How?

Show answer

Because energy security means uninterrupted availability at an affordable price, and reserves deliver neither on their own.

A reserve is not a supply. It becomes usable only if the technology exists to reach it and the cost of exploitation is less than the value of the energy — which is why 30% of the world's unexploited natural gas sits in the Arctic, unexploited. Political factors can also block it: a government may ban fracking or refuse nuclear.

Demand can outrun supply. The workbook's own framing: "even some countries with a high energy supply can experience energy insecurity where demand for energy is high". A rapidly developing economy adds demand faster than it can add capacity.

Distribution and conflict. Nigeria is the clearest case — it produces 2.2% of the world's oil, yet electricity supply is unreliable, oil wealth has provoked armed groups, and the revenue has not built the domestic grid.

And affordability is half the definition. The 2025 figure measured the share of households in each European country that could not afford to heat their homes in 2021 — insecurity in some of the richest countries on Earth.

U4

Why do examiners treat "tidal power is reliable" as a stronger point than "tidal power is clean"?

Show answer

Because clean is true of every renewable, so it does not discriminate between them, whereas reliability is the problem renewables actually have.

Electricity demand must be met at the moment it occurs; it cannot wait for the wind. Wind and solar are intermittent — no output when the wind drops, and solar is weakest in winter when UK demand peaks — so every unit of wind capacity needs backup capacity from gas, nuclear or storage, which adds cost and emissions. Tidal is predictable years in advance, because tides are astronomical, so it can be planned into the grid; geothermal and HEP are effectively constant, and HEP can be ramped up within minutes to match a demand spike.

The exam point: the 2023 examiner's report says of the 6-marker on wind and tidal power that "understanding of the reliability of tides and wind power allowed appropriate discussions in the best responses". That one idea is the difference between Level 2 and Level 3.

The counter to keep in reserve: tidal needs a very large tidal range, so it works almost nowhere, and barrages damage estuary ecosystems and bird feeding grounds. Reliability is not the only criterion.

U5

Camisea is described as extracting "the cleanest of the fossil fuels". Does that make it a sustainable project?

Show answer

No — "cleanest" is a relative claim, and sustainability is an absolute one.

What the claim does establish. Natural gas produces 45% less CO₂ than the alternatives, so if Camisea's gas displaces coal or oil, emissions fall. It also gives Peru domestic supply instead of imports, saving US$4 billion in energy costs, and US$34 billion of export revenue over 30 years that can fund development.

Why it is still not sustainable. Gas is non-renewable — the project has a stated 30-year life, which is the definition of a resource being depleted rather than sustained. And sustainable development means meeting present needs "without limiting the ability of future generations to meet their own needs": the deforestation along the pipeline, the landslides caused by clearing routes on steep Andean slopes, and the exposure of indigenous tribes to diseases they have no immunity to are all losses future generations inherit.

The contrast that makes the point: compare it with Chambamontera — renewable, low environmental impact, low running costs, community-owned, and with no stated end date. That is what a sustainable energy supply looks like, and it is why AQA pairs the two examples in the same spec bullet.

U6

Why did Practical Action make the Chambamontera community pay $750 per family rather than giving them the scheme?

Show answer

Because paying for it changes what happens afterwards. Contributing US$51,000 of the cost gives the community ownership, so the scheme is theirs to run and maintain rather than a donor's project that stops when the donor leaves. That is the whole logic of intermediate technology: simple, easily learned and easily maintained technology serving local needs, which can be repaired locally without imported parts or outside expertise.

It also avoids the problems the development-gap topic identifies with aid: donors deciding priorities, aid being diverted, and recipients becoming dependent rather than self-sufficient. The Chambamontera design makes the community the decision-maker.

Why micro-hydro specifically. The communities are cut off from the main electricity network in the Andes, and extending a national grid across mountains to scattered settlements would cost far more than it could ever recover. Local generation is not a second-best option here — it is the only economic one.

Use it like this: the same argument is the Favela-Bairro argument about legal land title, and the Fairtrade argument about the Gumutindo co-op. Development works when the people affected have a stake in it.

U7

Why do rising gas prices speed up the move to renewables, when renewables have high set-up costs?

Show answer

Because the two have completely different cost structures. A gas power station is relatively cheap to build and expensive to run, since it must buy fuel for every unit it generates. A wind turbine costs around £4 million up front — and a nuclear station around £22 billion — but has almost no fuel cost afterwards.

So when the gas price rises, every unit of gas-fired electricity gets more expensive while every unit of wind-generated electricity costs the same as before. Renewables become comparatively cheaper, so new investment moves towards them. This is exactly what the 2024 2-marker asked: "why might increasing gas prices mean that renewables become increasingly [important]?"

The second reason is security, not cost. The UK imported 35% of its energy in 2019 and domestic gas supply is falling as North Sea reserves deplete. Expensive imported gas is an energy security risk as well as an expense, because the price and the supply are both set abroad — so there is a political incentive to replace it, reinforced by the target of 65% renewables by 2030.

The limitation to acknowledge: high set-up costs still need financing, and renewables' intermittency means gas capacity has to be kept for backup even as its use falls. So the switch is slower than the price signal alone suggests.

U8

Energy conservation does not generate a single unit of electricity. Why does the spec treat it as a way of increasing sustainable supply?

Show answer

Because what matters is the balance of supply and demand, and reducing demand moves that balance exactly as adding supply would — AQA's own definition is that a sustainable energy supply "is the combination of energy savings, energy efficiency measures and technologies, as well as the use of renewable energy sources". Savings are listed first.

The chain the mark scheme wants. Less energy is needed to heat, cool and light buildings, so fossil fuels — which are running out — are used at a slower rate, which extends the time available to replace them. Decreased fossil fuel use means fewer greenhouse gas emissions from power stations, which contribute to climate change. Fewer petrol and diesel vehicles means less oil has to be obtained, which damages fragile environments during extraction and leaks during transport. And fewer vehicles releasing pollutants has a positive effect on human health.

It is also the cheapest option. Loft insulation costs a tiny fraction of the £22 billion for a nuclear station or the £4 million for a turbine, and it can be installed on existing buildings immediately rather than after a decade of construction.

And it brings co-benefits supply never does: schemes that cut congestion and raise public transport use reduce travel times, bringing economic benefits to businesses, while walking and cycling improve health and wellbeing. That is social, economic and environmental sustainability from one measure.

04Exam drill

What each command word actually wants

Section C is Question 03 (compulsory) plus Question 06 (Energy), worth around 25 marks. The school's exam technique guide opens with the rubric warning for exactly this section: "Don't answer the wrong questions. Know what options you are to choose." Then:

  • 1 mark — state / give / name / "what is…?" One clear point, or the exact definition. The reports show marks lost here for defining the wrong term — energy deficit instead of energy insecurity, food deficit instead of food insecurity.
  • 2 marks — outline / describe / explain how. Point plus development. For "describe the distribution", name continents and regions, give the overall pattern and then the anomaly — the reports repeatedly blame "poor locational knowledge" and "inaccurate compass points" and the use of "bottom of map" instead of south.
  • 2 marks — calculate. Show your working. Full marks for a correct answer even without working; one mark for correct working if the answer is wrong. Obey the rounding instruction exactly.
  • 3 marks — outline / suggest / explain. Either three separate developed points or one three-step chain. The mark schemes award each link in a chain: "economic development increases wages (1) and therefore tax receipts (1) which can be spent on better sanitation (1)".
  • 6 marks — explain / discuss / "to what extent", level marked, AO1 3 + AO2 3. Two or three developed paragraphs with evidence and explanation, plus counter-arguments and a short judgement if the command word is discuss, assess or to what extent. A named example is usually required: "max top Level 2 if the scheme is not named or cannot be inferred", and "max top Level 2 if a local renewable is used" where a fossil fuel was asked for. If the question names two elements — homes/workplaces and transport, advantages and disadvantages — covering only one also caps you at Level 2.
  • The maths. 10% of the paper's marks. In Section C this is percentage change, median, counting from a choropleth map and converting it to a percentage, graph completion and trend description. The reports say the same thing every year: "a significant minority were unable to perform a percentage calculation".

Twelve questions in the real spread: two short, four 4-markers, four 6-markers and two 9-markers. Section C's own extended answers are 6-markers, so the two 9-mark questions here are built from the Section C content in the shape the paper uses elsewhere — worth drilling, because resource management also underpins the Paper 3 issue evaluation. The 6- and 9-mark answers are written as plans, since AQA marks them in levels.

D1

What is energy insecurity? (2)

Show answer

When energy availability is not enough to ensure the population of an area enjoys good health, livelihood and earnings 1

— in other words, the opposite of energy security, which is the uninterrupted availability of energy sources at an affordable price, so a country can be insecure through unreliable supply or through cost 1

The 1-mark version needs only the first line. The 2023 report: "most correct unless students defined energy deficit."

D2

Outline one or more ways that physical factors can affect energy supply. (3)

Show answer

Geology determines where fossil fuels and geothermal heat exist, so a country without suitable rock cannot exploit them whatever its wealth — which is why 30% of the world's unexploited natural gas lies in the Arctic rather than where it is needed 1

Climate determines which renewables work: the UK's position in the mid-latitude westerlies makes it one of Europe's windiest countries, so wind power is viable, whereas solar output is weakest in winter when UK demand peaks 1

Relief and rainfall control hydro-electric power, because HEP needs steep gradients and a reliable flow — which is exactly why the Chambamontera micro-hydro scheme works in the Andes of Peru, and why a tidal scheme needs a coastline with a very large tidal range 1

D3

Explain why demand for different energy sources has changed in the UK. (4)

Show answer

Demand for fossil fuels has fallen because of their environmental impact: burning coal, oil and gas releases the greenhouse gases that drive climate change, so the UK has committed to cutting emissions and aims for 65% renewables by 2030 1

Domestic supplies of fossil fuels are also declining — North Sea gas reserves are being depleted and the remaining fields are harder and more expensive to exploit — so the UK had to import 35% of its energy in 2019, which is both costly and an energy security risk because the price and supply are set abroad 1

Demand for renewables has therefore risen sharply, and 47% of UK energy was green by 2020; wind suits the UK's position in the mid-latitude westerlies, and renewables have almost no fuel cost once built 1

Rising gas prices accelerate the switch, because gas-fired electricity gets more expensive with every price rise while wind and solar generation costs stay the same — so renewables become comparatively cheaper and new investment moves to them. Nuclear has fallen only slightly, because it is reliable and low-carbon even though a new station costs around £22 billion 1

D4

Suggest how energy supply issues can lead to conflict. (4)

Show answer

Energy reserves and energy demand are usually in different countries, so supply has to be traded — which gives a supplier political power, because it can raise prices or shut a pipeline to apply pressure, as Europe found over Russian gas 1

That dependence makes importing countries act to secure supply, by forming alliances, intervening in producer regions or racing to develop alternatives, which raises tension between states 1

Unexploited reserves make territory worth fighting over: 30% of the world's unexploited natural gas is thought to lie in the Arctic, so as the ice retreats several states have made overlapping claims to the seabed 1

Conflict also occurs within countries, between a government or TNC and the people whose land is being exploited — in the Niger Delta, oil wealth has provoked armed groups, and at Camisea in Peru the pipeline crosses the land of indigenous tribes who gain least from it 1

D5

Outline the link between increasing economic development and rising energy consumption. (4)

Show answer

As a country develops, its industrial base expands, and manufacturing is energy-intensive — so national demand rises with industrial output 1

Rising incomes change household consumption: people buy cars, fridges, washing machines, air conditioning and electronics, and heat and light larger homes, so energy use per person rises as well as in total 1

Development is also urbanising, and cities need continuous power for lighting, transport, construction, water treatment, hospitals and offices — which is why energy consumption per person is highest in HICs, with North America among the very highest in the world 1

The relationship is visible in the data as a positive correlation between GNI per person and energy consumption per person across the world's countries, so a scatter graph of the two shows consumption rising as GNI rises 1

D6

Explain how changing demand for food has affected the UK's carbon footprint. (4)

Show answer

Demand has grown for high-value exotic foods such as Madagascan vanilla and specialist honey, and 12% of the food eaten in the UK now comes from LICs, so food is travelling much further — raising food miles and therefore the emissions from air and sea freight 1

Demand for all-year seasonal produce has the same effect: strawberries can only be grown outdoors in the UK in summer, so out of season they are imported, or grown in heated and lit glasshouses, which itself consumes energy 1

Agribusiness has raised the footprint of UK-grown food too, because larger-scale farming uses more mechanisation and more chemicals — and fertiliser manufacture is energy-intensive, which is part of why 12% of the UK's total greenhouse gas emissions come from agriculture 1

Two changes pull the other way: rising demand for organic produce, which bans artificial fertilisers and builds soil fertility through crop rotation, compost, manure and clover, and rising demand for locally sourced food, which cuts the distribution distance sharply — so the net effect on the carbon footprint depends on which trend grows faster 1

D7

"Extraction of fossil fuels can bring both advantages and disadvantages." Explain this statement using an example you have studied. (6)

Show answer

Plan: name Camisea in the first clause, three developed advantages, three developed disadvantages, and a closing judgement. Level marked, AO1 3 + AO2 3. The mark scheme caps you at the top of Level 2 if the fossil fuel is not named or cannot be inferred, and again if you use a local renewable scheme instead.

The Camisea natural gas project extracts gas from the Peruvian Amazon and pipes it across the Andes to the coast, and its first advantage is national energy supply: it could save Peru US$4 billion in energy costs by replacing imported fuel with a domestic source, which also improves Peru's energy security because the price is no longer set abroad 1

The second advantage is revenue. Peru could make US$34 billion over the 30-year life of the project from exports, which is government money available for development spending on healthcare, water supply and education — and Peru's own 2025 exam figure showed the president involved in improving water and sanitation 1

The third is employment and infrastructure: the industry employs 1.2 million people, and the roads, power lines and services built for the project remain afterwards and could benefit local communities. Gas is also the cleanest of the fossil fuels, producing 45% less CO₂, so where it displaces coal or oil, emissions fall 1

However, the environmental cost falls on one of the most biodiverse environments on Earth. The deforestation associated with the pipeline destroys habitat permanently, because tropical rainforest regrows slowly on nutrient-poor latosols 1

Clearing routes for the pipelines has also led to landslides, because removing the forest removes the root network binding steep Andean slopes — so the damage spreads well beyond the cleared corridor itself 1

The human cost is sharpest: the project directly affects indigenous tribes whose land it crosses, and locals may not have immunity to diseases brought in by developers, a risk that has proved lethal to previously isolated Amazonian groups. Overall the advantages are real but temporary — the project has a stated 30-year life — whereas lost rainforest and lost lives are permanent, so the disadvantages are the more significant 1

D8

Suggest how energy supplies have been made more sustainable. Use an example of a local renewable energy scheme in an LIC/NEE. (6)

Show answer

Plan: name Chambamontera, say why local generation was chosen, then show the three strands of sustainability — environmental, economic, social. Level marked; the mark scheme requires a named local renewable scheme, so Camisea would cap you at Level 2.

The Chambamontera micro-hydro scheme in the Andes mountains of Peru generates hydroelectric power for isolated communities that are cut off from the main electricity network, where extending the national grid across mountainous terrain to scattered settlements would be prohibitively expensive — so local generation is the only realistic option 1

It is environmentally sustainable because hydro power is renewable, so the resource is not diminished by use, and no fossil fuels are burned — which means no greenhouse gas emissions and no indoor air pollution from the kerosene lamps or wood fires it replaces 1

Its environmental impact is also low because it is micro-scale: unlike a large HEP dam it floods no valley, displaces nobody and drowns no habitat, which matters because flooding large areas of upland is one of the standard objections to hydro power 1

It is economically sustainable because it has low maintenance and running costs and the community part-funded it — US$51,000 in total, about $750 per family, with support from the charity Practical Action 1

That contribution is the key to its durability: paying for the scheme gives local people ownership of it, and because it is intermediate technology — simple, easily learned and easily maintained — they can repair it themselves without imported parts or outside expertise, so it does not fail when the charity leaves and creates no aid dependence 1

It is socially and economically sustainable in its outcomes: electricity is now reliable and there is electric street lighting, which improves safety, extends the hours available for study and allows community life after dark, while power for machinery, lighting and refrigeration creates economic opportunities and removes the cost of kerosene and batteries. Overall, the scheme is sustainable on all three counts precisely because it was built at the scale the community can own and maintain 1

D9

To what extent can wind and tidal power and other strategies increase energy supply? (6)

Show answer

Plan: wind, then tidal, then "other strategies" — and the discriminator is reliability. The 2023 report: students "merely lifted information from the resource… and left little room to discuss other examples", whereas "understanding of the reliability of tides and wind power allowed appropriate discussions in the best responses".

Wind can increase supply substantially in the UK, because its position in the mid-latitude westerlies makes it among the windiest countries in Europe, and offshore turbines avoid most of the visual objection while catching stronger, steadier wind 1

However, wind is intermittent: there is no output when the wind drops or blows too hard, so every unit of wind capacity needs backup from gas, nuclear or storage, which adds cost and emissions. Each commercial turbine also costs around £4 million up front, and turbines can affect birds and bats 1

Tidal power's decisive advantage is that it is predictable — tides are astronomical, so output is known years in advance and can be planned into the grid rather than merely hoped for — and the UK has one of the world's largest tidal ranges, with very high energy density 1

However, tidal only works where the range is large, so it is geographically very restricted; barrages damage estuary ecosystems and the feeding grounds of wading birds; and capital costs are very high, so despite its reliability it contributes little at present 1

Other strategies can contribute more than either in the short term. HEP and geothermal are effectively constant, and HEP output can be raised within minutes to meet a demand spike; biomass uses waste and recently absorbed carbon, and Brazil cut its petrol consumption 40% with bio-ethanol; nuclear is reliable and almost carbon-free, though a new station costs around £22 billion; and solar is ideal for small-scale off-grid use, as at Santa Marta in Rio 1

Judgement: wind and tidal power can increase supply only partly on their own, because the one that is cheap is unreliable and the one that is reliable is geographically restricted and expensive. The UK reached 47% green energy by 2020 and targets 65% by 2030, but it will do so through a mix — renewables backed by nuclear and gas capacity, and reinforced by conservation, which reduces the demand that has to be met at all 1

D10

"Managing water quality and water pollution in the UK is a significant challenge." To what extent do you agree? (6)

Show answer

Plan: the sources of pollution, why they are hard to manage, the effects, then what is being managed successfully and a judgement. Level marked; "to what extent" requires both sides and a conclusion.

The challenge is significant first because the sources are numerous and varied: chemicals and pesticides from agriculture, untreated industrial waste, sewage pumped into rivers and seas, oil from boats and ships, and oil and heavy metals washed off roads 1

Several of these are diffuse rather than from a single pipe — fertiliser runs off thousands of fields and tyre particles wash off every road — so there is no single discharge to regulate, which makes enforcement far harder than closing one polluting factory 1

The effects are serious and economically costly: pesticides kill wildlife; fertilisers cause eutrophication, where nutrients from farmland and sewage produce algae that reduce oxygen levels and kill fish; drinking water is polluted; and fishermen and the tourist industry suffer economic losses 1

The scale is confirmed by the evidence put before Parliament — MPs concluded that "no river in England is clean", with raw sewage, microplastics, farm waste, car tyre particles, oils and wet wipes present in all of England's rivers, so people using rivers are now at risk 1

The challenge is also growing rather than shrinking: the Environment Agency names population growth, climate change and plastic pollution as the main future threats, and demand is expected to exceed supply by 2034, which concentrates pollutants in less water 1

However, there are effective strategies, and some have worked: sewage treatment, groundwater management to regulate levels and pollution, limits on over-abstraction, water transfer from the wet west to the dry south-east, and conservation through metering and grey water recycling. Judgement: I mostly agree — the strategies exist and are improving the worst point sources, but because the remaining pollution is diffuse, legal and driven by population growth and climate change, managing quality remains a significant and intensifying challenge 1

D11

"Reducing demand is more important than increasing supply in achieving a sustainable energy future."

To what extent do you agree with this statement? (9)

Show answer

Plan — intro defining sustainable energy supply; the case for conservation; the case for increasing supply; the complication; judgement that ranks them. Level marked. Add 3 SPaG marks if the paper awards them.

Intro. AQA defines a sustainable energy supply as "the combination of energy savings, energy efficiency measures and technologies, as well as the use of renewable energy sources" — so the real question is which of the three does most work, and conservation is listed first for a reason 1

The case for reducing demand — buildings. Cavity wall and loft insulation, double glazing, radiator thermostats, smart meters, energy-saving appliances, natural lighting and LEDs with motion sensors all cut the energy needed for the same comfort, and because they apply to existing buildings they take effect immediately 1

Why that is sustainability, not just saving. Less energy to heat, cool and light buildings means finite fossil fuels are used at a slower rate, which buys time to replace them; and less fossil fuel burned means fewer greenhouse gas emissions from power stations 1

The case for reducing demand — transport. Fuel-efficient and carbon fibre cars, electric and hybrid vehicles, car tax on polluting vehicles, hydrogen buses and electric trams, park and ride and clean air zones all reduce oil use — London's ULEZ cut NO₂ by 44% in the original zone. Less petrol and diesel means less oil extraction in fragile environments and fewer leaks in transport, and fewer pollutants improves human health 1

The cost advantage. Conservation is radically cheaper than capacity: loft insulation costs a fraction of the £4 million for a commercial wind turbine or the £22 billion for a new nuclear station, and it can be installed now rather than after a decade of construction 1

The case for increasing supply. Demand cannot be reduced indefinitely — economic development, population growth and the electrification of heating and transport all push consumption up, and energy is needed for industry, food production, water treatment and health care. Conservation alone leaves a large residual demand that must still be met 1

And supply decides what that demand is met with. The UK reached 47% green energy by 2020 and targets 65% by 2030; local schemes reach people a grid never will, as Chambamontera's micro-hydro does in the Andes, where the community part-funded it for US$51,000. Where there is no supply at all, conservation is meaningless 1

The complication. The two are not alternatives but complements, and conservation makes renewables work: because wind and solar are intermittent while tidal is predictable and geothermal and HEP constant, a grid built on renewables needs demand that is lower and more flexible. Lower demand shrinks the backup capacity needed, which is the main reason renewables still require gas 1

Conclusion. I mostly agree. Reducing demand is the more important strategy, because it is immediate, cheap, applicable to existing buildings and vehicles, and carries social and economic co-benefits that new capacity does not — but it cannot be the whole answer, since development and population growth guarantee a residual demand, and renewable supply is what determines whether that demand is met sustainably 1

D12

"The UK's changing energy mix creates more opportunities than problems."

Discuss this statement. (9)

Show answer

Plan — intro stating what the mix is changing to; the opportunities; the problems, economic and environmental; the security dimension; judgement. Level marked; discuss requires both sides, multiple supporting points, counter-arguments and a clear conclusion.

Intro. The UK's energy mix — the range of sources, renewable and non-renewable — is shifting away from fossil fuels and slightly away from nuclear towards renewables: 47% of UK energy was green by 2020, against a target of 65% by 2030 1

Opportunity — emissions. Renewables produce no greenhouse gases in operation, so the shift directly reduces the UK's contribution to climate change and is what allows it to meet its legally binding carbon targets 1

Opportunity — energy security. The UK imported 35% of its energy in 2019 while North Sea gas reserves decline, which exposes it to prices and politics set abroad; domestic wind, tidal, solar and HEP cannot be shut off by a supplier, so the shift reduces insecurity 1

Opportunity — cost and investment. Renewables have high set-up costs but almost no fuel cost, so when gas prices rise their electricity gets comparatively cheaper — and the construction and operation of offshore wind, tidal and nuclear capacity creates skilled employment and a multiplier effect in coastal and former industrial regions 1

Problem — reliability. Wind and solar are intermittent, and solar is weakest in winter when UK demand peaks, so the grid still needs backup capacity from gas or nuclear; only tidal is predictable, and geothermal and HEP constant, and both are geographically restricted in the UK 1

Problem — economic cost. A new nuclear power station costs around £22 billion and a commercial wind turbine around £4 million; low profitability is a recognised concern; and tourism can decline where environments become less attractive, which is a real cost in upland and coastal areas that depend on scenery 1

Problem — environmental cost. Renewables are not impact-free: turbines are considered visually unattractive and can affect birds and bats; large areas of upland land are flooded for reservoirs behind HEP dams; biomass reduces biodiversity where monoculture replaces mixed habitat; and nuclear produces waste that stays dangerous for thousands of years with no permanent disposal solution 1

Weighing the two. The problems are mostly transitional and technical — intermittency can be managed with storage, interconnectors and demand reduction, and set-up costs fall as deployment scales — whereas the problem the shift solves, climate change, is cumulative and irreversible. The strongest argument on the other side is that the backup capacity needed for intermittency keeps fossil fuels in the system longer than the headline percentages suggest 1

Conclusion. The changing energy mix creates more opportunities than problems, but only if it is paired with conservation: reducing demand through insulation, efficiency and transport measures shrinks the backup capacity intermittency requires, which is what turns a 47% renewable share into a genuinely sustainable supply rather than a renewable veneer on a gas-backed grid 1
