Paper 3: Issue Evaluation and Fieldwork

The whole of Paper 3 Geographical applications — Section A issue evaluation from the pre-release booklet (37 marks) and Section B fieldwork (39 marks), plus the geographical skills AQA can test on any of the three papers: OS maps and grid references, photographs, graphs and charts, statistics, and how to describe a distribution or a trend. 01How it actually works

Paper 3 is 1 hour 15 minutes, 76 marks, 30% of the GCSE, and it is the only paper where you are told what is coming. Section A — Issue evaluation, 37 marks is set on a pre-release resources booklet released about twelve weeks before the exam; you work through it in class, then sit the paper with a clean copy. Section B — Fieldwork, 39 marks splits in two: Question 4 is unseen fieldwork, somebody else's enquiry presented as data you have to read, analyse and criticise; Question 5 is familiar fieldwork, your own two enquiries, which you have to name on the paper. Six marks of SPaG are available, three on the Section A 9-marker and three on the Section B 9-marker.

The assessment objective split is what makes this paper different. Papers 1 and 2 are mostly AO1 (knowledge) and AO2 (understanding). Paper 3 is heavily AO3 — interpret, analyse and evaluate information to make judgements — and AO4 — select, adapt and use skills and techniques. That is why the examiners keep saying the same thing: copying the booklet is Level 1, using the booklet as evidence is Level 2, and linking points across the booklet to support a judgement is Level 3.

Section A: how the pre-release booklet actually works

Spec 3.3.1 Issue evaluation · Pre-Release Teaching Slides 2026, Lesson 1

  • Released twelve weeks early — the 2026 booklet was issued on Thursday 19 March 2026, so yours lands in late March 2027.
  • Three figures, always — the general problem, the range of possible solutions, then one specific proposed scheme with stakeholder views.
  • You annotate your copy in class, then hand it back — a clean, unmarked copy is issued in the exam.
  • The issue is synoptic — it is driven by one part of the course but reaches across the whole specification.
  • It always has a human and a physical side — the examiners call it the "human–environmental interface".
  • Some questions are about the place, some are general — read which, because answering the wrong one is self-limiting.
More detail

The specification promises that the sources "could include maps at different scales, diagrams, graphs, statistics, photographs, satellite images, sketches, extracts from published materials, and quotes from different interest groups". In practice the three figures do three different jobs, and knowing which is which tells you which figure to quote in which question:

The 2023 examiner's report makes the point explicitly, and it is the most useful sentence in all five reports: "There will be questions that specifically relate to the particular location of the issue and other questions that are more generally linked to the subject under discussion." In 2023, questions 2.2, 2.4 and 3 were about the Cayman Islands; questions 1.4 and 2.6 were broad discussions about tourism and development, and candidates who narrowed those two down to the Cayman Islands limited their own marks. The same trap works in reverse: a 2022 candidate who answered the general "waste as a resource opportunity" question entirely through the Cambridge incinerator was marked as self-limiting.

The twelve weeks are not for memorising the booklet — you get it back in the exam. They are for three things: annotating the figures so you know where every statistic lives, categorising the impacts of the proposal (social, economic, environmental; positive, negative), and ranking them by significance so that when a 9-marker asks for a judgement you already have one.

Reading the resources: using evidence rather than copying it

Spec 3.3.1 · Pre-Release Teaching Slides 2026, "Geographical applications – 9-marker advice"

  • Copying the booklet is Level 1 — even when the copied sentence is exactly the right sentence.
  • Developing the point is Level 2 — take the statistic, then say what it means and what it causes.
  • Linking across the booklet is Level 3 — this paper is synoptic, so joined-up points outscore separate ones.
  • Choosing which figure to use is itself a skill — you have the whole booklet, so pick the strongest evidence.
  • Always name the figure — "Figure 3 shows that…", "I can see from Figure 1 that…".
  • Don't argue against your own choice — balance is fine, but the stronger evidence must support your position.

✗ "Over 2000 local people have signed a petition to stop the development and 50 families will lose their homes." — accurate, relevant, and Level 1, because it is the booklet's words with nothing added.
✓ "Over 2000 local people have signed a petition, and more than 50 families will lose their homes — but the reservoir would supply 500 000 homes, including the 175 000 planned for Lincolnshire in the next five years, so the loss falls on a small local population while the benefit is regional. The scale of the two is not comparable, which is why I judge the scheme socially sustainable overall."

More detail

The teaching slides give the model of a Level 3 link, and it is worth learning the shape of it rather than the content: "the scheme will result in 50 families losing their homes, however in order to meet the rising housing demand of the area, water supply must be increased. The reservoir will provide water to 500,000 homes, including the 175,000 new homes which are planned to be built in Lincolnshire over the next 5 years". That is one sentence doing three things — a cost, a counter-argument, and a figure from a different page of the booklet that settles the comparison.

The school's own list of what separates the levels:

LevelWhat it looks like
Level 1 (basic)A limited number of appropriate points, mainly from one figure, largely copied or with limited development. An unsubstantiated judgement.
Level 2 (clear)Not just quoting — the points taken from the figures are developed. A structured response (intro, one way it is, one way it isn't). Good clarity and flow.
Level 3 (detailed)All of the above, plus an understanding of the synoptic nature of the issue: links between different aspects. Very clear grasp of the key concept (usually sustainability), a considered discussion of how complex the judgement is, specific points quoted, and evaluative observations throughout rather than only at the end.

Two further habits from the exam technique guide. For a photograph you cannot read, draw a mental cross through it and find one point in each quadrant. For any source-based answer, use the source as the structure: the headings in the figure become the paragraphs of your answer, which saves you planning time you do not have in 75 minutes.

The short Section A questions — what each mark size wants

Spec 3.3.1 and 3.4 · DC Exam Technique Guide; examiner's reports 2022 and 2023

  • 1 mark — read off, calculate or shade. The examiners expect "a high degree of accuracy"; bring a ruler and a calculator.
  • 2 marks for one reason — point plus development. A bare statement scores one and the examiners say over half of candidates stop there.
  • 2 marks for two things — two separate points, no development needed. Read which version you have.
  • 3 and 4 marks — develop with "this means that…", "as a result…", and refer to the figure by name.
  • 6 marks — two or three developed paragraphs with evidence; if the command is discuss, include the counter-argument.
  • Answer the command word — describe a pattern is not list the values, and explain is not identify.

✗ 2023 Q4.2, "Suggest what the map shows about the pattern of house prices": a list of areas A–G with their prices. Not creditworthy at all — listing values is not describing a pattern.
✓ "The highest prices are in the town centre and on the western edge; prices fall towards the east, so there is a clear west-to-east decline with a high-value core."

✗ 2023 Q5.1, "Suggest one reason why the chosen location was suitable": "It was near school." One mark only — the examiner's report says most candidates stopped exactly there.
✓ "It was near school, so travel took little time and we had longer on site to collect data." Point + development = two marks. Better still, link the development to the enquiry's aim.

Two structural rules from the guide. For a 6-marker: two or three main paragraphs with evidence and explanation, no introduction needed, but keep referring back to the question. For a 9-marker: a one-sentence introduction defining key terms and stating your overall argument, two or three paragraphs, and a one-to-two-sentence conclusion that reiterates the judgement.

Medium-length answers (3–6 marks) are rarely point-marked. Develop each point rather than adding more points, and do not worry about stating the obvious — the guide is explicit that this is often required.

The 9-mark decision question — how to justify a choice

Spec 3.3.1 · Pre-Release Teaching Slides 2026, Lesson 3; AQA mark schemes 2019–2025

  • It is nearly all AO3 — AO3 6 marks, AO4 3 marks, plus 3 for SPaG. The marks are for judgement, not recall.
  • Tick the box if there is one — and then use your strongest evidence on the side you ticked.
  • Two legitimate structures — by social / economic / environmental, or by positives / negatives. Pick one and hold it.
  • Sustainability is the recurring concept — meeting present needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs.
  • Short term versus long term is the easiest route to Level 3 — most schemes hurt locally now and help regionally later.
  • Finish with a conclusion that decides — the mark scheme penalises "a largely unsubstantiated judgement".

The structure the school teaches, worked on "Assess the sustainability of the proposed Lincolnshire reservoir project. [9]"

  1. Introduction — outline your argument. Overall, is the project socially, economically and environmentally sustainable? Define sustainability in one clause.
  2. Paragraph 1 — socially sustainable? Evidence and explain the social benefits, then recognise the short-term negative social impacts for local people.
  3. Paragraph 2 — environmentally sustainable? Evidence and explain the environmental positives and negatives.
  4. Paragraph 3 — economically sustainable? Evidence and explain the economic positives and negatives.
  5. Short conclusion — reiterate the argument and judge overall sustainability, referring to long-term impacts and whether the scheme will meet the region's rising demand.

The alternative structure — positives then negatives — works equally well, but it makes the synoptic links harder, because the link you want is usually between a social cost and an economic benefit.

✗ Choosing "No", then spending two-thirds of the answer on the advantages. The 2022 and 2023 reports both flag this: candidates "appeared to use evidence which was contrary to their choice, which was slightly self-defeating".
✓ Consider both sides — that is legitimate and often necessary — but make the stronger half of the evidence support your chosen position, and write a conclusion that brings the discussion back to it.

Worked example, part 1: the UK's water problem (2026 Figures 1 and 2)

Spec 3.3.1 · Geography Pre-Release Booklet 2026, pages 2–5

This is the 2026 booklet. Yours, in 2027, will be about something else entirely — so what matters here is the method, not the content. Work it through anyway: the questions AQA builds on a booklet are the same questions whatever the topic, and you will not get a second worked example on a real booklet.

  • The problem is a mismatch — the wet north and west have the water; the dry, crowded south-east has the demand.
  • Supply is falling, demand is rising — supply down 7% by 2045 from climate change, demand up 4 billion litres a day by 2050.
  • Two families of solution — increase supply (reservoirs, transfers, desalination, recycling) or reduce demand (efficiency, leakage, behaviour).
  • Leakage is the free win — over 3 billion litres a day lost in England, roughly 20% of supply.
  • People massively underestimate their own use — 57 litres estimated against 140 litres actual.
  • Water quality is part of the supply problem — polluted rivers are sources you cannot use.

UK water supply and demand — the figures that earn marks

  • Supply. Water companies supply over 16 billion litres a day, mostly to residential customers. Surface water is 64% of supply in England and 93% in Wales; England and Wales have 2259 groundwater sources and Scotland 86.
  • The rainfall gradient. Annual average rainfall 1991–2020 ranges from over 2000 mm in the north and west to under 800 mm in the south-east — which is the most heavily populated part of the UK. "Effective rainfall", the part left after evaporation and plant use, runs from more than 2500 mm a year in parts of the north-west to less than 200 mm in parts of eastern England.
  • Seasonal demand. Summer demand can rise by over 20% just as rainfall is lowest; in the hot, dry June of 2020 some companies reported demand up over 70%. Within a day, use spikes in the afternoon and evening as people water gardens.
  • Rising demand. Average household water use is up 70% since 1985. Demand is forecast to rise by about 4 billion litres a day by 2050, and it will not be spread evenly: of the increase in England 2020–2050, the South East takes 50%, the West 20%, Central and East 18%, the North 6% and the South West 6%.
  • Falling supply. Total supply is predicted to fall 7% by 2045 because of climate change, while groundwater use needs to come down for environmental reasons. Between 2020 and 2050 a year with drought restrictions is twice as likely as in the past. The Environment Agency's line: without action there will not be enough water in about 20 years.
  • Per-person use. The average person uses 140 litres a day; the Environment Agency thinks 100 litres or less is achievable within 20 years. Government targets: 122 litres per person per day by 2038 and 110 by 2050. A 2023 survey found people estimated their own use at 57 litres against an actual 140.
  • Where household water goes. Flushing toilets 30%, personal washing 25%, washing machines 21%, washing up 8%, garden 7%, drinking 4%, other 5%. Toilets being the biggest slice is why rainwater harvesting into an underground tank is the strongest home measure.
  • Leakage. Over 3 billion litres a day lost in England, about 20% of supply. Fixing it reduces abstraction from rivers and aquifers as well as serving customers.
  • Pollution. The Environment Agency recorded 325 category 1 and 2 incidents in 2021, up 24% on 2020's 263; 2022 was 307. Causes: illegal dumping, waste treatment, discharged untreated sewage and farming. The two most common pollutants in rivers were untreated sewage and agricultural slurry.
  • The solutions on offer. Water recycling; greywater from showers, baths and washing machines reused in the garden; desalination — the Thames plant makes 140–150 million litres a day, but plants are expensive to build and run; reservoirs storing winter surplus for transfer; reducing pollution; and awareness campaigns.
More detail

The booklet's water balance graph is the one diagram worth being able to explain, because a "describe the pattern" question on it is nearly free marks. It shows rainfall per day against evaporation per day across the year, in four phases:

PhaseWhat is happening
Groundwater surplusRainfall exceeds evaporation, so water soaks in and fills aquifers; supply can rely on rainfall and surface water.
Groundwater utilisationEvaporation rises above rainfall, so surface water alone is not enough and water is extracted from aquifers.
Groundwater deficitToo much has been taken; aquifers run low and rainfall is still too low to refill them.
Groundwater rechargeRainfall climbs back above evaporation, so rain percolates down and refills the aquifers.

The four key terms the slides define, which are the ones to use in any answer on UK water: groundwater — water that infiltrates and percolates through soil into rock and is stored in aquifers; surface water — water that stays on the surface and flows into rivers and streams, including lakes and reservoirs; water storage — a large volume of water kept, naturally in lakes or artificially in reservoirs; water transfer — the artificial movement of water by pipe and pump from an area of surplus to an area of deficit.

The single most useful analytical sentence about Figure 1, and the one the slides lead with: the South-East has the lowest rainfall in the UK but the highest water demand. Every 6-marker on "why is managing UK water increasingly difficult" starts there and then adds the three multipliers — population growth concentrated in the driest region, climate change cutting supply and making droughts twice as likely, and rising per-person consumption.

On the solutions, the slides set a specific task worth repeating for your own booklet: annotate each strategy as affecting supply or demand, then rank them by effectiveness. The ranking is the part that becomes evaluation marks later. A defensible ranking here: leakage reduction first (20% of supply, no new infrastructure), then water-efficient homes and rainwater harvesting (toilets alone are 30% of use), then reservoirs and transfers (big, slow, expensive, but the only thing that adds real volume), then desalination last (expensive to build and run, energy-hungry for a country that is not short of rain overall).

Worked example, part 2: the proposed Lincolnshire reservoir (2026 Figure 3)

Spec 3.3.1 · Geography Pre-Release Booklet 2026, pages 5–7; Pre-Release A3 Evaluation Grid

  • Anglian Water's proposal — a new reservoir and water transfer system south-east of Sleaford, Lincolnshire.
  • It works by seasonal storage — take winter water from the Trent and Witham, store it, treat it, supply it in summer.
  • The site was chosen on physical factors — suitability of local river systems and geology, assessed against other options.
  • The scale is the argument for it — 55 million m³, up to 166 million litres a day, 500 000 homes, £2.6 billion of investment.
  • The locality is the argument against it — over 50 families lose homes, farmland floods, construction closes roads.
  • Everyone agrees water is needed — including the NFU, so the dispute is about this scheme at this scale, not about reservoirs.

The proposed Lincolnshire reservoir, Anglian Water — facts and figures

  • Location. South-east of Sleaford, Lincolnshire, between the villages of Scredington, Helpringham and Swaton. Lincolnshire is one of the driest parts of the UK and has a rapidly growing population, with 175 000 new homes planned over the next five years.
  • Mechanism. Water is taken from the River Trent and River Witham in winter when levels are highest, transferred to the reservoir, then treated and supplied in the hotter summer months. An overflow drain manages levels and reduces winter flood risk.
  • Capacity. 55 million cubic metres of water; supplying up to 166 million litres a day to 500 000 homes; bringing £2.6 billion of direct and indirect investment to the area.
  • Design concessions. Embankments deliberately gently sloping to be less visually intrusive; the shape designed to encourage wildlife and to be attractive to visit; local habitats to be restored on completion, increasing the diversity of wildlife.
  • Stakeholders consulted. The Environment Agency, Natural England, local authorities, the agricultural industry, and local community groups.
  • Opportunities. Open for public use: sailing, wind surfing, cycling, walking. A network of footpaths and cycle tracks; an education centre and visitor centre; new wildlife and wetland habitats; improved public transport; renewable energy opportunities. Expected to attract thousands of visitors a year, creating opportunities for local business.
  • Opposition. Over 2000 local people signed a petition against it. More than 50 families will lose their homes; local businesses will suffer and jobs will go. During construction some local roads will close and others become congested — narrow country roads carrying construction traffic, with an increased risk of accidents. One resident's alternative: smaller reservoirs nearer major towns, plus tackling leakage and pollution.
  • The farmers. Flooding a large area removes homes and livelihoods. The uncertainty is itself damaging — it is affecting mental health and makes further investment in the farms pointless, at a time when farmers are being encouraged to grow and sell more food locally. Other businesses, such as horse-riding stables, will go too.
  • The NFU's position — the most useful source in the booklet, because it is on both sides. It fully supports expanding water supply infrastructure as a response to climate change and population growth, and says farming businesses will benefit from the extra water and that resilience in domestic food production matters. But it warns that large-scale schemes significantly harm local farm businesses and communities, and insists reservoirs be designed to fit the local environment, disruption be minimised, and local people be actively involved in decision-making at every stage.
  • The honest caveat, in Anglian Water's own words. The outlined area on the map is "only indicative and is likely to change as proposals develop" — worth quoting, because it supports the Local-Authority-style point that there is a gap between a plan and a reality.

Section B: the six enquiry strands, and the vocabulary that scores

Spec 3.3.2 Fieldwork · Fieldwork Revision Booklet 2026, Part 1

  • Every Section B question maps onto one of six strands — question, methods, presentation, analysis, conclusion, evaluation.
  • You must name both enquiries on the paper — titles have their own lines, and the answers must match the title you wrote.
  • The school used questions, not hypotheses — deliberately, because a question allows a "partly" answer when the evidence is mixed.
  • Six buzz words carry the evaluation marks — accurate, representative, reliable, valid, subjective, bias.
  • Two enquiries, contrasting environments, one physical and one human — and at least one must show physical–human interaction.
  • Risk assessment has six stages — and the risk score is severity (1–5) × likelihood (1–5), to a maximum of 25.

Sampling. It is never possible to measure everything — nobody is sizing every pebble on Brighton beach — so you sample, and the point of sampling is to reduce bias. Random sampling is based on chance, so everything has an equal probability of selection and bias is minimised, but coverage can be patchy. Systematic sampling takes readings at regular intervals — every 2 m along the groyne — which guarantees even coverage and is easy to justify, but can miss variation between the intervals and can coincide with a pattern in the landscape. Stratified sampling divides the population into groups and samples each in proportion, which is what the Stratford questionnaire needed and did not have: three random people per site missed whole age groups.

Risk assessment has six stages: identify the hazards; decide who might be at risk and how; work out the level of risk as severity (1–5) × likelihood (1–5), maximum 25; decide how it can be reduced or managed; record the findings and act on them; review and update. A 2-mark question — "outline one reason why it was important to complete a risk assessment" (2024) — wants the reason, not the list.

John's physical enquiry: groynes at Brighton Beach

Spec 3.3.2, strands 1–6 · Brighton Fieldwork Model Answers; Fieldwork Evaluation Summary

  • The title — "Evaluate the success of groynes as a method of coastal management on Brighton beach."
  • The theory — groynes interrupt longshore drift, trapping sediment updrift and maintaining a protective beach.
  • Two methods — beach slope profiles at 2 m systematic intervals, and a bi-polar analysis survey from −3 to +3.
  • The result — far more sediment on the west (updrift) side, a clear deficit on the east (downdrift) side.
  • The conclusion — the groynes work: they stop longshore drift and build a protective beach.
  • Learn this one for the 9-marker — the school's explicit advice, because it is more straightforward to analyse.

Brighton, East Sussex — the enquiry in the detail the exam wants

  • Aim. To investigate whether groynes have been a successful method of coastal management on Brighton beach. To count as successful they must stop longshore drift and help maintain the protective beach.
  • Location, and why it suited. A coastal town in East Sussex, approximately 80 km south of London — accessible by coach in a day. It is a tourist beach, so relatively safe. And there are many groynes along this stretch, giving a choice of sample sites for measuring the success of coastal management. Longshore drift here runs west to east, so west is updrift and east is downdrift.
  • Risk, and how it was reduced. Powerful destructive waves were approaching the coastline, so measurements were taken at a safe distance from the breaking waves, and teachers had checked tide times to ensure enough safe beach. Alternatively: the steep pebble beach risked slipping, so appropriate footwear was worn and work was done sensibly in small groups.
  • Method 1 — beach slope profiles. Measuring the build-up and depletion of sediment on the updrift (west) and downdrift (east) side of a groyne, to see whether the groynes were stopping longshore drift. Measured from just before the waves broke (for safety) inland towards the sea wall, at 2 m systematic intervals for a detailed picture. Done on three adjacent groynes, all concrete, out of a roughly 3 km stretch of coast.
  • Method 2 — bi-polar analysis survey. Rating each management strategy from −3 to +3 on four variables: vulnerability to erosion, aesthetic value, lifespan, and disturbance to natural processes and habitats. This turns a qualitative judgement into a number that can be plotted, and allows the groynes to be compared with the sea wall.
  • Presentation. Two slope profiles drawn as line graphs, one for each side of the groyne — appropriate because sediment height is continuous data and a line shows how height and angle change from shoreline to sea wall. The groyne shaded grey and the beach yellow so the two profiles could be compared visually. A bar chart of the bi-polar scores comparing the sea wall with the groynes — appropriate for category data, and the negative half of the y-axis shows where each strategy fails.
  • Analysis and conclusion. The west side of the groyne had markedly more sediment, because longshore drift was interrupted and sediment trapped; the east side had a significant deficit, suggesting the groyne was blocking sediment transport along the beach. Conclusion: the groynes are a successful method of coastal management — they stop longshore drift and build up a protective beach.
  • Evaluation — accuracy. Only three groynes out of a 3 km stretch, so no complete picture of the whole coastline; only concrete groynes, so no comparison with wooden ones. Sampling at 2 m intervals leaves variation between intervals unmeasured. Tape measures not laid completely flat, and the drop from the top of the groyne often exceeded a 1 m ruler, on edges that were not straight. Beach profiles are also strongly affected by tide — low tide is needed for reliable access — and ranging poles can sink or go out of alignment while clinometer readings vary between people.
  • Evaluation — reliability. Data collected on one day in October, so no seasonal comparison. Geographical theory says destructive waves in winter produce steeper beaches and constructive waves in summer gentler profiles, so October's stronger winds and more powerful destructive waves may have shaped both profiles. The bi-polar survey is subjective: scores from other groups showed a great range of variation, and judging lifespan or vulnerability to erosion properly needs secondary data the class did not have.
  • Improvements. Repeat measurements on more groynes along the full 3 km; collect in two contrasting seasons; compare concrete with wooden groynes; standardise the bi-polar scoring before starting; plot the bi-polar results on a digital map using GIS so different parts of the beach can be compared spatially.

John's human enquiry: Olympic regeneration at Stratford

Spec 3.3.2, strands 1–6 · Stratford Fieldwork Model Answers; Fieldwork Evaluation Summary

  • The title — "To what extent has the Olympic Regeneration of Stratford been successful?"
  • The theory — regeneration of a brownfield site brings social, economic and environmental change, unevenly.
  • Two methods — a three-question questionnaire, and environmental quality surveys at five sites A–E.
  • The result — all five sites scored positively, but the inside-the-park sites scored far higher than the fringes.
  • The conclusion — largely successful, especially environmentally, but not evenly across the area.
  • The weakness is the sample — three people per site, one weekday, probably more tourists than residents.

Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park, Stratford, East London — the enquiry in detail

  • Aim. To investigate how successful the regeneration of Stratford has been, as a result of the 2012 Olympic Games and the wider investment in the area.
  • Location, and why it suited. The Olympic Park lies north of the River Thames in East London, 45–60 minutes from school by train or coach — accessible. The Games ended in 2012 but regeneration continued and is ongoing, so the project is contemporary. It is the transformation of a brownfield site, with social, economic and environmental impacts all available to study. The former Athletes' Village is now the residential area East Village.
  • Risk, and how it was reduced. There are canals and waterways in the park, so a safe distance was kept from the water's edge and sensible behaviour was enforced. Alternatively: the site is large and getting lost was a risk, so maps and mobile phones were used and emergency contact numbers carried.
  • Method 1 — questionnaire. Three questions, asked of three people at each of the five sites. Q1 age band (16–25, 25–35, 35–45, 45–55, 60+) to see whether regeneration had affected age groups differently. Q2, a closed question: "To what extent do you think this area has benefited from the Olympic Park redevelopment?" — categories "A lot", "Somewhat", "Unsure", "Not benefited" — giving data that could be categorised. Q3, an open question: three words to describe the environment here, giving a "sense of place" and letting perception be compared with secondary data.
  • Method 2 — environmental quality survey. Five EQSs at five sites A–E, scoring four categories: buildings, traffic, open space and general quality. The class stayed in one position at each site for the whole survey, because sites looked different from different aspects. This allowed quantitative comparison between the sites and a test of whether regeneration had reached all of them equally.
  • The results. Site B, the London Blossom Garden, ranked highest with an EQS score of +23; every site scored positively. Site A, near Hackney Wick station, scored lowest for building quality — it is peripheral to the park, has grown since the 1990s with artists in converted warehouses, and has graffiti and more traffic. On the questionnaire, 75% of responses to Q2 were "a lot", and 100% were "a lot" or "somewhat".
  • Presentation. EQS data as four bar charts, one per category — bar charts suit discrete, categorical data and let site characteristics be compared visually, and they turn subjective scores into plottable quantities. Questionnaire Q3 as a word cloud per site, which suits subjective qualitative data and sizes the most frequent words for emphasis — clean, safe, green.
  • Analysis and conclusion. Positive change to buildings, traffic and environment across all five sites, but not equally: the sites inside the park (the Blossom Garden, the 2012 Gardens) had the highest environmental quality, while the fringes such as Hackney Wick had been less affected. The questionnaire data aligned with the EQS — people were more positive about inside-the-park sites — which strengthens the conclusion. Conclusion: the regeneration has been largely successful, especially environmentally.
  • Evaluation — accuracy and bias. The EQS is subjective; classmates scored the same sites differently, showing bias. Taking a group average of the total for each site removed outliers and improved accuracy and reliability. Environmental variation within a site can skew the score, and a single observer's results need benchmarking for consistency.
  • Evaluation — the sample. Three people per site is not a large enough sample, which reduces reliability. Data was collected one weekday, in the middle of the day: local residents — the people who actually experience the regeneration — were likely at work, so the sample probably over-represented tourists with a superficial view. A weekend would have reached more locals. Stratified sampling should have been used to guarantee a spread of age groups. Some participants had no historical knowledge of the area, which limits how useful their comparison can be. And a sunny, quiet day may have pushed responses positive.
  • Improvements. A larger and stratified sample across different days and times; bar charts or proportional symbols plotted on a map, ideally using GIS, to give a spatial view of inequality — more traffic by a main road, less by a park. Add secondary economic data, because most of the primary data was qualitative and descriptive: deprivation indices from gov.uk, and local business growth from the government's Business Register and Employment Survey (BRES).

✗ "We did an EQS and it showed the regeneration was successful." No title, no site count, no score, no limitation — and the examiners report that answers like this score one mark out of six.
✓ "At five sites across Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park we scored buildings, traffic, open space and general quality. Site B, the London Blossom Garden, scored +23, the highest; Site A near Hackney Wick scored lowest for building quality. Because the scoring is subjective we took a group average, which removed outliers — so the conclusion that regeneration has been uneven is reasonably valid."

OS maps: grid references, scale, distance, direction and relief

Spec 3.4.1 Cartographic skills · examiner's reports 2022 and 2024

  • Eastings before northings — along the corridor, then up the stairs. Always.
  • Four figures name a square, six figures name a point — the extra digits are tenths of a square, so 100 m.
  • Each grid square is 1 km by 1 km — which makes it 1 km² and gives you distance and area for free.
  • Scale — at 1:50 000, 2 cm on the map is 1 km; at 1:25 000, 4 cm is 1 km.
  • Relief from contours and spot heights — close contours mean steep, wide spacing means gentle, a lone spot height still counts.
  • "Physical geography" means relief and drainage — height, slope, rivers, lakes, ponds, woodland. Not roads and buildings.

✗ 2022 Q2.2, "Using map evidence, describe the physical geography of the area surrounding the proposed incinerator": roads and specific buildings. The examiner's report says very few candidates appeared to understand the term "physical geography" at all.
✓ "The land is low-lying and flat — a single contour line crosses the area and there is a spot height of 4 m just north of the proposed site. There are streams, lakes and ponds nearby, so drainage is dense and the water table is likely to be high."

More detail

Reading a grid reference. Take the 2026 booklet's OS extract of the reservoir site: its eastings run 08 to 16 and its northings 36 to 44. A question asking "what is found in grid square 1337?" means the square whose south-west corner is easting 13, northing 37 — go along to 13, then up to 37, and the square is the one up and to the right of that corner. For a six-figure reference you divide the square into tenths: 134 376 means easting 13 and four-tenths across, northing 37 and six-tenths up. Two habits stop most errors: always write the easting pair first, and remember that the number you read is the line to the left and below your point, never the nearest line.

Distance. Count grid squares — each is 1 km — and estimate the fraction. For a straight line, use the edge of a ruler or the straight edge of the answer booklet against the scale bar; for a curved line, mark a strip of paper along the route in short straight sections. The multiple-choice versions (2022 and 2024) only need you to get within a kilometre, which counting squares does comfortably. For area, count the whole squares inside the shape, then count part-squares and treat roughly two halves as one — so the answer comes out in km².

Direction. Give the eight points of the compass, and say it from the right feature. 2024 asked the direction of Paddock Wood from the proposed development: the answer was east. Getting the two features the wrong way round is the standard way to lose this mark.

Relief and drainage, which is what "describe the physical geography" means. The 2024 mark scheme shows the two levels clearly: Level 1 is "high land and low land, and a river"; Level 2 uses the map "with some precision" — the slope, the direction of flow of rivers, the height of the land. So the answer that reaches Level 2 looks like: "The land is low-lying, rising slightly towards the north, with gentle, relatively flat slopes" — a statement about height, a statement about gradient, and a direction, each one traceable to something on the map.

What else AQA can ask from an OS map. The specification lists it: four- and six-figure references; scale, distance and direction including curved-line distance; gradient, contour and spot height; identifying landscape features and describing their characteristics; relating cross-sections to relief features; drawing inferences about patterns of relief, drainage, settlement, communications and land use; interpreting transects; describing coastal, fluvial or glacial landscapes from a large-scale map; and inferring human activity, including tourism, from map evidence. The 2019 paper asked you to complete a cross-section from a contour map and then describe the slope from a point to the river — so cross-sections belong in the unseen fieldwork section too.

Photographs. Ground, aerial and satellite photographs can all appear, and the skills are: describe the human and physical landscape in them, draw a sketch from one, and annotate rather than merely label — a label names a feature, an annotation explains it. A field sketch beats a photograph when you want to select: you can leave out clutter, exaggerate what matters and write explanation onto the drawing. A photograph beats a field sketch for accuracy and speed, and it cannot be accused of bias in what it includes.

Statistics, trends and distributions: the numbers and the words

Spec 3.4.3 and 3.4.4 Numerical and statistical skills · mark schemes 2019, 2022, 2024, 2025

  • Five measures, all examinable by calculation — mean, median, mode, range, interquartile range.
  • Percentage change = (change ÷ original) × 100 — the original, not the new value, on the bottom.
  • Measures of central tendency have limits — and a 4-mark question in 2019 was entirely about those limits.
  • Describe a distribution with location — where the high values are, where the low ones are, and the direction of change.
  • Describe a trend with shape and data — overall direction, rate of change, peaks and troughs, anomalies, with figures.
  • Describe a relationship with its strength — positive or negative, strong or weak, and name the anomaly.

The five measures, worked on the 2019 housing quality data

Twelve areas scored for housing quality on a 1–10 scale: 4, 6, 6, 2, 9, 3, 10, 1, 5, 8, 10, 1. Sorted: 1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 6, 8, 9, 10, 10.

  • Mean — add them and divide by how many. 65 ÷ 12 = 5.41 (the mark scheme accepts "approximately 5.5"). Uses every value, which is its strength and its weakness: one extreme reading drags it.
  • Median — the middle value when ordered. Twelve values, so average the 6th and 7th: (5 + 6) ÷ 2 = 5.5. Unaffected by extremes, which is why it beats the mean on skewed data.
  • Mode — the most common value. Here 1, 6 and 10 each appear twice, so the data is trimodal and the mode cannot usefully be calculated — which is exactly the point the mark scheme wanted. Saying "there is no single mode, because the data is trimodal" is a credit-worthy observation, not a failure.
  • Range — highest minus lowest. 10 − 1 = 9. One number for the whole spread, but it is set entirely by the two most extreme readings.
  • Interquartile range — upper quartile minus lower quartile. Lower half (1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5) gives Q1 = 2.5; upper half (6, 6, 8, 9, 10, 10) gives Q3 = 8.5; IQR = 6. It describes the spread of the middle 50%, so it ignores extremes — which is why the 2019 mark scheme names it as a measure that "might be more useful".

The 2019 question was "Assess the usefulness of measures of central tendency… in analysing the housing quality data" [4], and the mark scheme's key indicative point is the one students miss: measures of central tendency have limited use here, because the enquiry is really about identifying differences and patterns between areas — an average of twelve different areas hides exactly what you went to find out. Two or more measures must be discussed for all four marks; one measure discussed clearly reaches low Level 2; and evidence of correct calculation alone is capped at one mark.

More detail

The calculations AQA has actually set. 2019: fill in a table of percentage loss of countryside, given the original area and the area lost (24 of 240 = 10%; 160 of 320 = 50%; 84 of 420 = 20%). 2022: total an environmental quality score across a bi-polar table, then complete mean, mode, median and range for thirteen beach widths — the answers were mean 17.6, mode 17, median 17.5, range 4. 2023: total three components of cruise spending. 2024: a percentage from a frequency table (29 of 50 people = 58%), and a total and mean for an EQS column (4+4+4+3+4+5 = 24, mean 4). 2025: a mean of six river widths (1680 ÷ 6 = 280 cm) and the range of six maximum depths (46 − 24 = 22 cm), and a straight-line distance from a scaled diagram.

Percentage change. (new − original) ÷ original × 100. Water pollution incidents rose from 263 in 2020 to 325 in 2021: 62 ÷ 263 × 100 = 24%, which is the figure the 2026 booklet quotes. Running it the wrong way round — dividing by the new value — gives 19% and loses the mark. For a decrease, the answer is negative or stated as "a fall of n%", and the original is still the denominator.

Describing a distribution. A distribution is spatial, so the answer needs location words: where the highest values are, where the lowest are, and the direction of change between them. The model for the 2026 rainfall map: "The northern and western parts of the UK are the wettest, with over 2000 mm a year in the north-west of Scotland and parts of Wales, while the south and east are the driest, with under 800 mm in the south-east — so rainfall decreases from north-west to south-east." Three components: highest with a figure and a place, lowest with a figure and a place, and the gradient between them. The 2023 report adds the Level 2 refinement for a map: use direction and distance, and comment on the rate of change — "temperature falls with distance from the town centre, but more steeply towards the west".

Describing a trend. A trend is temporal. Give the overall direction first, then the rate, then the detail: "Recycling rates rose steeply between 2000 and 2010, then levelled off after about 2012, remaining close to 45% to 2019 — so although the long-term trend is upward, progress has stalled for the last seven years." Quote the start and end values, name any peak or trough with its year, and mention anomalies if there are any — and if there are none, the exam technique guide says do not worry about it.

Describing a relationship. Name the direction and the strength, then evidence both: "There is a weak positive relationship — generally the further visitors travelled the longer they stayed, with those travelling over 200 km staying 7 or more days, but the scatter is wide and one visitor who travelled 50 km stayed 8 days, which is a clear anomaly." The 2023 report says the highest marks went precisely to candidates who judged the strength and identified anomalies with data; "it is a positive correlation" on its own is a Level 1 answer. The specification also expects you to sketch a trend line or line of best fit, interpolate and extrapolate from it, and to identify weaknesses in selective statistical presentation — a truncated y-axis, a cherry-picked date range, a sample of five.

Anomalies. A 2-mark question in 2020 asked how anomalies in your data could affect your enquiry, so have an answer ready: an anomaly pulls the mean away from the typical value and inflates the range, so it can make a pattern look weaker or stronger than it is; identifying it, suggesting a cause, and quoting the median instead is what protects the conclusion.

The 9-mark frame

Two different 9-markers, two different frames. Both are level-marked, both carry +3 SPaG, and both are lost by not deciding anything.

Section A — the decision question. Tick the box first if there is one.

  1. One-sentence introduction. Define the key term — usually sustainability: meeting the needs of the present without compromising future generations' ability to meet their own. State your overall position in the same breath.
  2. Social. Benefits with figures from the booklet, developed; then the short-term local cost; then which outweighs which and why.
  3. Environmental. The same shape. The strongest move here is scale: local habitat loss now against regional climate adaptation for decades.
  4. Economic. The same shape again. Reach for the stakeholder who is on both sides — in 2026 that is the NFU.
  5. Two-sentence conclusion. Reiterate the judgement, refer to long-term impacts, and say whether the scheme actually solves the problem Figure 1 set out.

Section B — the fieldwork question. Name the enquiry on the title line, then PEEL × 3 plus a conclusion.

  1. Frame the judgement. "To evaluate whether my conclusions are valid I need to judge how accurate my data collection was, and how reliable my results were — whether I would get the same results on a different day."
  2. Paragraph 1 — the quantitative method. Beach slope profiles: what made them reliable (complete profile shoreline to sea wall, 2 m intervals, three adjacent groynes), then the limitation (3 groynes of 3 km, concrete only, one day), then what it does to the validity of the conclusion.
  3. Paragraph 2 — the qualitative or subjective method. Bi-polar analysis: what it allowed (comparing groynes with the sea wall on four variables), then the limitation (subjective, wide variation between groups, judgements like lifespan need secondary data), then the effect on reliability.
  4. Paragraph 3 — how the methods interact. Do they support each other or leave a gap? At Stratford the questionnaires agreed with the EQS, which strengthens validity; at Brighton the bi-polar survey adds a dimension the profiles cannot measure but cannot be relied on alone.
  5. Conclusion that decides. "Although the data is a snapshot in time, which limits reliability, I am confident it provides evidence that the groynes at Brighton are a successful method of coastal management — they stop longshore drift and build a protective beach."

Sentence starters that carry the marks: "One major strength/limitation of the beach profiles was…" · "Another useful method was the bi-polar survey, which…" · "When combined, these methods allowed me to…" · "This suggests we can have relative confidence in the validity of our conclusions, that…" · "However, if we had repeated this on a different day with different weather…" · "Overall, the data was partially/mostly effective because…" · "In conclusion, my fieldwork has enabled me to reach some valid conclusions, although…"

03Understanding it

U1

If you get the pre-release booklet twelve weeks early and a clean copy in the exam, what is there left to prepare?

Show answer

Not the facts — they are in front of you. What you cannot do in the exam is think, because 37 marks in roughly 35 minutes leaves no time for it. So the twelve weeks are for the three jobs that have to be done in advance.

Annotate. Know where every statistic lives, so that when a question needs a figure you turn to the right page instead of hunting. Categorise. Sort the proposal's impacts into social, economic and environmental, positive and negative — the A3 evaluation grid. Rank. Number the points within each box from most to least significant, which means you walk in already holding a judgement.

The exam point: a Level 3 answer makes links across the booklet. Links are the thing you cannot improvise under time pressure, which is why the ranking step matters more than it looks.

U2

Why is copying the right sentence from the booklet only worth Level 1?

Show answer

Because the question is not testing whether you can find the evidence — it is testing AO3, which is interpreting and evaluating it to make a judgement. Selecting an appropriate quotation shows you understood the question; it does not show you understood the geography.

The ladder is explicit in the mark schemes. Level 1: points identified, largely copied, mainly from one figure. Level 2: the same points developed — "this means that…", "as a result…". Level 3: points from across the booklet linked, with evaluative observations throughout rather than only in the conclusion.

The exam point: the cheapest upgrade available is to follow every quoted figure with a sentence beginning "this means that". It converts Level 1 material into Level 2 material without needing any extra knowledge.

U3

Some Section A questions are about the specific place and some are general. Why does getting this wrong cost marks even when the answer is good?

Show answer

Because the mark scheme for a general question rewards breadth of understanding across the specification, and a place-specific answer cannot supply it. The examiners call this self-limiting: you have not written anything wrong, you have simply closed off most of the available marks.

The 2023 report gives both directions. Questions 2.2, 2.4 and 3 were tied to the Cayman Islands. Questions 1.4 ("To what extent can tourism create opportunities for economic development?") and 2.6 were broad discussions of tourism and development, where answers drawing on wider learning — economic leakage, seasonality, the multiplier effect, ecotourism — scored highest. In 2022 the mirror-image error: candidates answered the general question on waste as a resource entirely through the Cambridge incinerator.

The exam point: check for a place name in the question stem. No place name means the question wants the topic, so bring the rest of the course in.

U4

Why did the school choose questions for the two enquiries rather than hypotheses?

Show answer

Because a hypothesis forces a verdict and a question permits a "partly". A hypothesis — "the beach gets wider from west to east" — is either supported or not, and real fieldwork data is usually mixed. A question — "to what extent has the Olympic regeneration of Stratford been successful?" — lets the honest answer be "largely, but unevenly", which is exactly what the data showed.

That matters for the 9-marker, because the wordings include "to what extent did your results and conclusions answer the enquiry question or hypothesis" (2024). A nuanced conclusion is easier to defend than a binary one, and nuance is what the Level 3 descriptors reward.

The exam point: write the title exactly as it was — "Evaluate the success of groynes as a method of coastal management on Brighton beach", "To what extent has the Olympic Regeneration of Stratford been successful?" — because the examiners say the answers must visibly reflect the aim in the stated title.

U5

What is the actual difference between accurate, reliable and valid, and why does the 9-marker need all three?

Show answer

They are three links in a chain, and the 9-marker is about the chain rather than the links.

Accuracy is about the measurement: how close your reading was to the true value. A one-metre ruler used on a drop of more than a metre, down a groyne edge that was not straight, is an accuracy problem. Reliability is about repeatability: would you get this again? Three groynes on one October day, when destructive winter waves steepen beaches, is a reliability problem. Validity is about the conclusion: given the accuracy and the reliability, is the conclusion actually supported?

The chain runs one way. Inaccurate measurement produces results you cannot trust; unreliable sampling produces results that may not generalise; and the two together determine whether the conclusion is valid. The 2023 report found that most candidates described their results or their limitations well but never linked either to the validity of the conclusion, and so stalled at Level 2.

The exam point: write the chain explicitly. "We sampled at 2 m intervals, which was accurate enough to show the sediment difference. However we only sampled three of the groynes along 3 km, on one day, so the result is not reliable across the whole coast or across seasons. Therefore my conclusion is valid for these three groynes but cannot be extended to the whole beach."

U6

Both of John's enquiries used a subjective scoring survey. Is that a flaw in the fieldwork, or something to use?

Show answer

Both. It is the single most useful thing in either enquiry, because it hands you a genuine evaluation point in every question type.

The strength is that a bi-polar or environmental quality survey converts a qualitative impression into a number that can be plotted, compared across sites and totalled. That is why the Brighton bi-polar survey could compare groynes with the sea wall on four variables, and why the Stratford EQS could rank five sites — neither comparison is possible with words alone.

The limitation is that the number is still a judgement. Classmates scored the same Stratford sites differently; Brighton groups' bi-polar scores showed a great range of variation. Some of the Brighton categories — lifespan, vulnerability to erosion — cannot honestly be judged by eye at all without secondary data.

And there are two fixes, which is what turns the limitation into a mark: take a group average of totals, which removes outliers (the Stratford answer), or run a standardisation survey at one agreed point and adjust everyone's scores to the average before starting. The 2024 mark scheme adds more: describe what each score means, widen the range of criteria, avoid an odd-numbered scale so the middle value is not a default.

The exam point: "it was subjective" is one mark. "It was subjective, which is why we took a group average of the totals to remove outliers, improving accuracy and reliability" is the Level 2 or 3 version.

U7

In the unseen fieldwork section you know nothing about the place. So what is actually being tested?

Show answer

AO3 and AO4 only — interpretation, analysis, evaluation, and the selection and use of skills. Nothing in Question 4 rewards place knowledge, which is why it is the most learnable part of the paper: the skills recur, and so do the criticisms.

Three things make the difference. Read the stem, because it names the aim or hypothesis and every judgement has to be made against it — not against the data in general. Attempt every completion question, because the examiner's reports note year after year that significant proportions of candidates leave pie charts, isolines and scattergraphs blank "across the ability range", and these are 1-mark gifts. Criticise the method systematically, using a fixed checklist: sample size, number of sites, one day or one time, subjectivity, narrow scale, limited criteria, equipment.

The exam point: the same criticisms that evaluate John's own fieldwork evaluate a stranger's. The vocabulary transfers completely, which is why learning Brighton and Stratford properly also prepares Question 4.

U8

Why would the interquartile range sometimes be a better answer than the mean, and when would none of the measures of central tendency help?

Show answer

The mean uses every value, which makes it sensitive to anomalies: one reading of 10 among a set of 2s and 3s drags it upwards and misdescribes the typical case. The median is immune to that, because it only cares about position. The range has the opposite problem to the mean — it is set entirely by the two most extreme readings, so a single anomaly defines it. The interquartile range describes the spread of the middle 50% and therefore ignores both extremes, which is why the 2019 mark scheme offers it as a measure that "might be more useful".

But the deeper answer, and the one the 2019 question was really after, is that sometimes an average is the wrong tool altogether. The housing quality enquiry surveyed twelve different areas of a town in order to find differences between them. Collapsing twelve areas into one number of 5.41 destroys exactly the information the enquiry existed to collect. The mark scheme says it outright: measures of central tendency "have limited use in the context of the data since it is really about identifying differences and patterns".

The exam point: when a question asks you to assess the usefulness of a statistical measure, the strongest point is not about the arithmetic. It is about whether the measure answers the enquiry's question.

04Exam drill

What each part of Paper 3 actually wants

Paper 3 is 76 marks in 75 minutes, so about a mark a minute with nothing to spare. Section A is 37 marks and Section B is 39, which makes roughly 36 minutes each plus three minutes of reading. The command words do different jobs:

  • 1 mark — read off, calculate or complete. AO4. The examiners expect "a high degree of accuracy" and repeatedly note that candidates skip graph completions for no reason. Bring a ruler and a calculator; both are allowed and both are assumed.
  • 2 marks — check whether it wants one thing or two. "Give two ways" is two separate points, no development. "Suggest one reason" is point plus development, and the 2023 report says most candidates stop after the point and score one.
  • 3 marks — justify, usually. Name the method or technique, say what it measured, and say why that answers the enquiry question. Over half of candidates in 2023 scored one mark here by only naming the method.
  • 4 marks — a judgement with data, marked in two levels. Either "to what extent does the data support the hypothesis" or "describe the physical geography from the OS map". Level 2 needs precision: the strength of the relationship and the anomaly, or the slope, the height and the direction of flow. A correct calculation with the wrong conclusion is capped at Level 1.
  • 6 marks — two or three developed paragraphs, marked in three levels. No introduction needed, but keep returning to the question. If the command is discuss, include the counter-argument; if it is assess, judge the size of the effect. PEEL × 2 is the school's structure: Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link.
  • 9 marks (+3 SPaG) — the two big ones, both level-marked, both AO3 6 / AO4 3. Section A: a decision, structured social / economic / environmental, with a conclusion that decides. Section B: your own enquiry, PEEL × 3 plus a conclusion, linking accuracy to reliability to validity. Mark points below are a coverage target, not a tick list — AQA marks these in levels and rewards development and linkage, not point count.

D1

A student interviewed 50 people about their local bus service. Of the 50, 29 rated cleanliness as "reasonable". What percentage of the people interviewed considered cleanliness to be reasonable? (1)

Show answer

58%. 29 ÷ 50 = 0.58, × 100 = 58% 1

The shortcut worth knowing: out of 50, double the count. Out of 25, multiply by four. Out of 20, multiply by five. These are the sample sizes AQA actually uses, and the arithmetic is quicker than the calculator. (AQA 2024, Q4.1.)

D6

Identify one potential risk in your physical geography fieldwork and explain how the risk was reduced. (3)

Show answer

Enquiry: "Evaluate the success of groynes as a method of coastal management on Brighton beach."

Risk. The waves approaching the coastline at Brighton were powerful destructive waves, so there was a risk of being caught by a breaking wave while measuring near the water 1

We carried out all measurements at a safe distance from the breaking waves, starting the slope profile from just above the point where they broke rather than at the water's edge 1

Our teachers had also checked the tide times in advance, so that we worked on a falling tide with enough safe beach exposed for the whole profile 1

Equally acceptable, and worth the same three marks: the steep pebble beach risked slipping, so we wore appropriate footwear, and worked sensibly in small groups when taking measurements at the groynes. (AQA 2019, Q5.2.)

D7

Justify one primary data collection method used in your physical geography enquiry. (3)

Show answer

Enquiry: "Evaluate the success of groynes as a method of coastal management on Brighton beach."

Method: beach slope profiles. We measured the build-up and depletion of sediment on the updrift (west) and downdrift (east) side of each groyne, because if the groynes were stopping longshore drift there would be more sediment on the updrift side 1

We measured from just above where the waves broke, for safety, inland towards the sea wall, so that we captured the complete profile and not just part of it 1

We sampled at 2 m systematic intervals, which gave a detailed enough picture of how much sediment had built up on each side while still being quick enough to repeat on three adjacent groynes 1

✗ "I used beach slope profiles." That is identifying, not justifying, and the 2023 report says over 50% of candidates scored one mark for exactly this. The command word is justify: every sentence must contain a "because" or a "so that" linking the method to the aim in your title.

D8

Students tested the hypothesis "the velocity of a river increases downstream" by timing a ball over 10 m, five times at each of three sites 1 km apart. Times in seconds — Site A (upstream): 26, 24, 22, 24, 25. Site B: 22, 23, 18, 20, 24. Site C (downstream): 20, 18, 20, 18, 19. To what extent does this data support the hypothesis? Use the data to support your answer. (4)

Show answer

The data broadly supports the hypothesis. Mean times fall downstream — Site A 24.2 s, Site B 21.4 s, Site C 19.0 s — so velocity rises from 10 ÷ 24.2 = 0.41 m/s to 10 ÷ 21.4 = 0.47 m/s to 10 ÷ 19.0 = 0.53 m/s 1

That is an increase of about 29% from upstream to downstream, and it is consistent with the Bradshaw model, in which velocity increases downstream as the channel becomes smoother and more efficient 1

However the support is only partial, because the ranges overlap. Site A spans 22–26 s and Site B spans 18–24 s, so the fastest reading at Site A (22 s) is faster than two of the five readings at Site B 1

Site B also contains the single fastest reading of the whole investigation at 18 s, equal to the fastest at Site C, which is an anomaly — so while the means show a clear downstream trend, the individual readings do not cleanly separate the sites, and three sites with five readings each is a small basis for the conclusion 1

✗ "Yes, because the times get smaller." Level 1 — a basic reference to the data with no measure of strength.
✓ Level 2 needs "appears to support but": calculate something, state the trend with figures, then test it by looking at the spread and naming the anomaly. And note the trap in the arithmetic — a shorter time is a faster velocity. A manipulated calculation with the wrong conclusion is capped at Level 1. (AQA 2024, Q4.11.)

D9

Using Figure 1, "Water supply and demand in the United Kingdom", explain why it is increasingly difficult to manage water resources in the UK. (6)

Show answer

Two or three developed paragraphs; marked in three levels, so develop rather than list.

Paragraph 1 — the mismatch between where the water is and where the people are. Figure 1 shows the north and west receive over 2000 mm of rainfall a year while the south-east, the most heavily populated part of the UK, receives under 800 mm 1; the gap is wider still in effective rainfall, which runs from more than 2500 mm in parts of the north-west to less than 200 mm in parts of eastern England, so the driest regions have least water actually available for supply and depend on costly transfers or on groundwater that is already being over-abstracted 1

Paragraph 2 — demand is rising, unevenly, and in the wrong place. Average household use is up 70% since 1985 and total demand is forecast to rise by about 4 billion litres a day by 2050 1; crucially the increase is not spread evenly — the South East alone accounts for 50% of the projected growth to 2050, against 6% for the North — so demand is growing fastest exactly where the resource is scarcest, which means national averages conceal the real problem and a region cannot simply be left to balance its own books 1

Paragraph 3 — supply is falling and becoming less predictable. Total supply is predicted to fall 7% by 2045 because of climate change, groundwater use has to be reduced for environmental reasons, and between 2020 and 2050 a year with drought restrictions is twice as likely as in the past 1; on top of that, demand peaks in summer when rainfall is lowest — up over 20% in a normal summer and over 70% in the hot, dry June of 2020 — so managers are being asked to meet a larger, spikier demand from a smaller and less reliable supply, which is why the Environment Agency warns of shortages within about 20 years 1

✗ Listing six statistics from Figure 1. The command is explain, so every figure needs a "which means that…" after it.
✓ Three linked reasons, each with a figure and a consequence, and at least one explicit link between paragraphs — the point that demand is growing fastest in the driest region is worth more than either fact alone.

D10

Assess the effectiveness of your data presentation technique(s) in your physical geography enquiry. (6)

Show answer

Enquiry: "Evaluate the success of groynes as a method of coastal management on Brighton beach." PEEL × 2 — one paragraph per technique, each with a limitation. Level-marked on whether you assess rather than describe.

Technique 1 — slope profiles drawn as line graphs. I drew two profiles, one for the updrift (west) and one for the downdrift (east) side of the groyne. A line graph is the right choice because sediment height is continuous data, and it was the only way to show how the height and angle of the beach changed from the shoreline to the sea wall 1

It was effective because the difference was immediately visible: the west side carried much more sediment, trapped by the interruption of longshore drift, while the east side showed a clear deficit — colouring the groyne grey and the beach yellow made the two profiles directly comparable side by side 1

Limitation. Line graphs become crowded with several lines, so plotting all three groynes on one pair of axes would have been unreadable; with one line per graph the comparison worked, but it meant six separate graphs and no single image of the whole 3 km stretch 1

Technique 2 — a bar chart of the bi-polar scores. I plotted the bi-polar totals for the sea wall and the groynes across the four variables — vulnerability to erosion, aesthetic value, lifespan, disturbance to natural processes and habitats. A bar chart suits discrete, categorical data, and it turned subjective judgements into quantities that could be compared 1

It was effective because the negative half of the y-axis, running to −3, showed where each strategy fails as well as where it succeeds — which a simple positive scale would have hidden, and which was the whole point of comparing two management methods 1

Limitation and improvement. The scores were our own opinions, so the bars would differ between groups — the presentation looks objective but rests on subjective data. Plotting the results as proportional symbols on a digital map using GIS would have been better, because it would have added the spatial dimension the bar chart lacks, letting us compare different parts of the beach rather than two strategies in the abstract 1

✗ Writing about slope profiles, tape measures and bi-polar surveys as methods. The 2023 report is blunt: a significant proportion of candidates discussed data collection instead of data presentation and scored nothing at all.
✓ Structure each paragraph on the three criteria the examiners credit — ease of construction, visual clarity, usefulness for analysing the data — and finish each with a limitation and an improvement.

D11

Study Figure 3, "The proposed Lincolnshire reservoir". Assess the sustainability of the proposed Lincolnshire reservoir project. Use evidence from the resources booklet and your own understanding. (9) [+3 SPaG]

Show answer

A full model answer, written out rather than planned, because this is the question worth the most marks on the paper. AO3 6, AO4 3, plus 3 SPaG. The structure is the school's: intro, social, environmental, economic, conclusion.

Introduction. To a large extent the proposed Lincolnshire reservoir is socially and environmentally sustainable. Sustainability means meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own, and on that test the scheme is likely to cause some short-term social and economic disruption for local people, but local people will benefit economically in the longer term, and the social benefits for the wider region outweigh the social negatives for the local area 1

Socially sustainable — the benefits. The reservoir will supply up to 166 million litres of water a day to 500 000 homes. This is essential to meet the UK's rising demand for water, and especially the demand from the 175 000 new homes planned in Lincolnshire over the next five years. Domestic water supply is fundamental to quality of life, and as climate change further threatens the UK's water security — supply is forecast to fall 7% by 2045, and drought restrictions are twice as likely between 2020 and 2050 — new storage on this scale will be essential to meeting demand 1

Other strategies matter, but they cannot do the job alone. Reducing consumption and improving efficiency are important — household use has risen 70% since 1985 and over 3 billion litres a day are lost to leakage — yet even acting on all of them leaves a gap, because demand is forecast to rise by around 4 billion litres a day by 2050. Only increasing supply closes it. The reservoir also adds social value directly: it will be open for public use, with sailing, wind surfing, cycling and walking, and a network of footpaths and cycle tracks so people can reach the recreation and the wildlife 1

Socially sustainable — the costs, and the judgement. Against this, over 2000 local people have signed a petition against the development, more than 50 families will lose their homes, and during construction some roads will close while others become congested with construction traffic on narrow country lanes. One farmer reports that the uncertainty is already harming mental health. These are real and serious, but they are short-term and highly localised, whereas the water supply benefit is permanent and regional. Fifty families set against 500 000 homes is not a comparison of like with like, so on balance the scheme is socially sustainable 1

Environmentally sustainable. Environmentally the reservoir will flood a large area of mostly greenfield land, including farmland and trees, harming biodiversity and removing habitat. However, once construction is complete local habitats will be restored and the diversity of wildlife in the area increased, which limits the long-term damage, and the shape of the reservoir has been designed specifically to encourage wildlife 1

At the larger scale the reservoir is an important adaptation to climate change, and this is where the environmental case is strongest. Lincolnshire is already one of the driest parts of the UK and its rainfall reliability is falling, which threatens water supply for ecosystems as well as for people. Taking winter water from the Trent and the Witham when flows are highest, and releasing it in summer when they are lowest, works with the natural water balance rather than against it — and the overflow drain reduces winter flood risk as a secondary benefit. Anglian Water has also addressed visual impact, making the embankments gently sloping so they are less intrusive; that may be of little comfort to those losing their homes, but it is evidence that environmental concerns shaped the design rather than being added afterwards 1

Environmentally — the counter-argument. Residents argue that a project of this scale could be reduced or avoided altogether if authorities focused on leakage, pollution and greywater systems. That argument has force: leakage alone is about 20% of supply, and 325 serious pollution incidents in 2021 — a 24% rise on the previous year — represent sources that cannot be used. But these measures reduce waste rather than adding capacity, and against a 4 billion litre a day increase in demand they are necessary without being sufficient 1

Economically sustainable. Economically the scheme harms local residents, especially in the short term. Flooding the land removes farmers' homes and livelihoods and closes other businesses such as horse-riding stables; a local business owner expects several leisure businesses to close, and construction will cause congestion and delays. However, the National Farmers Union — representing the group that loses most — accepts that expanding water supply is vital to the long-term resilience of food production and that farm businesses will benefit from the additional water, which suggests real long-term economic gains even for farmers. At the regional scale the scheme brings £2.6 billion of direct and indirect investment, and once complete is expected to attract thousands of visitors a year to the sailing, watersports and cycling facilities, spending money with local cafés and providers. So the economic costs are concentrated and immediate while the economic benefits are dispersed and lasting 1

Conclusion. The proposed Lincolnshire reservoir does have real limitations, particularly for the local residents and farmers who bear the cost in the short term, and the NFU is right that local people must be involved in the decision-making at every stage. But judged over the long term — which is what sustainability means — the social, economic and environmental benefits are substantially larger than the costs, and the scheme addresses a water deficit that the demand-reduction measures alone cannot close. It is therefore a largely sustainable option for managing water supply and demand in the region 1

✗ A list of advantages, then a list of disadvantages, then "so it depends". That is Level 1 or low Level 2: the mark scheme's Level 1 descriptor is "points largely copied, with limited development… a largely unsubstantiated judgement".
✓ Three things move this to Level 3. Evaluate as you go rather than only at the end. Link across the booklet — the 500 000 homes figure from Figure 3 set against the 4 billion litre demand increase in Figure 1 is exactly the synoptic move the examiners reward. And use scale and time as your weighing device: local versus regional, short term versus long term. SPaG carries 3 marks here, so use paragraphs and geographical terminology — abstraction, adaptation, greenfield, stakeholder, biodiversity.

D12

For one of your fieldwork enquiries, to what extent did the data collected allow you to reach valid conclusions? (9) [+3 SPaG]

Show answer

Title: "Evaluate the success of groynes as a method of coastal management on Brighton beach." Written as a plan with the sentences that carry each point — AQA marks this in levels, so the eleven points below are a coverage target rather than a tick list. PEEL × 3 plus a conclusion.

Frame the judgement first. To evaluate whether my conclusions are valid I need to judge critically how accurate my data collection was, since that determines the results I got, and then look at the bigger picture and consider how reliable those results were — whether I would get the same measurements again on a different day 1

Paragraph 1 — the slope profiles were reliable, for three specific reasons. We sampled from the shoreline all the way to the sea wall, so we captured a complete profile on both the updrift and downdrift side rather than a fragment. We used 2 m intervals, which gave sufficient detail of how much sediment had accumulated. And we collected data at three adjacent groynes rather than one, which increased reliability by showing the pattern repeated — so the data supports the validity of the conclusion that the groynes are effective at trapping sediment and building a protective beach, because the same updrift surplus and downdrift deficit appeared at each groyne 1

Paragraph 1, limitation. However, three groynes out of a roughly 3 km stretch is a small sample, and all three were concrete. Repeated measurements along the whole stretch, and a comparison with wooden groynes, would have given a more complete picture and increased the validity of the conclusion that longshore drift is being stopped along the coastline rather than at these three structures 1

Paragraph 1, the seasonal point — the strongest limitation available. We collected data on one day in October, when winds are usually stronger and waves more destructive. Geographical theory says destructive waves in winter produce steeper beaches and constructive waves in summer gentler profiles, so the profiles either side of the groyne may partly reflect the season rather than the groyne. Fieldwork in two contrasting seasons would have made the data more reliable and the conclusion more secure 1

Paragraph 2 — the bi-polar analysis, what it allowed. To judge how effective the management was, we compared the groynes with the sea wall using a bi-polar survey scored from −3 to +3 on four variables: vulnerability to erosion, aesthetic value, lifespan, and disturbance to natural processes and habitats. This let us discuss the pros and cons of each strategy and still plot the result, because it converts a qualitative judgement into a number 1

Paragraph 2, limitation. Its major limitation is the subjective nature of the answers. The scores given by pupils in other groups showed a great range of variation, so the reliability of this technique is questionable; a standardisation survey at one agreed point, with everyone adjusting to the group average, would have reduced that bias. It is also difficult to judge categories such as lifespan and vulnerability to erosion by eye at all — those need detailed secondary data such as maintenance records and erosion rates, which we did not have, so the accuracy of those particular judgements was low 1

Paragraph 3 — how the two methods interact. The two methods measure different things, and that is both a strength and a gap. The profiles give objective, quantitative evidence on the question the title actually asks — whether the groynes trap sediment — and the bi-polar survey adds the dimension the profiles cannot reach, which is whether trapping sediment is a good way to manage this coast given its cost, appearance and effect on habitats 1

But they do not corroborate each other, because they are not measuring the same variable. My conclusion therefore rests almost entirely on the profile data, with the bi-polar survey adding context rather than confirmation — which is weaker than at Stratford, where the questionnaire responses and the environmental quality scores pointed the same way and so reinforced one another 1

Conclusion that decides. My fieldwork has enabled me to reach some valid conclusions. The data is a snapshot in time, which limits its reliability, and the subjective element limits the accuracy of part of it — so the conclusion has internal validity for these three groynes but cannot be extended to all beaches. Within those limits, I am confident the data provides evidence that the groynes at Brighton beach are a successful method of coastal management: they stop longshore drift and help build up a protective beach 1

✗ Describing the results, or listing the limitations of the data collection, and stopping there. The 2023 examiner's report says this is what the majority of candidates did — thoughtful observations that never addressed accuracy of results and so never reached validity of conclusions.
✓ The question is about a chain, so write the chain: accuracy → reliability → validity, with "therefore" between the links. And name the enquiry on the title line, because the examiners insist the answer must visibly reflect the aim in the title you wrote.
