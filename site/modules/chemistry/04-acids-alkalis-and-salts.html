02How it actually works

An acid in water releases H⁺ ions; an alkali releases OH⁻. Everything else — indicators, neutralisation, the three salt preparations — is bookkeeping about where those ions and their partners end up.

Acids, alkalis and the pH scale

Spec 2.28 – 2.33 · 2021 Q1 · 2022 Q1

Acids (HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃) are a source of hydrogen ions, H⁺, in solution. Alkalis (NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂) are a source of hydroxide ions, OH⁻. The pH scale runs 0–14: 0–3 strongly acidic, 4–6 weakly acidic, 7 neutral, 8–10 weakly alkaline, 11–14 strongly alkaline.

The three indicators the spec names, with their exam colours:

  • Litmus: red in acid, blue in alkali (purple neutral).
  • Phenolphthalein: colourless in acid, pink in alkali.
  • Methyl orange: red in acid, yellow in alkali.

Universal indicator gives the approximate pH by colour sweep (red → green at 7 → purple). To show a solution is acidic: blue litmus turns red — say the change, not just the final colour.

In the proton language the spec asks for: an acid is a proton donor, a base a proton acceptor — neutralisation is H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, a proton handed over.

Bases, alkalis, and the reactions that make salts

Spec 2.37, 2.38 · 2022 Q1d · 2024 Q10a

A base is any metal oxide, metal hydroxide or ammonia; an alkali is a base that dissolves in water. The salt-forming reactions, each with its giveaway observation:

  • acid + metal → salt + hydrogen — effervescence, metal disappears. (Nitric acid + metals is excluded from the spec — use Mg or Zn with HCl or H₂SO₄ in answers you invent.)
  • acid + base (oxide/hydroxide) → salt + water — the classic neutralisation.
  • acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide — effervescence, gas turns limewater milky.

Which salt? The acid decides the surname: hydrochloric → chlorides, sulfuric → sulfates, nitric → nitrates. HCl + KOH → potassium chloride + water.

Observation questions: say what your eyes see — effervescence/bubbles, the solid disappears, a colour change. "Hydrogen is given off" is a deduction, not an observation, and the schemes ignore it. 2021 also rejected extra wrong observations bolted on ("white flame") — do not decorate.

The solubility rules — the map that picks the method

Spec 2.34 · 2022 Q7a · 2024 Q2b

Learn these six lines; every salt question starts by consulting them:

  • All sodium, potassium and ammonium salts — soluble. ("Sodium salts are always soluble" — the 2025 marker, twice.)
  • All nitrates — soluble.
  • Chlorides — soluble, except silver and lead(II).
  • Sulfates — soluble, except barium, calcium and lead(II).
  • Carbonates — insoluble, except sodium, potassium, ammonium.
  • Hydroxides — insoluble, except sodium, potassium (calcium slightly).

The "which pair precipitates?" MCQ is just cross-checking: potassium carbonate + calcium nitrate → potassium nitrate (soluble) and calcium carbonate (insoluble — precipitate!). Sodium chloride + ammonium nitrate → both products soluble → nothing happens.

Method 1 — soluble salt from an acid + excess insoluble solid

Spec 2.39 · 2024 Q10b · 2021 Q5a

For salts of Mg, Zn, Cu… : react the acid with an excess of the insoluble metal, oxide or carbonate.

  1. Add the solid to the acid until it is in excess — so that all the acid is used up (that is the reason the schemes want).
  2. Filter off the excess solid.
  3. Heat the filtrate until the crystallisation point (crystals just start to form / a saturated solution) — never to dryness.
  4. Leave to cool and crystallise.
  5. Pour off / filter the remaining liquid, and dry the crystals with a named method: pat with filter paper, leave in a warm place or a (warm) oven.

The two killers, straight from the schemes: heating to dryness collapses the answer to 1 mark (it destroys hydrated crystals — the water of crystallisation is boiled away, and for some salts the salt decomposes); and "leave the crystals to dry" without a method was refused the final mark in 2024 — name how.

Method 2 — soluble salt from an acid + alkali (titration route)

Spec 2.40C · 2025 Q2a

When both reactants are soluble (sodium, potassium, ammonium salts), there is no excess solid to filter: use an indicator to find the exact neutralisation volume, then repeat with the same volumes without the indicator (or remove it with charcoal), and crystallise as before: heat to the crystallisation point, cool, decant, dry.

2025 asked only for the crystallisation half — and the marks were exactly: heat to crystallisation point · cool to crystallise · pour off excess liquid · dry in a warm place. Adding any new chemical at this stage scored zero: the solution already contains only the salt and water.

Method 3 — insoluble salt by precipitation (mix, filter, wash, dry)

Spec 2.41C · 2022 Q7b · 2023 Q10 · 2025 Q7a

For BaSO₄, PbBr₂, AgCl, Cu(OH)₂…: mix two solutions that each carry half of the target — lead(II) nitrate + sodium bromide → PbBr₂↓ + sodium nitrate (both parents chosen soluble; nitrates and sodium salts always are).

  1. Mix the two solutions — the schemes reject mixing solids.
  2. Filter — the salt you want is the residue on the paper.
  3. Wash the residue with distilled water — rinses away the soluble by-product clinging to it.
  4. Dry — filter paper, warm oven, leave to dry. No direct heating with a Bunsen (2022 rejected it).

Wrong-method answers are the biggest single loss on salt questions. Describing the excess-base method for a precipitation scored 1 of 4 in 2025 and 1 of 3 in 2023. And it is the residue that is washed and dried — 2023's marker: "the salt you want is the residue", not the filtrate. If the product is insoluble, there is no crystallisation step anywhere.

Choosing the method — the ten-second decision

every salt question

  1. Is the target salt insoluble? (check the rules) → Method 3: mix solutions, filter, wash residue, dry.
  2. Soluble, and the partner solid is insoluble (metal/oxide/carbonate)? → Method 1: excess solid, filter, crystallise.
  3. Soluble, and both reactants soluble (Na/K/NH₄ salt from acid + alkali)? → Method 2: titrate, then crystallise.

2021's find-the-mistake question was this decision in disguise: nitric acid can't make a sulfate; magnesium carbonate is insoluble so there is no "solution of magnesium carbonate"; and boiling to dryness wrecks the hydrated crystals.

03Cheat sheet

The one decision everything follows from

Look up the solubility first. Insoluble target → precipitate, filter, wash the residue, dry. Soluble target → make a solution of just salt + water, then heat only to the crystallisation point — never to dryness.

04Understanding it

U1

Why does the soluble-salt method demand an excess of the solid, when excess sounds wasteful — and how does the excess then leave the story?

Show answer

The excess guarantees that all the acid has reacted. If any acid survived, it would stay dissolved in the filtrate and end up concentrated in your crystals — the product would be contaminated with acid you cannot see.

An excess of an insoluble solid is trivially removed: filter it off. That is the whole design of the method — the impurity you deliberately created is the one impurity filtering can remove. (2024's one-marker: "so that all the nitric acid reacts". "To make more salt" misses the logic entirely.)

U2

Heating the filtrate "until crystals just start to form" and then stopping feels unfinished. Why is stopping there exactly right, and what goes wrong for a hydrated salt if you keep heating to dryness?

Show answer

Stopping at the crystallisation point leaves a hot saturated solution. As it cools, solubility falls and the salt comes out of solution slowly as well-formed crystals, leaving the remaining impurities behind in the liquid you then pour off.

Boiling to dryness does the opposite: everything in the solution — impurities included — is baked onto the dish, and for a hydrated salt the heat also drives off the water of crystallisation, so you get a powdery anhydrous solid (or a decomposed one) instead of the hydrated crystals the question asked for. That is why every scheme caps a heated-to-dryness answer at 1 mark.

U3

In the precipitation method, what exactly does washing the residue with distilled water remove — and why does washing the filtrate instead earn the 2023 marker's special scorn?

Show answer

The precipitate on the filter paper is soaked in the reaction mixture, which contains the soluble by-product (sodium nitrate, in the PbBr₂ preparation) and any unreacted solutions. A rinse of distilled water dissolves those away; the insoluble product itself cannot dissolve, so it stays put — the wash purifies for free.

The filtrate is the liquid that passed through: by-product solution. Washing it is washing the waste. The confusion comes from Method 1, where the filtrate is the valuable part — which is exactly why the papers keep asking both: they are testing whether you know which side of the filter paper your product is on. Insoluble product → residue; soluble product → filtrate.

U4

Silver chloride is insoluble. A student proposes making it by adding excess silver to hydrochloric acid, then filtering. Give two separate reasons the plan fails, and the correct recipe.

Show answer

Reason 1 — wrong method for the product: even if the reaction worked, an insoluble product forms wherever it is made — mixed in with the excess solid — and filtering would collect both together. The excess-solid method only works when the product stays dissolved.

Reason 2 — the reaction doesn't go: silver is below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it does not react with dilute acids at all.

Correct recipe: precipitation from two soluble parents — silver nitrate solution + sodium chloride solution (nitrates always soluble, sodium salts always soluble) → AgCl precipitates → filter, wash the residue, dry.

U5

Copper sulfate solution is mixed with sodium hydroxide solution. State what you would SEE, name both products, and explain which preparation method this reaction belongs to.

Show answer

Seen: a blue precipitate/solid forms (and the blue solution fades). Not "crystals", not "fizzing" — nothing here makes a gas, and 2023 rejected fizzing outright.

Products: copper(II) hydroxide (the blue precipitate — hydroxides are insoluble except Na/K) and sodium sulfate (stays dissolved — sodium salts always soluble).

Method: precipitation (Method 3): filter, wash the residue, dry gently. The question told you a precipitate formed; answers describing excess-base-and-crystallise were the standard way to lose all three method marks.

U6

Aluminium sulfate is used by gardeners to acidify soil: Al₂(SO₄)₃(s) + 6H₂O(l) → 2Al(OH)₃(s) + 3H₂SO₄(aq). Using only the state symbols, explain why the products make the soil acidic and not neutral.

Show answer

Both an acid and a base are produced — but their states differ. Al(OH)₃ is (s): solid, insoluble, so its OH⁻ ions are locked away and never enter the soil water. H₂SO₄ is (aq): dissolved, so its H⁺ ions are released into solution.

Free H⁺ with no free OH⁻ to cancel it → the soil water is acidic. The general lesson (it earned both marks in 2021): ions only count when they are in solution — (s) means chemically present but unavailable.

05Exam drill

D1

A student prepares zinc nitrate crystals by adding excess zinc powder to dilute nitric acid.

(a) State why an excess of zinc is used. (1)
(b) Describe what the student should do next to obtain pure, dry crystals of zinc nitrate. (5)

Show answer

(a) So that all the nitric acid reacts / is used up 1

(b) Filter off the excess zinc 1; heat the filtrate until the crystallisation point / until crystals just start to form 1; leave to cool and crystallise 1; pour off / filter the remaining liquid 1; dry the crystals with filter paper / in a warm place or oven 1

Heating to dryness → max 1. "Leave them to dry" without a method → last mark refused.

D2

Lead(II) bromide is insoluble. A teacher makes it from lead(II) nitrate solution and sodium bromide solution.

(a) Describe how to obtain a pure, dry sample of lead(II) bromide from the two solutions. (4)
(b) Explain why lead(II) nitrate and sodium bromide are suitable starting compounds. (2)

Show answer

(a) Mix the two solutions 1; filter 1; wash the residue/precipitate with distilled water 1; dry with filter paper / in a warm oven (no direct Bunsen heating) 1

(b) Both are soluble — all nitrates are soluble 1 and sodium salts are always soluble 1 — so each provides one of the ions in solution, and the only insoluble combination is PbBr₂

D3

Solutions are mixed in three test tubes: (1) copper(II) sulfate + calcium chloride; (2) magnesium nitrate + potassium sulfate; (3) sodium carbonate + copper(II) sulfate. In which tubes does a precipitate form? Name each precipitate. (3)

Show answer

Tube 1: yes — calcium sulfate (sulfates: calcium is an exception) 1

Tube 2: no — magnesium sulfate and potassium nitrate are both soluble 1

Tube 3: yes — copper(II) carbonate (carbonates insoluble except Na/K/NH₄) 1

Method: swap the partners, check each new pairing against the rules.

D4

A student plans to make hydrated magnesium sulfate crystals:
Step 1 — pour 100 cm³ of dilute nitric acid into a beaker.
Step 2 — add a solution of magnesium carbonate until there is no more effervescence.
Step 3 — heat until all the water has boiled off.
There is one mistake in each step. Identify each mistake. (3)

Show answer

Step 1: wrong acid — a sulfate needs sulfuric acid, not nitric 1

Step 2: magnesium carbonate is insoluble — there is no such thing as its solution; add the solid 1

Step 3: never boil to dryness — heat to the crystallisation point and let it cool and crystallise, or the water of crystallisation is lost 1

D5

Dilute hydrochloric acid reacts with potassium hydroxide solution.

(a) Name the type of reaction. (1)
(b) Name the two products. (2)
(c) Explain why the excess-solid method cannot be used to prepare pure potassium chloride from these reactants, and name the technique used instead. (2)

Show answer

(a) Neutralisation 1

(b) Potassium chloride 1 and water 1

(c) Both reactants are soluble, so excess alkali cannot be filtered off — it would stay dissolved and contaminate the salt 1; instead use a titration with an indicator to find the exact neutralising volumes, then repeat without the indicator and crystallise 1

D6

Sulfur dioxide dissolves in water to form an acidic solution.

(a) Identify the ion that causes the solution to be acidic. (1)
(b) State how litmus paper shows the solution is acidic. (1)
(c) Magnesium ribbon is added to the acidic solution. Give two observations. (2)

Show answer

(a) The hydrogen ion, H⁺ 1 — if you give both name and formula, both must be right

(b) Blue litmus turns red 1 — state the change

(c) Effervescence / bubbles 1; the magnesium ribbon disappears / gets smaller 1 — "hydrogen given off" is ignored (not an observation); do not add extra wrong observations
