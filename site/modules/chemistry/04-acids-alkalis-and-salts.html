02How it actually works

An acid in water releases H⁺ ions; an alkali releases OH⁻. Everything else — indicators, neutralisation, the three salt preparations — is about where those ions and their partners end up.

Acids and alkalis — H⁺ and OH⁻

Spec 2.31 · 2021 Q10c · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q6(a) · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q7(b)

An acid in water is a source of hydrogen ions, H⁺; an alkali in water is a source of hydroxide ions, OH⁻.

  • Acids: hydrochloric acid HCl, sulfuric acid H₂SO₄, nitric acid HNO₃.
  • Alkalis: sodium hydroxide NaOH, potassium hydroxide KOH, calcium hydroxide Ca(OH)₂, ammonia solution.

The water matters. Hydrogen chloride dissolved in an organic solvent forms no H⁺ ions, so it does not turn litmus red. In water it forms H⁺ ions and becomes hydrochloric acid — the 2024 Edexcel four-marker.

"Which ion makes the solution acidic?" — the hydrogen ion, H⁺. If you give the name and the formula, both must be right.

The pH scale

Spec 2.29 · 2022 Q1

pH runs from 0 to 14: below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral, above 7 is alkaline.

pHClassificationExample
0–3Strongly acidicdilute hydrochloric acid
4–6Weakly acidicvinegar, rain water
7Neutralpure water, salt solution
8–10Weakly alkalinesoap, baking soda solution
11–14Strongly alkalinesodium hydroxide solution

The lower the pH, the more H⁺ ions. The spec's bands are exact — pH 10 is weakly alkaline, pH 11 is strongly alkaline.

Indicators

Spec 2.28, 2.30 · 2021 Q1 · 2022 Q1 · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q4(b) · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q2(b)

An indicator changes colour depending on whether a solution is acidic or alkaline.

IndicatorIn acidIn alkali
Litmusredblue
Phenolphthaleincolourlesspink
Methyl orangeredyellow

Universal indicator is a mixture of indicators. It gives the approximate pH: red (pH 0–3) → orange/yellow (4–6) → green (7) → blue (8–10) → purple (11–14).

  1. Add a few drops of universal indicator to the solution (or dip in the paper).
  2. Compare the colour with the colour chart.
  3. Read off the pH.

To show a solution is acidic: blue litmus turns red — state the change, not just the final colour.

Neutralisation and proton transfer

Spec 2.32, 2.35, 2.36 · 2022 Q1d · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q4(g)

In neutralisation the acid donates H⁺ (a proton) and the base accepts it — H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

A hydrogen ion is a hydrogen atom that has lost its electron, so it is just a proton. That gives the spec's definitions:

  • An acid is a proton donor.
  • A base is a proton acceptor.

"Explain why this is an acid–base reaction" — two marks

1. The acid gives an H⁺ ion, which is a proton 1

2. The OH⁻ ion accepts the proton, forming water 1

Alkalis neutralise acids: add exactly the right amount and the solution is neutral, pH 7, containing just a salt and water.

Bases and alkalis — not the same thing

Spec 2.38

A base is any substance that neutralises an acid; an alkali is a base that dissolves in water.

  • Bases: metal oxides (CuO, MgO), metal hydroxides (Cu(OH)₂, NaOH) and ammonia.
  • Alkalis are the soluble ones: NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂ (slightly), ammonia solution.

So every alkali is a base, but not every base is an alkali. Copper(II) oxide is a base — it neutralises acid — but it is insoluble, so it is not an alkali.

Naming the salt — the acid decides the surname

Spec 2.37 · 2022 Q1d · 2021 Q5a

The metal (or ammonium) from the base gives the first name; the acid gives the second.

  1. First name from the metal: magnesium, zinc, copper(II), sodium — or ammonium from ammonia.
  2. Second name from the acid: hydrochloric → chloride; sulfuric → sulfate; nitric → nitrate.
  3. Put them together: potassium hydroxide + hydrochloric acid → potassium chloride + water.

That's why a sulfate cannot be made from nitric acid — 2021's find-the-mistake question.

The three acid reactions and what you see

Spec 2.37 · 2021 Q10c · 2024 Q10a

Acids react with metals, bases and carbonates, and every one of them makes a salt.

ReactionProductsWhat you see
acid + metalsalt + hydrogeneffervescence; the metal gets smaller and disappears
acid + base (oxide or hydroxide)salt + waterthe solid disappears; often a colour change (CuO + acid → blue solution)
acid + carbonatesalt + water + carbon dioxideeffervescence; the solid disappears; the gas turns limewater milky

The spec leaves nitric acid + metals out of what you must describe, but both the school (2024, zinc) and Edexcel (January 2023, magnesium) set it anyway. Treat it like any other acid + metal.

Observation questions: say what your eyes see — effervescence/bubbles, the solid disappears, a colour change. "Hydrogen is given off" is a deduction, not an observation, and the schemes ignore it. 2021 also rejected extra wrong observations bolted on ("white flame") — do not decorate.

The solubility rules — the map that picks the method

Spec 2.34 · 2022 Q7a · 2024 Q2b · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q3(a) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q7(a)

Six rules tell you whether any salt dissolves, and every salt question starts by checking them.

  • All sodium, potassium and ammonium salts — soluble. ("Sodium salts are always soluble" — the 2025 marker, twice.)
  • All nitrates — soluble.
  • Chlorides — soluble, except silver and lead(II).
  • Sulfates — soluble, except barium, calcium and lead(II).
  • Carbonates — insoluble, except sodium, potassium, ammonium.
  • Hydroxides — insoluble, except sodium, potassium (calcium slightly).

Worked example — does a precipitate form?

Potassium carbonate solution + calcium nitrate solution.

  1. Swap the partners: the new pairs are potassium nitrate and calcium carbonate.
  2. Check each against the rules: potassium nitrate — soluble (potassium, and a nitrate). Calcium carbonate — carbonates are insoluble, and calcium is not an exception.
  3. Conclusion: yes — calcium carbonate precipitates.

Sodium chloride + ammonium nitrate → sodium nitrate and ammonium chloride, both soluble → no precipitate.

Method 1 — soluble salt from an acid + excess insoluble solid

Spec 2.39, 2.42 · 2024 Q10b · 2021 Q5a · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q11(a)

React the acid with more insoluble solid than it can use, filter off the extra, then crystallise the salt from the solution.

Use this for salts of magnesium, zinc, copper and other metals whose oxide, carbonate or the metal itself is insoluble. The required practical: hydrated copper(II) sulfate from copper(II) oxide and dilute sulfuric acid.

  1. Warm the dilute acid in a beaker.
  2. Add the solid a little at a time, stirring, until it is in excess — some stays undissolved. This makes sure all the acid is used up.
  3. Filter off the excess solid. The filtrate is the salt solution.
  4. Heat the filtrate until the crystallisation point — crystals just start to form on a cold glass rod. Never to dryness.
  5. Leave to cool and crystallise.
  6. Pour off or filter the remaining liquid, and dry the crystals with a named method: pat with filter paper, or leave in a warm place.

The two killers, straight from the schemes: heating to dryness collapses the answer to 1 mark (it drives off the water of crystallisation, and some salts decompose); and "leave the crystals to dry" without a method was refused the final mark in 2024 — name how.

Why excess? ✗ "to make more salt". ✓ "so that all the acid reacts" — so the solution contains only the salt and water.

Titration — finding exactly how much acid neutralises the alkali

Spec 2.33C · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q4 · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q2

A titration adds acid from a burette to a measured volume of alkali until the indicator just changes colour.

  1. Use a pipette to put exactly 25.0 cm³ of alkali into a conical flask. A pipette is more accurate than a measuring cylinder.
  2. Add a few drops of indicator — methyl orange or phenolphthalein, not universal indicator.
  3. Fill the burette with acid and read the start volume.
  4. Add acid, swirling the flask to mix, until the indicator just changes colour — the end point. Stand the flask on a white tile to see the colour change clearly.
  5. Read the final volume. The difference is the titre.
  6. Repeat until you have concordant titres — within 0.20 cm³ of each other.

Why not universal indicator? It changes colour gradually through many colours, so there is no sharp end point — 2022 Edexcel.

Method 2 — soluble salt from an acid + alkali

Spec 2.40C · 2025 Q2a

When both reactants are soluble, there is no excess to filter off, so a titration finds the exact volumes first.

This is for sodium, potassium and ammonium salts.

  1. Titrate with an indicator to find the exact volume of acid that neutralises 25.0 cm³ of alkali.
  2. Repeat with the same volumes but no indicator, so the salt isn't coloured.
  3. Heat the solution to the crystallisation point.
  4. Leave to cool and crystallise.
  5. Pour off the remaining liquid and dry the crystals in a warm place.

2025 asked only for steps 3–5 — and adding any new chemical at that stage scored zero: the solution already contains only the salt and water.

Method 3 — insoluble salt by precipitation

Spec 2.41C, 2.43C · 2022 Q7b · 2023 Q10 · 2025 Q7a · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q6(a) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q3(b)

Mix two solutions that each carry one ion of the insoluble salt; it forms as a solid that you filter, wash and dry.

Choose two soluble starting compounds — nitrates and sodium salts always are. For the required practical, lead(II) sulfate: lead(II) nitrate solution + sodium sulfate solution (or dilute sulfuric acid).

  1. Mix the two solutions — the schemes reject mixing solids.
  2. Filter — the salt you want is the residue on the paper.
  3. Wash the residue with distilled water — this rinses away the soluble by-product.
  4. Dry — filter paper, a warm oven, or leave in a warm place. No direct heating with a Bunsen (2022 rejected it).

Wrong-method answers are the biggest single loss on salt questions. Describing the excess-base method for a precipitation scored 1 of 4 in 2025 and 1 of 3 in 2023. And it is the residue that is washed and dried — 2023's marker: "the salt you want is the residue", not the filtrate. If the product is insoluble, there is no crystallisation step anywhere.

Choosing the method — the ten-second decision

every salt question · 2021 Q5a

Check whether the salt you want is soluble, then check whether its partner reactant is soluble — that picks the method.

  1. Is the target salt insoluble? (check the rules) → Method 3: mix solutions, filter, wash the residue, dry.
  2. Soluble, and the partner is an insoluble solid (metal, oxide or carbonate)? → Method 1: excess solid, filter, crystallise.
  3. Soluble, and both reactants soluble (Na/K/NH₄ salt from acid + alkali)? → Method 2: titrate, then crystallise.

2021's find-the-mistake question was this decision in disguise: nitric acid can't make a sulfate; magnesium carbonate is insoluble so there is no "solution of magnesium carbonate"; and boiling to dryness wrecks the hydrated crystals.

04Understanding it

U1

Why does the soluble-salt method demand an excess of the solid, when excess sounds wasteful — and how does the excess then leave the story?

Show answer

The excess guarantees that all the acid has reacted. If any acid survived, it would stay dissolved in the filtrate and end up concentrated in your crystals — the product would be contaminated with acid you cannot see.

An excess of an insoluble solid is easily removed: filter it off. That is the whole design of the method — the impurity you deliberately created is the one impurity filtering can remove. (2024's one-marker: "so that all the nitric acid reacts". "To make more salt" misses the point entirely.)

U2

Heating the filtrate "until crystals just start to form" and then stopping feels unfinished. Why is stopping there exactly right, and what goes wrong for a hydrated salt if you keep heating to dryness?

Show answer

Stopping at the crystallisation point leaves a hot saturated solution. As it cools, solubility falls and the salt comes out of solution slowly as well-formed crystals, leaving the remaining impurities behind in the liquid you then pour off.

Boiling to dryness does the opposite: everything in the solution — impurities included — is baked onto the dish, and for a hydrated salt the heat also drives off the water of crystallisation, so you get a powdery anhydrous solid instead of the hydrated crystals the question asked for. That is why every scheme caps a heated-to-dryness answer at 1 mark.

U3

In the precipitation method, what exactly does washing the residue with distilled water remove — and why does washing the filtrate instead earn the 2023 marker's special scorn?

Show answer

The precipitate on the filter paper is soaked in the reaction mixture, which contains the soluble by-product (sodium nitrate, in the PbBr₂ preparation) and any unreacted solutions. A rinse of distilled water dissolves those away; the insoluble product itself cannot dissolve, so it stays put.

The filtrate is the liquid that passed through: by-product solution. Washing it is washing the waste. The confusion comes from Method 1, where the filtrate is the valuable part — which is exactly why the papers keep asking both: they are testing whether you know which side of the filter paper your product is on. Insoluble product → residue; soluble product → filtrate.

U4

Silver chloride is insoluble. A student proposes making it by adding excess silver to hydrochloric acid, then filtering. Give two separate reasons the plan fails, and the correct recipe.

Show answer

Reason 1 — wrong method for the product: even if the reaction worked, an insoluble product forms wherever it is made — mixed in with the excess solid — and filtering would collect both together. The excess-solid method only works when the product stays dissolved.

Reason 2 — the reaction doesn't happen: silver is below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it does not react with dilute acids at all.

Correct recipe: precipitation from two soluble starting compounds — silver nitrate solution + sodium chloride solution (nitrates always soluble, sodium salts always soluble) → AgCl precipitates → filter, wash the residue, dry.

U5

Copper sulfate solution is mixed with sodium hydroxide solution. State what you would SEE, name both products, and explain which preparation method this reaction belongs to.

Show answer

Seen: a blue precipitate/solid forms (and the blue solution fades). Not "crystals", not "fizzing" — nothing here makes a gas, and 2023 rejected fizzing outright.

Products: copper(II) hydroxide (the blue precipitate — hydroxides are insoluble except Na/K) and sodium sulfate (stays dissolved — sodium salts always soluble).

Method: precipitation (Method 3): filter, wash the residue, dry gently. The question told you a precipitate formed; answers describing excess-base-and-crystallise were the standard way to lose all three method marks.

U6

Aluminium sulfate is used by gardeners to acidify soil: Al₂(SO₄)₃(s) + 6H₂O(l) → 2Al(OH)₃(s) + 3H₂SO₄(aq). Using only the state symbols, explain why the products make the soil acidic and not neutral.

Show answer

Both an acid and a base are produced — but their states differ. Al(OH)₃ is (s): solid, insoluble, so its OH⁻ ions are locked away and never enter the soil water. H₂SO₄ is (aq): dissolved, so its H⁺ ions are released into solution.

Free H⁺ with no free OH⁻ to cancel it → the soil water is acidic. The general lesson (it earned both marks in 2021): ions only count when they are in solution — (s) means present but unavailable.

U7

Why is universal indicator a poor choice for a titration, when it tells you more about pH than methyl orange does?

Show answer

A titration needs to spot one moment: the exact drop where the alkali is neutralised. Methyl orange and phenolphthalein change sharply between just two colours, so that drop is obvious.

Universal indicator changes gradually through red, orange, yellow, green, blue — there is no single clear point to stop at. More information about pH is useless if you can't see the end point. (2022 Edexcel: "no clear end point / colour change is gradual".)

U8

Dry hydrogen chloride gas dissolved in an organic solvent does not change the colour of blue litmus. The same gas dissolved in water turns it red. Explain.

Show answer

An acid is a source of H⁺ ions in aqueous solution. HCl is a covalent molecule. In water it forms H⁺ ions (and Cl⁻ ions), so the solution is acidic and litmus turns red.

In the organic solvent the HCl molecules stay as molecules — no H⁺ ions form — so there is nothing to change the indicator, and the litmus stays blue. Acidity is about the H⁺ ions, not just the formula. (2024 Edexcel 2C Q7, four marks.)
