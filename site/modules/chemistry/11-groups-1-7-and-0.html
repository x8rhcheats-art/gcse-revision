Three mark-scheme lines that cost the most marks in this topic.

2024, on the colour of phenolphthalein in the sodium solution: "REJECT red or purple". The answer is pink, nothing else.

2023, on the six-mark displacement question: "Penalise incorrect use of -ine and -ide". Bromine is the element. Bromide is the ion in potassium bromide.

January 2023, on halogen boiling points: "No M2, M3 or M4 if any mention of breaking covalent/ionic bonds". Boiling a halogen breaks the intermolecular forces. It does not break the covalent bond inside the molecule.

02How it actually works

There is one idea behind this whole module: an atom's outer electrons decide how it reacts. Elements in the same group have the same number of outer electrons, so they react in the same way. As you go down a group the outer shell gets further from the nucleus, and that single change explains both reactivity trends. Group 1 gets more reactive going down. Group 7 gets less reactive going down. Group 0 has a full outer shell, so it hardly reacts at all.

Why a group behaves like a family

Spec 1.23 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q4(b)(i)

Elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their outer shell, so they have similar chemical properties. Chemical reactions happen at the outer shell, and that is where group members match.

1 — lithium 2.1, sodium 2.8.1, potassium 2.8.8.11Loses 1 electronLi⁺, Na⁺, K⁺
7 — fluorine 2.7, chlorine 2.8.77Gains 1 electronF⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻
0 — helium 2, neon 2.8, argon 2.8.8Full shell (2 for helium, 8 for the rest)Does not lose, gain or share electronsNone

✗ "They have the same number of electrons." Lithium has 3 electrons and sodium has 11.
✓ "They have the same number of electrons in the outer shell — one."

Explaining the Group 1 trend with electrons (triple only)

Spec 2.4C · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q1(b) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q4(b)(ii)

Going down Group 1, the outer electron is further from the nucleus, so it is less strongly attracted and is lost more easily. So reactivity increases.

  1. A Group 1 atom reacts by losing its one outer electron.
  2. Down the group there are more shells, so the outer electron is further from the nucleus. The inner shells also shield it.
  3. So the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is weaker.
  4. So the outer electron is lost more easily, and the metal is more reactive.

Mark script — "Explain, in terms of electron configuration, why sodium is more reactive than lithium" (3)

1. Sodium's outer shell is further from the nucleus. Sodium has more shells (2.8.1 compared with 2.1), so it is a larger atom. 1

2. There is less attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron in sodium 1

3. So the outer electron is more easily lost 1

The 2022 question asked it the other way round ("why is lithium less reactive"). The same three points work in reverse: smaller atom, outer electron more strongly attracted, less easily lost.

✗ "Sodium has more electrons, so it loses them more easily."
✗ "…so its outer electrons are lost more easily." The 2024 scheme ignored the plural "electrons" in M1 and M2 but did not allow it in M3. Sodium loses one outer electron.
✓ "Sodium's outer electron is further from the nucleus, so there is weaker attraction, so it is lost more easily."

Displacement as redox: ionic and half-equations

Spec 2.7 · link to module 08 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q7(d) (the scheme accepted the ionic equation)

In a displacement, the more reactive halogen gains electrons and is reduced, and the halide ion loses electrons and is oxidised. The halogen is the oxidising agent.

The potassium ions do nothing. They are spectator ions, so leave them out of the ionic equation:

Chlorine and bromide — ionic equationCl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂charges balance: −2 on each side
Bromine and iodide — ionic equationBr₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂

Split one into two half-equations:

Reduction half-equationCl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻chlorine gains electrons — reduced
Oxidation half-equation2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻bromide ions lose electrons — oxidised

A halogen is more reactive when it is better at gaining electrons, which makes it a stronger oxidising agent. Chlorine takes electrons from bromide ions. Bromine cannot take them back from chloride ions.

✗ "Cl₂ + Br⁻ → Cl⁻ + Br₂" — the atoms and charges do not balance.
✗ "The chlorine is oxidised." Chlorine gains electrons: OIL RIG, so it is reduced.
✓ "Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂. Chlorine is reduced and bromide ions are oxidised. Chlorine is the oxidising agent."

Explaining the Group 7 trend with electrons (triple only)

Spec 2.8C · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q3(c)

Going down Group 7, the outer shell is further from the nucleus, so there is weaker attraction for an incoming electron, and one is gained less easily. So reactivity decreases.

  1. A halogen atom reacts by gaining one electron to fill its outer shell.
  2. Down the group there are more shells, so the outer shell is further from the nucleus and more shielded.
  3. So the nucleus attracts an extra electron less strongly.
  4. So an electron is gained less readily, and the halogen is less reactive.

Group 0: why the noble gases do not react

Spec 1.24 · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q1(b) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q3(b)(ii) · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q6(b) · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q1(a)(i) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q4(a)(ii) · 2024 school Q1(c)

Noble gas atoms have a full outer shell of electrons, so they do not need to lose, gain or share electrons. That makes them unreactive (inert).

  1. Helium has 2 outer electrons, which fills its only shell. Neon (2.8) and argon (2.8.8) have 8.
  2. A full outer shell is stable.
  3. So the atom has no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons, and it does not form bonds.
  4. So the noble gases exist as single atoms (they are monatomic) and hardly ever react.

Mark script — "Explain, in terms of electron configuration, why neon is unreactive" (2)

1. Neon has a full outer shell — 8 outer electrons, 2.8 1

2. so it does not need to lose or gain (or share) electrons 1

Using an unreactive gas. Argon is about 1% of air and is the least reactive gas in it. It is used wherever something must be kept away from oxygen:

  • Titanium is extracted with magnesium (TiCl₄ + 2Mg → Ti + 2MgCl₂) in a container of argon. Argon is unreactive, so it will not react with the hot magnesium. In air, the oxygen would oxidise the magnesium (2022 Edexcel 2C Q6(b)).
  • Filament light bulbs are filled with argon, so the hot filament does not react with oxygen.
  • Helium is used in balloons because it has a low density and, unlike hydrogen, is unreactive, so it will not burn.

✓ "Argon is unreactive, so it will not react with the magnesium. Oxygen in air would oxidise it."

✗ "Neon is stable." That is only half an answer.
✓ "Neon has a full outer shell, so it does not need to lose, gain or share electrons."

04Understanding it

U1

Lithium, sodium and potassium react with water at very different speeds. Why do chemists still say this is evidence that they belong to one family?

Show answer

Because the kind of reaction is the same every time. Each metal gives a metal hydroxide and hydrogen, and each leaves an alkaline solution: 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂. The same equation fits all three, with only the symbol changed.

The speed is a difference, and differences show a trend (spec 2.2). The similarities show a family (spec 2.1). Both come from the outer shell: each atom has one outer electron to lose, and it is lost more easily further down the group.

U2

Potassium has 19 protons and sodium has 11. With a much more positive nucleus, why does potassium lose its outer electron more easily?

Show answer

The extra protons are outweighed by two other effects. Potassium's outer electron is in the fourth shell (2.8.8.1), so it is much further from the nucleus than sodium's (2.8.1). It also has a full extra shell of electrons between it and the nucleus, which shields it.

Attraction falls off quickly with distance. So the net pull on potassium's outer electron is weaker, the electron is lost more easily, and potassium is more reactive. That is why the mark scheme wants "further from the nucleus" and "less attraction". Counting protons or electrons scores nothing.

U3

The outer shell gets further from the nucleus going down both Group 1 and Group 7. So why does reactivity go up in one group and down in the other?

Show answer

The two groups react in opposite ways. Group 1 atoms lose an electron, and weaker attraction makes that easier, so reactivity increases.

Group 7 atoms gain an electron. The nucleus has to pull a new electron in, and weaker attraction makes that harder, so reactivity decreases.

It is the same cause (weaker attraction further down) with opposite effects. Always say which job the atom is doing, losing or gaining, before you explain the trend.

U4

Bromine water is added to potassium chloride solution and nothing happens. How can "nothing happens" be evidence of anything?

Show answer

If bromine were more reactive than chlorine, it would take chlorine's place: Br₂ + 2Cl⁻ → 2Br⁻ + Cl₂. The orange colour would fade, because chlorine water is almost colourless.

It stays orange, so bromine cannot displace chlorine. Bromine is not able to take electrons from chloride ions, so chlorine must be more reactive than bromine.

In the 2023 six-marker, the "no change" pair gave three of the marks: the observation, "bromine cannot displace chlorine", and "chlorine is more reactive than bromine".

U5

Going down Group 7, boiling points rise but reactivity falls. A student says: "Iodine's bonds are stronger, so it boils higher and reacts less." What is wrong with this?

Show answer

The student has mixed up two different things.

Boiling separates whole molecules from each other. It overcomes the intermolecular forces between I₂ molecules, and those get stronger as the molecules get bigger. The I–I covalent bond does not break when iodine boils. Mentioning broken covalent bonds lost all three explanation marks in January 2023.

Reactivity depends on single atoms gaining an electron. Iodine's outer shell is far from the nucleus, so the attraction for an electron is weak. It is an atom property, not a bond strength property.

U6

Helium has only 2 outer electrons, but argon has 8. Why are both unreactive, and why are they placed in the same group?

Show answer

What matters is that the outer shell is full, not the number 8. Helium's only shell holds a maximum of 2, so 2 electrons fill it. Argon's outer shell is full with 8 (2.8.8).

A full outer shell is stable, so neither atom needs to lose, gain or share electrons. Neither forms bonds, both exist as single atoms, and both are chemically similar. That is why helium sits in Group 0 with the others. The school's 2024 scheme accepted "Group 8" for this group, but "Group 0" is the standard name.

U7

Nobody has ever seen enough astatine to test it. How can you confidently predict what happens when chlorine water is added to sodium astatide solution?

Show answer

Astatine is below iodine in Group 7, so it is the least reactive halogen. Its outer shell is furthest from the nucleus, so it has the weakest pull on an extra electron.

Chlorine is more reactive, so it would displace astatine: Cl₂ + 2NaAt → 2NaCl + At₂. Every other halogen would displace it too. Going the other way, astatine would displace nothing.

Using a trend to predict an element you have never studied is exactly what spec 2.6 asks you to do.

U8

Sodium melts into a ball on water, but lithium stays solid. Both reactions give out heat. Why the difference?

Show answer

There are two reasons, and they add together.

Melting point: sodium melts at 98 °C and lithium at 181 °C. The heat from the reaction is enough to melt sodium but not lithium.

Rate: lithium is less reactive, so it reacts more slowly and releases its heat more slowly. The water carries the heat away before the metal gets that hot.

Potassium has an even lower melting point (63 °C) and reacts even faster. So it melts, and it gives out enough heat to ignite the hydrogen.
