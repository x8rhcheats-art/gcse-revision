02How it actually works

Solubility is a number with strict units: how many grams of solute saturate 100 g of water at a given temperature. The whole topic is that sentence plus a graph of how the number changes with temperature.

The four words, exactly

Spec 1.4

The solute is the solid that dissolves; the solvent is the liquid doing the dissolving; together they make a solution. A solution is saturated when no more solute will dissolve at that temperature — the phrase "at that temperature" carries the mark, because a saturated solution at 70 °C is an unsaturated one at 80 °C.

What affects how much dissolves? Temperature (the examinable factor — 2024's answer), plus the choice of solvent and, of course, how much solvent you have. Stirring and crushing change the speed of dissolving, never the amount — the schemes explicitly ignore them.

Solubility curves — reading them without losing marks

Spec 1.5C, 1.6C · 2022 Q4 · 2023 Q12 · 2025 Q5

A solubility curve plots g per 100 g of water against temperature; for most solids it rises. Each point on the curve reads: "a saturated solution at this temperature holds this much." The three set-piece readings:

  • Where are two solubilities equal? Where the curves cross. Draw the vertical line down from the crossing and show it — the answer without the construction line lost a mark in both 2023 and 2025.
  • How much dissolves in some other amount of water? Scale linearly: 124 g per 100 g means 124 × 2.5 = 310 g per 250 g. If the data is "per 25 cm³", multiply by 4 for per 100 g — 1 cm³ of water has a mass of 1 g, so cm³ of water and g of water are the same number.
  • Read the curve the question names. Two curves are printed precisely so you can grab the wrong one under time pressure — 2023's most common error.

Cooling a saturated solution — where the crystals come from

Spec 1.6C · 2023 Q12b · 2024 Q2c · 2025 Q5d

Cool a saturated solution and the water can hold less; the surplus crystallises out. The mass of crystals is a subtraction of two curve readings:

The two-read subtraction

1. Solubility at the hot temperature (read the curve, show the lines) 1

2. Solubility at the cold temperature, subtract: mass crystallising per 100 g = hot − cold 1

2023: 45 − 13 = 32 g. If the solution used 250 g of water, scale the answer by 2.5 at the end.

This is the quantitative version of the crystallisation step in salt preparation: heat to saturation, cool, and the falling curve hands you crystals.

Measuring solubility — the evaporating-basin method

Spec 1.7 (practical) · 2024 Q2d

The school's method, in the order the flowchart question wants:

  1. Put a known volume of water in a beaker; add solute a little at a time, stirring, until no more dissolves (some solid remains — the solution is saturated).
  2. Filter to obtain a measured volume of the clear saturated solution.
  3. Weigh an empty evaporating basin; add the solution and weigh again.
  4. Gently heat to evaporate all the water, then weigh the basin with the dry salt.

Mass of salt = (basin + dry salt) − (empty basin); scale to 100 g of water. 2024's numbers: 78.2 − 50.5 = 27.7 g from 50 cm³ of water → × 2 = 55.4 g per 100 g.

Two subtleties the papers have asked: values measured near 100 °C are less accurate because water evaporates while you work (2022); and yes — here you do evaporate to dryness, because the product being weighed is the total dissolved salt, not nice hydrated crystals. Purpose decides the technique.

04Understanding it

U1

A saturated solution of KNO₃ at 70 °C sits in an open beaker overnight and cools to 20 °C. Describe everything that is different in the morning, with the reasoning.

Show answer

Crystals of KNO₃ now sit at the bottom. At 70 °C the water held the full 70-degree solubility; at 20 °C it can hold far less. The surplus had nowhere to go but out of solution — it crystallised.

The remaining liquid is still saturated — but at 20 °C, holding the 20-degree amount. The mass of crystals = (solubility at 70°) − (solubility at 20°), scaled to the actual mass of water. That subtraction is the exam question, every year.

U2

2025's table gave the mass dissolving in 25 cm³ of water, and the answer line demanded "g per 100 g of water". What conversion is needed, and why does the question feel entitled to swap between cm³ and g of water without comment?

Show answer

Multiply the reading by 4 — four lots of 25 make 100.

The silent swap works because 1.00 cm³ of water has a mass of 1.00 g — the density of water is the one conversion factor the papers assume without stating (2024 printed it as a hint; 2025 didn't). So 25 cm³ of water is 25 g of water, and volume-of-water numbers slot straight into solubility units.

It only works for water — never apply it to another liquid or to the solution itself.

U3

Why are solubility values measured at 95 °C systematically less accurate than those at 25 °C — and is the error high or low?

Show answer

Near boiling, the water evaporates significantly while the measurement is being made. Less water is present than you think.

The same mass of dissolved salt sitting in less water than recorded means the calculated "per 100 g" figure comes out too high — the salt gets credited to water that has already left. (The one-mark exam answer is just "water evaporates"; the direction of the error is the understanding behind it.)

U4

In the evaporating-basin practical you heat to complete dryness — the exact step the salt-preparation module forbids. Why is it right here and wrong there?

Show answer

Different goal, different technique. Here the product is a number: the total mass of dissolved salt. Boiling everything off and weighing the solid residue measures exactly that — you want every last gram out of solution, and you don't care what state the crystals are in.

In salt preparation the product is the substance: well-formed, hydrated crystals. There, heating to dryness wrecks the crystals and drives off the water of crystallisation.

If an exam answer needs one sentence: evaporate to dryness to measure solubility; heat only to the crystallisation point to prepare crystals.

U5

Two curves cross at 68 °C. A student answers "at 68 °C the solutions are the same" and scores one mark of two. What exactly did the second mark want?

Show answer

The construction line on the graph. The schemes in 2023, 2024 and 2025 all award a mark for the visible working: the vertical line dropped from the crossing point (or lines to the axes for any reading).

The habit that banks these marks: for any value read from a graph, rule the lines first, read second, and leave the lines on the paper. It also silently protects you — an answer read from the wrong place with lines shown can still earn the method mark.

05Exam drill

D1

The solubility of copper(II) chloride is 45 g per 100 g of water at 60 °C and 13 g per 100 g at 10 °C.

(a) A saturated solution is made with 100 g of water at 60 °C and cooled to 10 °C. Determine the mass of copper(II) chloride that crystallises. (2)
(b) State what must be shown on the graph when the values are read from solubility curves. (1)

Show answer

(a) Readings 45 and 13 1; mass crystallising = 45 − 13 = 32 g 1

(b) The working/construction lines used to read each value 1

D2

The solubility of solid B is 124 g per 100 g of water at 60 °C. Calculate the mass of B that dissolves in 250 g of water at 60 °C. Show your working. (2)

Show answer

124 g dissolves per 100 g of water 1; 124 × 2.5 = 310 g 1

Linear scaling — and read the correct curve before you scale.

D3

A student determines the solubility of a salt: mass of empty evaporating basin = 50.5 g; mass of basin + dry salt after evaporating 50.0 cm³ of saturated solution = 78.2 g. [1.00 cm³ of water has a mass of 1.00 g]

(a) Calculate the solubility of the salt in g per 100 g of water. (2)
(b) Before evaporating, the student filtered the mixture. State why. (1)

Show answer

(a) Mass of salt = 78.2 − 50.5 = 27.7 g (in 50 g of water) 1; solubility = 27.7 × 2 = 55.4 g per 100 g of water 1

(b) To remove the undissolved solid, so only the saturated solution is evaporated 1

D4

These steps for measuring solubility are jumbled: A — gently heat the basin to remove all the water; B — filter to obtain 50.0 cm³ of saturated solution; C — record the mass of basin and salt solution; D — record the mass of the empty basin; E — record the mass of basin and dry salt; F — put 50.0 cm³ of water into a beaker; G — add salt, stirring, until no more dissolves; H — pour the solution into the basin.

Write the eight letters in the correct order. (2)

Show answer

F → G → B → D → H → C → A → E 1 1

The logic: make the saturated solution (F, G), separate it (B), weigh empty before filling (D), then solution (H, C), then evaporate and weigh dry (A, E). The basin must be weighed empty before anything goes in it.

D5

(a) State what is meant by a saturated solution. (2)
(b) State one factor that affects the solubility of a solid in water. (1)
(c) Suggest why solubility values measured at 95 °C may be less accurate than those measured at lower temperatures. (1)

Show answer

(a) A solution in which no more solute can dissolve 1 at that temperature 1

(b) Temperature 1 (stirring and surface area affect only the speed — ignored by the scheme)

(c) Water evaporates (near boiling), so the mass of water present is less than recorded 1
