02How it actually works

This module runs in spec order, and that is also the order the papers use. States of matter and mixtures open Paper 1C almost every series. Separation is the method-naming question. Solubility is the ten-mark graph. Solubility itself is a number with strict units: how many grams of solute saturate 100 g of water at a given temperature. Nearly every mark in that half is either a definition, a reading off the graph, or one line of arithmetic.

The three states, and the particle diagram

Spec 1.1 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q1(a) · 2023 Nov Edexcel 1C Q1(a) · 2024 Nov Edexcel 1C Q1(b)

Going solid → liquid → gas, the particles get further apart, more randomly arranged and faster-moving.

StateArrangementMovementSpacing
SolidRegular, close-packed, touchingVibrate about fixed positionsTouching
LiquidClose together but randomly arrangedMove around past each otherTouching, but able to slide
GasRandom, far apartMove quickly in all directionsFar apart, not touching

The drawing mark is strict, and the schemes say so. For a liquid: circles still touching, but not in rows. For a gas: circles spread out and none touching — 2023 Nov Edexcel 1C Q1(a) is marked "REJECT any particles touching", and 2024 Nov asks for "6 circles similar size randomly arranged none touching", with at least one near the top of the box so the spread is obvious.

✗ Gas particles drawn evenly spaced in a grid — that is random spacing failed.
✗ Liquid particles drawn with gaps between them — a liquid has nearly the same spacing as a solid.
✓ Solid: rows, touching. Liquid: jumbled, touching. Gas: scattered, none touching, similar sized circles.

Changes of state — the six names

Spec 1.2 · 2024 Nov Edexcel 1C Q1(a), (c) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q3(a) · 2021 Jan Edexcel 1C Q1(a)

Learn all six names with their directions, and the state symbol equation that goes with each.

ChangeNameExample equation
Solid → liquidMeltingH₂O(s) → H₂O(l)
Liquid → solidFreezingH₂O(l) → H₂O(s)
Liquid → gasBoiling or evaporationH₂O(l) → H₂O(g)
Gas → liquidCondensingH₂O(g) → H₂O(l)
Solid → gas, and backSublimation (subliming)CO₂(s) → CO₂(g)

2024 Nov Edexcel 1C asked for three of these from a word box, then for the equation for water freezing — H₂O(l) → H₂O(s), one mark, and the state symbols are the whole mark. Heating and cooling are not changes of state; they appear in the word box as distractors.

Evaporation and boiling are not the same thing, and the difference is examined: evaporation happens at any temperature and only from the surface; boiling happens at one fixed temperature and throughout the whole liquid. Say "throughout the liquid", not "it bubbles" — the schemes ignore bubbles.

Diffusion — and why gases diffuse faster

Spec 1.3 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q2(d) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1CR Q1(b) · 2020 Edexcel 1C Q2(a)

Diffusion is the spreading out of particles from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration, because the particles are moving randomly.

Two standard demonstrations, and the explanation is the same both times:

  • A crystal of potassium manganate(VII) dropped into still water: the purple colour spreads through the whole beaker without stirring, because the dissolved particles move randomly and spread from where they are concentrated to where they are not.
  • Bromine vapour released at one end of a tube: the brown colour fills the tube. It happens faster in a gas than in a liquid, because gas particles move much faster and are much further apart, so they are less obstructed.

Diffusion appears in the changes-of-state word box as a distractor — it is not a change of state, so if a question asks for the name of a change from liquid to gas, diffusion is the wrong answer. 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q2(d) set up exactly the dissolving crystal above, then asked for the state-symbol equation (X(s) → X(aq)) and, separately, for the other process going on in the beaker — diffusion.

Why heating makes a liquid evaporate faster

Spec 1.1, 1.2 · 2023 Nov Edexcel 1C Q1(b) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q1(c)

The particles gain kinetic energy, so more of them have enough energy to escape from the surface.

  1. Heating increases the kinetic energy of the particles, so they move faster.
  2. More particles then have enough energy to overcome the forces of attraction holding them in the liquid, and escape from the surface.

2023 Nov Edexcel 1C Q1(b), two marks, and the scheme is explicit about what fails:

"REJECT breaking of bonds" — there are no bonds to break here. Say forces of attraction between particles.
"IGNORE it has more energy / heat energy increases" — name kinetic energy, or say the particles move faster.
"IGNORE particles vibrate more" — vibrating is what solid particles do.
"IGNORE more collisions" — that is a rates answer, not an evaporation answer.

Element, compound or mixture

Spec 1.8 · 2025 Edexcel 2C Q1(a) · 2024 Nov Edexcel 1C Q2

An element contains only one kind of atom; a compound has atoms of two or more elements chemically bonded together; a mixture is two or more substances not chemically bonded.

  • Element — one kind of atom only. Calcium, neon, fluorine. Note that F₂ is still an element: two atoms, but the same kind.
  • Compound — different elements chemically bonded. Water, H₂O. Both halves score: 2024 Nov Edexcel 1C Q2(b) gives one mark for "atoms of two different elements" and one for "chemically bonded / combined together".
  • Mixture — substances just mixed, not chemically bonded, so they keep their own properties and can be separated physically. Petrol, crude oil, air, sea water, ink.
  • Diatomic molecule — a molecule of exactly two atoms: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂.

2025 Edexcel 2C Q1(a) was four one-mark lifts from a single word box — compound, mixture, diatomic molecule, metal element — and then asked which technique separates crude oil. That whole question is recall, and it opened the paper.

✗ "A compound is two or more elements joined." — "Joined" is doing the work of "chemically bonded", and the schemes do not accept it on its own.
✗ "A mixture is a compound of several things."
✓ Compound: "atoms of different elements chemically bonded together."
✓ Mixture: "two or more substances not chemically bonded together."

The four words, exactly

Spec 1.4 · 2024 Q2

A solute dissolves in a solvent to make a solution; the solution is saturated when no more solute will dissolve at that temperature.

  • The solute is the substance that dissolves — in this topic, a solid such as potassium nitrate.
  • The solvent is the liquid it dissolves in — almost always water.
  • The solution is the mixture they make together.
  • A saturated solution is one in which no more solute can dissolve at that temperature. Some undissolved solid is usually left at the bottom — that is how you know it is saturated.

✗ "A saturated solution is full up." — No temperature, no second mark.
✗ "A saturated solution has lots of solute in it." — "Lots" is not a definition.
✓ "A solution in which no more solute will dissolve at that temperature."

The phrase "at that temperature" carries the mark. A solution saturated at 70 °C is unsaturated at 80 °C, because hot water can hold more.

What changes solubility — and what only speeds up dissolving

Spec 1.4, 1.5C · 2024 Q2(a)

Temperature changes how much solid can dissolve; stirring and crushing only change how fast it dissolves.

Two different questions hide in the word "dissolving":

QuestionWhat affects itWhat does not
How much dissolves (solubility)Temperature. For most solids, hotter water dissolves moreStirring, crushing, shaking
How fast it dissolvesStirring, crushing into smaller pieces, warming—

The 2024 scheme wanted temperature. It also allowed the amount of solvent, because more water dissolves more solid in total. It ignored stirring. Stirring gets you to the same final amount sooner — it never raises the amount.

✗ "Stirring increases the solubility."
✓ "Temperature affects solubility. Stirring only makes the solid dissolve faster."

Solubility in g per 100 g of solvent

Spec 1.5C · 2024 Q2(d)(ii) · 2025 Q5(c)

Solubility is the mass of solute, in grams, that dissolves in 100 g of solvent to make a saturated solution at a stated temperature.

Three parts, and each one matters:

  1. A mass of solute in grams.
  2. Per 100 g of solvent — not per 100 g of solution, and not per whatever volume you happened to use.
  3. At a stated temperature, because the value changes with temperature.
Solubility from an experimentsolubility = (mass of solute ÷ mass of water) × 100solubility in g per 100 g of water · both masses in g · 1 cm³ of water has a mass of 1 g

Water has a density of 1 g per cm³, so 25 cm³ of water is 25 g of water. That lets the papers swap between cm³ and g of water without comment. It works for water only.

Reading a solubility curve

Spec 1.6C · 2022 Q4 · 2023 Q12 · 2025 Q5

Every point on a solubility curve is a saturated solution: at this temperature, 100 g of water holds exactly this much.

The x-axis is temperature in °C. The y-axis is solubility in g per 100 g of water. For most solids the curve rises — hotter water dissolves more. To read a value:

  1. Check the label: you are on the curve the question names.
  2. Rule a line up from the temperature to the curve.
  3. Rule a line across to the y-axis and read the value.
  4. Leave both lines on the graph. They are the "working shown" mark.

Where a solution sits compared with the curve tells you its state. On the curve: saturated. Below it: unsaturated — more could still dissolve at that temperature.

2023's commentary: "Several read off the WRONG curve." Two curves are printed so you can grab the wrong one under time pressure. Put your finger on the label before you rule a line.

Where two curves cross

Spec 1.6C · 2022 Q4(i) · 2023 Q12(a) · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q4(b) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q3(a)(i)

Two substances have the same solubility at the temperature where their curves cross.

  1. Find the crossing point.
  2. Rule a vertical line straight down from it to the temperature axis.
  3. Read the temperature and give the unit, °C.

The crossing answer — two marks

1. Working shown on the graph — the vertical line from the crossing 1

2. The temperature read correctly, e.g. 56 °C 1

That is the exact split in 2023 Q12(a) and in 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q3(a)(i). The answer alone gets one mark.

Below the crossing one substance is more soluble. Above it the other one is. If a question asks "which is more soluble at 30 °C?", read both curves at 30 °C and compare — do not assume the steeper curve wins everywhere.

Scaling to a different mass of water

Spec 1.5C · 2022 Q4(ii) · 2025 Q5(c) · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q4(c)

The mass that dissolves is in direct proportion to the mass of water, so you scale by multiplying or dividing.

Double the water, double the solid. So you only need a scale factor: new mass of water ÷ 100, or the reverse to get back to per 100 g.

Worked example 1 — more water (2022 Q4)

Solubility of B at 60 °C = 124 g per 100 g of water.

Scale factor for 250 g of water = 250 ÷ 100 = 2.5

Mass dissolving = 124 × 2.5 = 310 g

Worked example 2 — data per 25 cm³ (2024 Edexcel 2C Q4 / school 2025 Q5)

Reading from the graph at 30 °C: about 24 g dissolves in 25 cm³ of water.

25 cm³ of water = 25 g of water, and 100 ÷ 25 = 4

Solubility = 24 × 4 = 96 g per 100 g of water

The scheme gives one mark for the reading and one for the × 4. Forget the × 4 and your answer is in the wrong units, so it scores only the reading.

How many crystals form on cooling

Spec 1.6C · 2023 Q12(b) · 2024 Q2(c)(iii) · 2025 Q5(d) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q3(a)(ii) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q3(c)(iii)

When a saturated solution cools, the water can hold less, so the extra solute crystallises out.

  1. At the hot temperature the solution is saturated — it holds the hot-temperature amount.
  2. As it cools, the solubility falls.
  3. The water can no longer hold all the solute.
  4. The surplus comes out of solution as crystals. The liquid left is still saturated, at the new, lower temperature.
Mass crystallising on coolingmass of crystals = solubility at hot temperature − solubility at cold temperatureboth read from the same curve · gives the mass per 100 g of water · scale if the water is not 100 g

The two-read subtraction — worked (2023 Q12)

1. Solubility of CuCl₂ at 60 °C, lines shown on the graph = 45 g 1

2. Solubility at 10 °C = 13 g, so mass crystallising = 45 − 13 = 32 g 1

If the solution used 250 g of water, scale at the end: 32 × 2.5 = 80 g.

Watch the units of the graph. In 2024 Edexcel 2C Q4(d) the curves were plotted per 25 cm³, so 21 − 8 = 13 g was already the answer for 25 cm³ of water. No scaling was needed because the question asked about 25 cm³.

2024: "Must show working on the graph." Rule both readings.
✗ "32 g" written on the answer line with a clean graph.
✓ Two sets of construction lines on the graph, then "45 − 13 = 32 g".

Plotting the points and drawing the curve

Spec 1.6C · 2024 Q2(c) · 2025 Q5(a) · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q4(a) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q3(c)

Plot every point to within half a small square, then draw one smooth curve through or close to them.

  1. Plot each point with a neat cross, accurate to ± half a small square.
  2. Look at the shape. Solubility points almost always lie on a curve, not a straight line.
  3. Draw a single, smooth curve of best fit in one stroke. Do not join the dots with a ruler.
  4. If it does not pass through every point, leave an even spread of points either side.
  5. With two sets of data, label each curve with its substance.

2024 scheme: "Best-fit curve must be a single line (not sketchy)".
2025 scheme: unlabelled curves lost a mark.
✗ Feathery, doubled-up lines, or dot-to-dot straight segments.
✓ One clean curve per substance, labelled KNO₃ and NaNO₃.

The plotting marks are the easiest on the paper. In 2024 Edexcel 2C Q4 they were four of the ten marks — two for points, two for curves.

Measuring solubility — the evaporating-basin practical

Spec 1.7C (practical) · 2024 Q2(d) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q3(b)

Make a saturated solution at a known temperature, evaporate the water from a weighed sample, and weigh the solid left behind.

The school's method, in the order the 2024 flowchart question wanted:

  1. Put a known volume of water in a beaker, at the chosen temperature.
  2. Add the solid a little at a time, stirring, until no more dissolves and some solid is left over. The solution is now saturated.
  3. Filter to remove the undissolved solid, keeping a measured volume of the clear saturated solution.
  4. Weigh an empty evaporating basin.
  5. Pour the solution into the basin and weigh it again.
  6. Heat gently to evaporate the water, then weigh the basin and dry solid.

Worked example — the 2024 numbers

Mass of salt = (basin + dry salt) − (empty basin) = 78.2 − 50.5 = 27.7 g

This dissolved in 50 cm³ = 50 g of water. Scale factor = 100 ÷ 50 = 2

Solubility = 27.7 × 2 = 55.4 g per 100 g of water

Weighing the basin with the solution is not wasted. It gives you the mass of water directly: mass of water = (basin + solution) − (basin + dry salt). 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q3(b) worked this way — 5.1 g of solid and 15 g of water, so 5.1 ÷ 15 × 100 = 34 g per 100 g.

To measure solubility at another temperature, keep the beaker in a water bath at that temperature and check it with a thermometer, because solubility changes with temperature.

Evaporate to dryness here, but not when making crystals

Spec 1.7C · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q3(b)(iv) · links to salt preparation, module 4

The purpose decides the heating: measuring solubility needs all the water gone; making crystals needs most of it left.

GoalHow far you heatWhy
Measure solubilityEvaporate to dryness, gentlyYou need the total mass of dissolved solid, so every gram must come out of solution
Prepare a pure, dry saltHeat only to the crystallisation point, then leave to coolYou want well-formed, hydrated crystals; boiling dry wrecks them

"Gently" matters. Heat a hydrated salt such as copper(II) sulfate too strongly and it loses its water of crystallisation and turns into the anhydrous salt. The mass of solid is then wrong. That was the 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q3(b)(iv) mark.

✗ "Always heat to dryness to get the salt."
✓ "Evaporate to dryness to measure solubility; heat only until crystals start to form to prepare crystals."

Why some readings are less accurate

Spec 1.7C · 2022 Q4(iii)

Near 100 °C, water evaporates while you work, so there is less water than you think.

  1. At 95 °C the water is close to boiling, so some of it evaporates during the measurement.
  2. The dissolved solid is then shared between less water than was recorded.
  3. So the values measured at high temperature are less accurate.

The one-mark answer in 2022 was simply "water evaporates". The same idea explains why the temperature must be held steady throughout. If the saturated solution cools while it is being filtered, some solid crystallises out early and is lost.

Pure or impure — the melting and boiling point test

Spec 1.9 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q9(b)(iii)

A pure substance melts and boils at one fixed temperature; a mixture melts or boils over a range of temperatures.

So you can test purity without any chemistry at all — just a thermometer. Pure water boils at exactly 100 °C and freezes at exactly 0 °C at normal pressure. Anything dissolved in it raises the boiling point and lowers the freezing point, and the change happens over a spread rather than at a point.

2023 Edexcel 1C Q9 collected the water driven off a hydrated salt and asked how to prove it was pure water. Two marks: measure its boiling point and show it is 100 °C (or its freezing point, and show it is 0 °C). The chemical test with anhydrous copper(II) sulfate shows water is present — only the physical test shows it is pure. That distinction is in module 14 too, and it is worth two marks each time it appears.

Separating mixtures — picking the right method

Spec 1.10 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q1 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q4(b) · 2025 Edexcel 2C Q1(b) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q1 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q2(b)–(c)

Five techniques, and the question is always just "what is in the mixture?"

MethodUse it to separateWhat it exploits
FiltrationAn insoluble solid from a liquidThe solid will not fit through the filter paper. The solid left behind is the residue; the liquid that passes through is the filtrate
CrystallisationA soluble solid from its solution, as crystalsEvaporate some of the water, then cool — the solid is less soluble in cold water, so it crystallises out
Simple distillationThe solvent from a solution — pure water from sea waterOnly the solvent boils off; the solute stays behind. The vapour is then cooled back to a liquid
Fractional distillationTwo or more liquids from each other — crude oil, or ethanol from waterThey have different boiling points, so they condense at different heights in the column
Paper chromatographyDissolved solids from each other — the dyes in an inkThey have different solubilities in the solvent, so they travel different distances

2023 Edexcel 1C opened the whole paper with this, for seven marks: gasoline from crude oil (fractional distillation), the dyes in black ink (chromatography), pure water from sea water (simple distillation), and then a four-mark passage to complete — dissolving the copper(II) sulfate, filtering off the insoluble copper(II) oxide, evaporating some of the water, then crystallisation to get the hydrated salt. That four-step sequence — dissolve, filter, evaporate, crystallise — is the answer to every "obtain a pure, dry sample of the soluble substance" question.

The mirror image is "obtain a pure, dry sample of the insoluble one": filter, wash the residue with distilled water, then dry it — in a warm oven or between filter papers. That was 2024 Edexcel 2C Q6(a), three marks, for lead(II) bromide, and 2025 Edexcel 2C Q5(d)(ii) wanted the soluble version, four marks, for potassium sulfate crystals. Module 4 works both through as salt preparations; this is the same apparatus with the mixture named instead of the reaction.

Simple distillation — the apparatus marks

Spec 1.10 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q4(b)

Heat the solution, and the condenser turns the vapour back into a liquid that drips into the receiving flask.

  1. The solution is heated in a flask. The solvent boils first because it has the lower boiling point, and leaves as a vapour. The solute stays in the flask.
  2. The vapour passes into the condenser — a tube with cold water flowing through an outer jacket, entering at the bottom so the jacket stays full.
  3. The condenser cools the vapour, so it condenses back to a liquid, and the pure solvent — the distillate — runs into the receiving flask.
  4. The thermometer bulb sits level with the side arm, so it reads the temperature of the vapour, not the liquid.

2024 Edexcel 1C Q4(b)(ii) asked what happens to the ethanol vapour in the condenser, for two marks, and both marks are separate:

"M1 (the condenser/X) cools the (ethanol) vapour" and "M2 so it condenses OR forms liquid (ethanol)".

✗ "It condenses." — That is M2 only. You dropped the mark for saying it was cooled.
✓ "The condenser cools the vapour, so it condenses back into a liquid."

Paper chromatography — reading the chromatogram

Spec 1.11, 1.13 · 2025 Edexcel 1C Q3 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q5 · 2021 Edexcel 1C Q4

The dyes separate because they have different solubilities in the solvent: the most soluble dye travels furthest up the paper.

The method, with every detail that carries a mark:

  1. Draw the start line in pencil, not ink.
  2. Put a small spot of each sample on the start line.
  3. Stand the paper in a beaker with a shallow depth of solvent, so the start line is above the level of the solvent.
  4. Leave it until the solvent front is near the top, then take the paper out and mark the solvent front in pencil straight away, before it dries and disappears.

Then read it:

What you seeWhat it means
One spot for a sampleThat sample contains one dye — it is a pure substance
Two or more spotsA mixture, of that many dyes
Spots from two samples at the same heightThey contain the same dye — same height means same Rf
A spot that has not moved from the start lineThat dye is insoluble in the solvent, so you cannot tell how many dyes it contains
The spot that travelled furthestThe most soluble dye

Three mark schemes, three places the marks go:

Why pencil? 2025 Edexcel 1C Q3(a): "ink would dissolve in the solvent" and "ink would travel / run / produce spots which would interfere with the results" — the scheme also allows "unable to calculate Rf value". Two separate marks: pencil is insoluble, and ink would run and spoil the chromatogram.

Why above the solvent? 2024 Edexcel 1C Q5(a)(i): "They will not dissolve/diffuse into the solvent" and "so that the dyes can travel up the paper". If the spots are under the surface the dyes wash straight off into the beaker.

The insoluble-spot trap. 2025 Q3(c) and 2024 Q5(a)(iii) are the same question twice. A sample that has not moved looks like "one spot", but the scheme says you can only be certain about the one that did move: the stationary one is insoluble, "so you cannot tell how many dyes it has".

Rf values

Spec 1.12 · 2025 Edexcel 1C Q3(d) · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q5(b)

Rf is how far the spot went as a fraction of how far the solvent went, measured from the start line both times.

Rf value — memoriseRf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solventboth distances measured from the start line, in the same unit · the spot is measured to its centre · Rf has no unit and is always less than 1
  1. Measure from the start line to the solvent front, in mm. This is the bottom of the fraction, and it is the same for every spot on that chromatogram.
  2. Measure from the start line to the centre of the spot you were asked about, in mm.
  3. Divide. Both measurements earn a mark of their own before the division does.
  4. Round to whatever the question demands, and only at the end.

Worked example — 2025 Edexcel 1C Q3(d), four marks

Start line to solvent front = 6.9 cm  ·  start line to the centre of spot W = 2.2 cm

Rf = 2.2 ÷ 6.9 = 0.3188…

The question asked for two significant figures, so Rf = 0.32

The marks were one for each measurement, one for the correct fraction written down, and one for the answer — so write the fraction out even if the arithmetic goes wrong, and the scheme carries your figures forward.

Worked example — 2024 Edexcel 1C Q5(b), three marks

Baseline to solvent level = 65 mm  ·  baseline to the dye in G = 39 mm

Rf = 39 ÷ 65 = 0.6

The scheme accepted anything from 0.57 to 0.64, because reading a ruler against a printed spot is not exact — but it would not award the final mark for a value incorrectly rounded.

✗ Measuring to the top or bottom edge of the spot. Measure to its centre.
✗ Measuring the solvent front from the bottom of the paper, or from the surface of the solvent. Measure from the start line, the same place you measure the spot from.
✗ Giving an Rf greater than 1. The spot cannot overtake the solvent carrying it, so a value above 1 means the two distances were swapped.
✓ Same start line, spot centre, divide, and no unit.

04Understanding it

U1

A saturated solution of KNO₃ at 70 °C sits in an open beaker overnight and cools to 20 °C. Describe everything that is different in the morning, with the reasoning.

Show answer

Crystals of KNO₃ now sit at the bottom. At 70 °C the water held the full 70-degree solubility; at 20 °C it can hold far less. The surplus had nowhere to go but out of solution — it crystallised.

The remaining liquid is still saturated — but at 20 °C, holding the 20-degree amount. The mass of crystals = (solubility at 70°) − (solubility at 20°), scaled to the actual mass of water. That subtraction is the exam question, every year.

U2

2025's table gave the mass dissolving in 25 cm³ of water, and the answer line demanded "g per 100 g of water". What conversion is needed, and why does the question feel entitled to swap between cm³ and g of water without comment?

Show answer

Multiply the reading by 4 — four lots of 25 make 100.

The silent swap works because 1.00 cm³ of water has a mass of 1.00 g — the density of water is the one conversion factor the papers assume without stating (2024 printed it as a hint; 2025 didn't). So 25 cm³ of water is 25 g of water, and volume-of-water numbers slot straight into solubility units.

It only works for water — never apply it to another liquid or to the solution itself.

U3

Why are solubility values measured at 95 °C systematically less accurate than those at 25 °C — and is the error high or low?

Show answer

Near boiling, the water evaporates significantly while the measurement is being made. Less water is present than you think.

The same mass of dissolved salt sitting in less water than recorded means the calculated "per 100 g" figure comes out too high — the salt gets credited to water that has already left. (The one-mark exam answer is just "water evaporates"; the direction of the error is the understanding behind it.)

U4

In the evaporating-basin practical you heat to complete dryness — the exact step the salt-preparation module forbids. Why is it right here and wrong there?

Show answer

Different goal, different technique. Here the product is a number: the total mass of dissolved salt. Evaporating everything off and weighing the solid residue measures exactly that — you want every last gram out of solution, and you don't care what the crystals look like.

In salt preparation the product is the substance: well-formed, hydrated crystals. There, heating to dryness wrecks the crystals and drives off the water of crystallisation.

If an exam answer needs one sentence: evaporate to dryness to measure solubility; heat only to the crystallisation point to prepare crystals.

U5

Two curves cross at 68 °C. A student answers "at 68 °C the solutions are the same" and scores one mark of two. What exactly did the second mark want?

Show answer

The construction line on the graph. The schemes award a mark for visible working again and again: the line dropped from the crossing in 2023 Q12 and 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q3, and the readings for the crystallisation subtraction in 2024 and 2025.

The habit that banks these marks: for any value read from a graph, rule the lines first, read second, and leave the lines on the paper. It also silently protects you — an answer read from the wrong place with lines shown can still earn the method mark.

And "the solutions are the same" is loose wording. Say the solubilities are equal at 68 °C.

U6

A solution contains 40 g of solid X in 100 g of water at 70 °C. The solubility of X is 60 g per 100 g at 70 °C, 40 g at 45 °C and 25 g at 20 °C. Is the solution saturated at 70 °C? What happens as it cools to 20 °C?

Show answer

Not saturated at 70 °C. The water could hold 60 g but holds only 40 g, so the point sits below the curve. Another 20 g could still dissolve.

As it cools, nothing visible happens at first. At 45 °C the solubility has fallen to 40 g — exactly what is dissolved — so the solution becomes saturated. That is where crystals first appear.

Below 45 °C crystals keep forming. At 20 °C the water holds 25 g, so 40 − 25 = 15 g has crystallised. Notice it is 40 − 25, not 60 − 25: you subtract from what was actually dissolved, not from the 70 °C solubility.

U7

Sugar stirred into tea disappears much faster than sugar left unstirred. A student concludes that stirring increases the solubility of sugar. Why is that wrong?

Show answer

Stirring changes the rate of dissolving, not the amount that can dissolve. It brings fresh water to the surface of the crystals, so they dissolve sooner.

Solubility is the maximum mass that dissolves in 100 g of water at that temperature. Leave the unstirred cup long enough and exactly the same maximum is reached. Only changing the temperature changes that maximum.

This is why the 2024 scheme accepted "temperature" as a factor affecting solubility and ignored "stirring".

U8

A student measures the solubility of copper(II) sulfate but heats the evaporating basin strongly until the blue solid turns white. Explain what has gone wrong and how it affects the result.

Show answer

The blue solid is hydrated copper(II) sulfate: its crystals contain water of crystallisation. Strong heating drove that water off and left white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate.

So the solid weighed at the end is lighter than the salt that was dissolved. A smaller mass of solid gives a solubility that is too low.

The fix is in the word the method uses: heat gently, just enough to evaporate the water from the solution. 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q3(b)(iv) gave its mark for "copper(II) sulfate would lose water / become anhydrous".

D3

Show answer

(a) Mass of salt = 78.2 − 50.5 = 27.7 g (in 50 g of water) 1; solubility = 27.7 × 2 = 55.4 g per 100 g of water 1

D4

These steps for measuring solubility are jumbled: A — gently heat the basin to remove all the water; B — filter to obtain 50.0 cm³ of saturated solution; C — record the mass of basin and salt solution; D — record the mass of the empty basin; E — record the mass of basin and dry salt; F — put 50.0 cm³ of water into a beaker; G — add salt, stirring, until no more dissolves; H — pour the solution into the basin.

Write the eight letters in the correct order. (2)

Show answer

F → G → B → D → H → C → A → E: G in the correct place 1; H, C and A in the correct places 1

The logic: make the saturated solution (F, G), separate it (B), weigh empty before filling (D), then solution (H, C), then evaporate and weigh dry (A, E). The basin must be weighed empty before anything goes in it.
