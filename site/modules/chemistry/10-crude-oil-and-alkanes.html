02How it actually works

Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons — compounds of hydrogen and carbon only. Everything in this module is sorting that mixture (distillation), naming its members (alkanes, isomers), and burning them (combustion and its pollution).

The fractionating column — the four-marker to memorise

Spec 4.7, 4.8 · 2023 Q11 · 2024 Q9a · 2025 Q8a — three years running

Crude oil is separated into fractions — mixtures of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points — in a fractionating column (the equipment; "fractional distillation" is the process — 2022 asked for the equipment and ignored the process name). The four mark points, in order:

The four-mark script

1. The crude oil is heated / vaporised (before entering the column) 1

2. The vapours rise up the column 1

3. The column is hotter at the bottom than the top (a temperature gradient) 1

4. Each vapour rises until it condenses — at the height where the column temperature drops below its boiling point 1

The two marks that die: forgetting the vapours rise, and saying "turns to liquid" without tying it to the boiling point.

The fractions, top to bottom — refinery gases · gasoline (petrol) · kerosene (paraffin) · diesel · fuel oil · bitumen — with uses: bottled/camping gas · car fuel · jet fuel · lorry fuel · ships and power stations · road surfacing. Going down the column: molecules get longer, boiling point rises, colour darkens, viscosity rises (bitumen is the most viscous; refinery gases boil lowest — 2021's two easy marks).

Why longer molecules boil higher

Spec 4.10 · 2024 Q9b

Same story as the bonding module, applied to alkanes: longer chains → stronger intermolecular forces between the molecules → more energy needed to separate them → higher boiling point. Three marks in 2024: longer chains · stronger forces between molecules · more energy to overcome. The scheme rejected "forces between atoms" and gave nothing if covalent bonds were "broken".

The alkanes — family, formulae, isomers

Spec 4.1 – 4.5, 4.19 – 4.21 · 2023 Q8 · 2024 Q4

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons — single C–C bonds only — with general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂: methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈, butane C₄H₁₀, pentane C₅H₁₂. A homologous series: same general formula, similar reactions, each member differing by CH₂, boiling points rising steadily.

The five ways to write a molecule (2024's butane table): name butane · molecular C₄H₁₀ · empirical C₂H₅ (simplest ratio) · structural CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ · displayed — every atom and every bond drawn, including all C–H bonds. The mark schemes reject displayed formulae with –CH₃ written as a lump.

Isomers: compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural/displayed formulae. Say "molecular formula" — "same chemical formula" lost the mark in 2023. The set pieces:

  • C₄H₁₀: butane (straight chain) and methylpropane (three-carbon chain with a CH₃ branch on the middle carbon).
  • C₅H₁₂: pentane, 2-methylbutane, 2,2-dimethylpropane — the exam gives pentane and asks for the other two.

Drawing check: count the carbons, count the hydrogens, confirm every carbon has exactly four bonds — and make sure your two "isomers" aren't the same molecule bent differently.

Combustion — complete, incomplete, and the poison

Spec 4.11 – 4.13 · 2024 Q4d · 2025 Q9

A fuel releases heat energy when burned. In plenty of air, hydrocarbons burn completely to carbon dioxide and water — the 2025 cohort's favourite error was writing "hydrogen" instead of water. Balanced set piece: 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O; methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.

In limited air/oxygen, combustion is incomplete: carbon monoxide (and soot) form. CO is poisonous because it reduces the capacity of the blood to carry oxygen — the spec's own wording; mention haemoglobin only if you also mention oxygen (2025's scheme insisted).

The pollution chains — say every link

Spec 4.14 – 4.16 · 2023 Q6a · 2025 Q8b

Two chains, each marked link by link, each ruined by skipping a step:

  • Sulfur: hydrocarbon fuels contain sulfur impurities → burning gives sulfur dioxide, SO₂ → dissolves in rain → acid rain (kills fish, damages limestone buildings). 2025: the acid-rain mark was dependent on the first two — jumping straight to "acid rain" scored 0, "sulphur oxide" was rejected (name it: dioxide), and greenhouse-gas talk is the wrong chain entirely.
  • Nitrogen: in a car engine the temperature is so high that nitrogen and oxygen from the air react → oxides of nitrogen (NOₓ) → also acid rain. The nitrogen comes from the air, not the fuel — that distinction is the mark.

Substitution — methane meets bromine

Spec 4.22 · 2021 Q9c · 2024 Q4c · 2025 Q9d — every other year

Alkanes are saturated, so they cannot add anything — a halogen must swap in for a hydrogen:

CH₄ + Br₂ → CH₃Br + HBr, needing ultraviolet light (accept sunlight; heat is ignored — UV is the answer). The organic product is bromomethane; the other product is HBr — written exactly so, capital H, capital B, lower-case r. The reaction type is substitution — one atom substituted for another — not displacement (that's metals), not addition (that needs a double bond), not reduction. Mono-substitution only at this level.

Cracking — on the checklist, due an appearance

Spec 4.17, 4.18 · in the school checklist; not yet on a paper — which makes it a fair bet

Demand for short fractions (petrol) outstrips supply; long fractions are in surplus. Catalytic cracking breaks long alkanes into shorter alkanes + alkenes, using a silica or alumina catalyst at 600–700 °C. Why: to match supply to demand for the different fractions (and the alkenes feed plastics production). A typical cracking equation just conserves atoms: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄.

04Understanding it

U1

In the fractionating column, why does bitumen leave at the bottom and refinery gas at the top — walk the physics from "heated crude enters" to both exits.

Show answer

The heated crude enters as a mixture of vapours (all but the heaviest, which stay liquid — bitumen drains straight out at the hot bottom). The vapours rise into progressively cooler regions. A long-chain compound has a high boiling point — the column temperature falls below it almost immediately, so it condenses low down (fuel oil, diesel). Short chains have low boiling points — the column never gets cold enough until near the top (gasoline), and the shortest (refinery gases, bp below room temperature) never condense at all and leave as gas.

Every fraction is executing the same rule — rise until your boiling point beats the local temperature — and the exits are just where that happens for each chain length.

U2

Butane and methylpropane are isomers. State three properties they share and one that differs, and explain why the difference exists.

Show answer

Shared: molecular formula (C₄H₁₀), Mr (58), and general chemistry (both saturated alkanes — same combustion, same substitution reactions).

Different: boiling point. Butane's straight chain lets molecules lie alongside each other, giving more contact and stronger intermolecular forces; branched methylpropane packs less closely, weaker forces, so it boils lower (−12 °C vs −1 °C).

The principle: molecular formula fixes the chemistry; the shape fixes the physics. That is what "same molecular formula, different structure" means in practice.

U3

Why does a Bunsen flame burn blue with the air-hole open and yellow-sooty with it closed — and why is the closed-hole flame the dangerous one indoors?

Show answer

Open hole: plenty of air → complete combustion → CO₂ + H₂O, maximum energy, clean blue flame.

Closed hole: limited oxygen → incomplete combustion → carbon monoxide and unburned carbon (the yellow glow and the soot are hot carbon particles).

The danger: the CO. It binds in the blood and reduces its capacity to transport oxygen — and it is colourless and odourless, so nothing warns you. Every gas appliance question (boilers, heaters in sealed rooms) is this chemistry wearing different clothes.

U4

Acid rain questions offer two different chains — sulfur and nitrogen. For each, state where the element comes from, and identify the step students most often skip.

Show answer

Sulfur chain: the sulfur is an impurity in the fuel itself. Burning it gives SO₂; SO₂ + rain → acid rain. The skipped step: saying the fuel contains sulfur — 2025 made the acid-rain mark dependent on it, so answers that opened with "SO₂ is released" had already lost M1 and with it M3.

Nitrogen chain: the nitrogen is from the air, not the fuel. Car-engine temperatures are high enough to make nitrogen and oxygen react → oxides of nitrogen → acid rain. The skipped step: the high engine temperature — without it there is no reason inert N₂ would react.

And keep the chains apart from CO₂/greenhouse: acid rain and global warming are different questions with different gases.

U5

Why do alkanes react with bromine by substitution rather than addition, and why does the reaction only start in ultraviolet light?

Show answer

Substitution, not addition: alkanes are saturated — every carbon already has four single bonds, so there is nowhere for extra atoms to add. The only way in is to swap: a bromine atom replaces a hydrogen, giving CH₃Br and the displaced hydrogen leaves in HBr. (Addition belongs to alkenes, which have a C=C double bond to open up.)

The UV: the light provides the energy to split Br₂ and start the reaction — in the dark, methane and bromine sit together indefinitely. Exam-level answer: "the reaction requires ultraviolet light" — with heat explicitly ignored by the scheme.

U6

An oil company cracks decane (C₁₀H₂₂) into octane and one other product. Write the equation, identify the other product's family, and explain why the company bothers.

Show answer

C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄ — atoms conserved: 10 = 8 + 2 carbons, 22 = 18 + 4 hydrogens.

C₂H₄ (ethene) fits CₙH₂ₙ — an alkene, unsaturated. Cracking a saturated alkane must produce at least one double-bonded product because hydrogens are conserved but the fragments need fewer of them.

Why: demand and supply are mismatched — customers want petrol-length molecules, the ground supplies too many long ones. Cracking (silica/alumina catalyst, 600–700 °C) converts surplus long fractions into short ones — and the alkene by-product feeds polymer manufacture.
