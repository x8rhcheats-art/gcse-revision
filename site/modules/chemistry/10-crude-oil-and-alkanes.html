02How it actually works

Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons — compounds of hydrogen and carbon only. Everything in this module is sorting that mixture (distillation), naming its members (alkanes, isomers), breaking them up (cracking) and burning them (combustion and its pollution).

Hydrocarbons and crude oil

Spec 4.1, 4.7 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q1(a)(i) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q7(a)

A hydrocarbon is a compound of hydrogen and carbon only, and crude oil is a mixture of lots of them.

Two words carry the marks. Compound — the hydrogen and carbon are chemically bonded, not just mixed. Only — the moment a molecule contains an oxygen or a bromine atom, it is no longer a hydrocarbon. 2023 Jan showed a table of structures and asked which was not a hydrocarbon: the answer was the one with an –OH group, because it contains oxygen.

Crude oil is a mixture, not a compound. Its hydrocarbons are not bonded to each other, so they keep their own boiling points — and that is exactly what lets us separate them.

✗ "A hydrocarbon contains hydrogen and carbon." — misses only: glucose contains hydrogen and carbon too.
✗ "Crude oil is a hydrocarbon." — it is a mixture of hydrocarbons.
✓ "A hydrocarbon is a compound of hydrogen and carbon only."

Fractional distillation — the four-marker to memorise

Spec 4.8 · 2023 Q11 · 2024 Q9a · 2025 Q8a · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q4(a) · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q3(b)

Heat the crude oil so it boils, let the vapours rise up a column that gets cooler towards the top, and each hydrocarbon condenses at the height where the column is cooler than its boiling point.

Crude oil is separated into fractions — groups of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points. The equipment is the fractionating column; the process is fractional distillation. 2022 asked for the equipment and ignored the process name, so read which one the question wants. The chain, in order:

  1. The crude oil is heated until most of it is vaporised (turned into gas).
  2. The vapours enter the bottom of the column and rise up it.
  3. The column is hotter at the bottom and cooler at the top — a temperature gradient.
  4. Each vapour keeps rising until it reaches a height where the column temperature is below its boiling point.
  5. There it condenses back to a liquid and is tapped off as a fraction.
  6. Short molecules (low boiling points) get near the top; long molecules (high boiling points) condense low down; the shortest leave the top as gas, and bitumen drains from the bottom.

The four-mark script

1. The crude oil is heated / vaporised (before entering the column) 1

2. The vapours rise up the column 1

3. The column is hotter at the bottom than the top (a temperature gradient) 1

4. Each vapour rises until it condenses — at the height where the column temperature drops below its boiling point 1

The two marks that die: forgetting the vapours rise, and saying "turns to liquid" without tying it to the boiling point.

✗ "The crude oil is separated by boiling point." — true, but it describes nothing. No heating, no rising, no condensing: 0 of 4.
✗ "The fractions turn back into liquid at the top." — no link to boiling point, and most don't reach the top.
✓ "The vapours rise up the column, which is cooler at the top, and each condenses where the temperature falls below its boiling point."

✗ "Bitumen has the lowest boiling point because it is at the bottom." — the bottom is the hottest part; bitumen has the highest.
✗ "Kerosene is used for cars." — cars use gasoline; kerosene is aircraft fuel.
✓ "Refinery gases have the lowest boiling point; bitumen is the most viscous."

Why longer molecules boil higher

Spec 4.10 · 2024 Q9b

Longer molecules have stronger forces between the molecules, so more energy is needed to pull them apart.

Boiling separates whole molecules from each other. It does not break the covalent bonds inside them. The chain:

  1. A longer hydrocarbon molecule has more atoms and more surface in contact with its neighbours.
  2. So the intermolecular forces between the molecules are stronger.
  3. So more energy is needed to overcome them.
  4. So the boiling point is higher.

The three-mark script (2024)

1. Fraction B has longer chains / larger molecules 1

2. Stronger intermolecular forces between the molecules 1

3. More energy needed to overcome them 1

✗ "Stronger forces between the atoms." — rejected: atoms are held by covalent bonds, which are not broken.
✗ "More covalent bonds need to be broken." — the scheme gave no marks at all for this.
✓ "Longer molecules have stronger intermolecular forces, so more energy is needed to overcome them."

Homologous series and the alkanes

Spec 4.3, 4.4, 4.19, 4.20 · 2021 Q9 · 2022 Q11b · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q5(a) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q4(a)(i)

The alkanes are a family of saturated hydrocarbons that all fit the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.

General formula of the alkanes — memoriseCₙH₂ₙ₊₂n = number of carbon atoms · double it and add two to get the hydrogens

A homologous series is a family of compounds like this. Two real Edexcel papers asked for two characteristics. Pick any two:

  • the same general formula
  • the same functional group
  • similar chemical properties (they react in the same ways)
  • a trend in physical properties, such as boiling point rising with chain length
  • each member differs from the next by CH₂

A functional group is the atom or group of atoms that decides how a compound reacts. For alkenes it is the C=C double bond.

Alkanes are saturated: they contain only single carbon–carbon bonds, so every carbon already has its full four bonds and nothing more can add on.

Naming — the start of the name counts the carbons; the ending -ane means alkane:

CarbonsPrefixAlkaneFormula

2eth-ethaneC₂H₆
3prop-propaneC₃H₈
4but-butaneC₄H₁₀
5pent-pentaneC₅H₁₂
6hex-hexaneC₆H₁₄

✗ "Triane" for C₃H₈. — 2023: "spelling must be correct". Prefixes are meth, eth, prop, but, pent.

Isomers — a step-by-step method for drawing and naming

Spec 4.3, 4.5, 4.21 · 2023 Q8 · 2024 Q4b · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q5(a) · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q8(a)(i) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q7(e) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q8(a)(iv)

Isomers have the same molecular formula but a different arrangement of atoms — the same atoms, joined up differently.

The two-mark definition — asked on six papers

1. Compounds with the same molecular formula 1

2. but different structural / displayed formulae 1

"Same chemical formula" lost M1 in 2023. Say molecular.

The method for drawing every isomer of an alkane:

  1. Draw the longest chain — all the carbons in one straight line. That is the unbranched isomer (butane, pentane).
  2. Shorten the chain by one carbon and attach that carbon as a CH₃ branch to a middle carbon — never to an end carbon (that just makes the long chain again).
  3. If carbons remain, shorten again and use two branches — on the same middle carbon, or on different ones.
  4. Add hydrogens so every carbon has exactly four bonds.
  5. Check the count: same number of C and H as the molecular formula.
  6. Check it is new: find the longest continuous carbon chain in your drawing. If it matches one you already have, it is the same molecule bent round, not an isomer.
  7. Name it: the longest chain gives the name (but-, pent-); each branch is "methyl"; a number says which carbon it is on.

Worked example — the isomers of C₅H₁₂

Five in a row: CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ — pentane

Four in a row + one branch on carbon 2: CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ — 2-methylbutane

Three in a row + two branches both on the middle carbon: C(CH₃)₄ — 2,2-dimethylpropane

Check: each has 5 C and 12 H. There are exactly three. For C₄H₁₀ the same method gives two: butane and methylpropane, CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃.

The spec only requires you to name the unbranched isomers (butane, pentane), but the branched names above are what the mark schemes use, so learn them.

✗ Drawing pentane with a bend in it and calling it an isomer. — Bending a chain does not change it. Trace the longest chain: still five.
✗ A CH₃ branch on the end carbon of a four-carbon chain. — That is just pentane again.
✗ A carbon with five bonds. — Count every carbon: exactly four lines.
✓ Longest chain first, then shorten by one and branch in the middle, then check counts and chains.

Fuels and complete combustion

Spec 4.6, 4.11, 4.12 · 2024 Q4d · 2025 Q9a–b

A fuel releases heat energy when it burns; with plenty of oxygen, a hydrocarbon burns completely to carbon dioxide and water.

Complete combustion — memorisehydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + waterneeds plenty of air · the carbon goes to CO₂, the hydrogen goes to H₂O

Every carbon atom in the fuel ends up in a CO₂ molecule, and every pair of hydrogen atoms ends up in an H₂O molecule. The reaction type is combustion — one of the three the spec asks you to recognise, with substitution and addition.

✗ "Methane burns to give carbon dioxide and hydrogen." — 2025: "Lots of students said hydrogen instead of water".
✓ "Complete combustion gives carbon dioxide and water."

Balancing combustion equations — worked method

Spec 4.12 · 2024 Q4d · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q8(d), (e)(i)

Balance carbon first, then hydrogen, and oxygen last — because oxygen is the only thing on the left you can freely change.

  1. Write the formulae: fuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.
  2. Carbon: put the number of C atoms in the fuel in front of CO₂.
  3. Hydrogen: halve the number of H atoms in the fuel and put it in front of H₂O.
  4. Oxygen: count the O atoms on the right. Halve it for the number in front of O₂.
  5. If that gives a half (e.g. 3½ O₂), double everything.
  6. Check each element on both sides.

Worked example 1 — propane, C₃H₈

Carbon: 3 C → 3CO₂

Hydrogen: 8 H → 4H₂O

Oxygen on the right: (3 × 2) + (4 × 1) = 10 O atoms → 5O₂

C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Worked example 2 — ethane, C₂H₆ (the one that needs doubling)

Carbon: 2 C → 2CO₂

Hydrogen: 6 H → 3H₂O

Oxygen on the right: (2 × 2) + 3 = 7 O atoms → 3½ O₂ — a half, so double everything

2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O — the 2024 school paper's equation

Worked example 3 — working backwards from masses (2024 Edexcel 1C Q8(d) style)

1 mol of an alkane burns completely and makes 396 g of CO₂ and 180 g of H₂O.

Moles of CO₂ = 396 ÷ 44 = 9 → 9 carbon atoms in the fuel

Moles of H₂O = 180 ÷ 18 = 10 → 20 hydrogen atoms in the fuel, so it is C₉H₂₀ (fits CₙH₂ₙ₊₂)

Oxygen on the right: (9 × 2) + 10 = 28 O atoms → 14O₂

C₉H₂₀ + 14O₂ → 9CO₂ + 10H₂O

✗ "C₂H₆ + 3½O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O" — accepted by some schemes, but whole numbers are safer. Double it.
✗ Changing a formula to balance, e.g. writing CO₃. — Never change the small numbers, only the big ones in front.
✓ C, then H, then O; double if you get a half; check every element.

Incomplete combustion and carbon monoxide

Spec 4.12, 4.13 · 2025 Q9c · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q8(e)

With too little oxygen, some carbon ends up as poisonous carbon monoxide or as soot instead of carbon dioxide.

In a limited supply of air, there is not enough oxygen to turn all the carbon into CO₂. The hydrogen still becomes water. The possible products are:

  • carbon monoxide, CO — a colourless, odourless, poisonous gas
  • carbon, C — soot, the black smoke and yellow flame
  • water, and usually some carbon dioxide as well
Incomplete combustion of methane2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂Obalance the same way: C, then H, then O

Worked example — octane, C₈H₁₈ (2024 Edexcel 1C Q8(e))

Products given: 5CO, 3C and water.

Carbon: 5 + 3 = 8 ✓

Hydrogen: 18 H → 9H₂O

Oxygen on the right: 5 (in CO) + 9 (in H₂O) = 14 O atoms → 7O₂

C₈H₁₈(l) + 7O₂(g) → 5CO(g) + 3C(s) + 9H₂O(l) — one mark for balancing, one for state symbols

Why CO is poisonous: it reduces the capacity of the blood to transport oxygen. That is the spec's own wording. You may add that it combines with haemoglobin in red blood cells — but only alongside the oxygen point.

✗ "CO is poisonous because it is toxic." — just repeats the question.
✗ "CO binds to haemoglobin." — on its own, 2025's scheme wanted oxygen mentioned.
✓ "Carbon monoxide reduces the capacity of the blood to carry oxygen."

Acid rain — sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen

Spec 4.14, 4.15, 4.16 · 2023 Q6a · 2025 Q8b · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q3(c) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q7(g)

Burning fuels releases sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen, which dissolve in rainwater to make acid rain.

Two chains. Each is marked link by link, and skipping a link loses the marks after it.

The sulfur chain:

  1. Some hydrocarbon fuels contain sulfur as an impurity.
  2. When the fuel burns, the sulfur reacts with oxygen to form sulfur dioxide, SO₂.
  3. SO₂ dissolves in rainwater, making it acidic.
  4. This is acid rain — it kills fish and trees and damages limestone buildings.

The nitrogen chain:

  1. Nitrogen and oxygen from the air are drawn into a car engine.
  2. The engine reaches a high enough temperature for them to react.
  3. They form oxides of nitrogen (NOₓ).
  4. These dissolve in rainwater to form nitric acid — acid rain.

The three-mark script — sulfur (2025, 2022 Edexcel 1C)

1. The fuel contains sulfur (impurity) 1

2. Burning produces sulfur dioxide 1

3. which dissolves in rain to cause acid rain 1 — dependent on the first two

✗ "Sulphur oxide causes acid rain." — rejected in 2025. Name it: sulfur dioxide.
✗ "The nitrogen comes from the petrol." — it comes from the air.
✗ "CO₂ is a greenhouse gas, causing global warming." — the wrong question. "Lots spoke about greenhouse gases instead."
✓ "The fuel contains sulfur, which burns to sulfur dioxide, which dissolves in rain to form acid rain."

Cracking — making supply match demand

Spec 4.17, 4.18 · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q3(c) · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q3(b) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q4(b)(i)

Cracking breaks long-chain alkanes, which nobody wants enough of, into shorter alkanes and alkenes, which are in demand.

Crude oil contains more long-chain hydrocarbons than we need and fewer short ones (for petrol) than we want. Catalytic cracking fixes the mismatch:

  1. Take a long-chain alkane from a fraction in surplus, e.g. fuel oil.
  2. Heat it to vaporise it, and pass the vapour over a hot catalyst of silica or alumina at 600–700 °C.
  3. The long molecules break into shorter-chain alkanes and alkenes.
  4. The short alkanes are used as petrol; the alkenes are used to make polymers (plastics).
Cracking decaneC₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄decane → octane (alkane) + ethene (alkene) · catalyst silica or alumina, 600–700 °C

Worked example — finding the missing product

C₁₄H₃₀ → C₁₀H₂₂ + 2 ____

Carbons left: 14 − 10 = 4, shared between 2 molecules → 2 C each

Hydrogens left: 30 − 22 = 8, shared between 2 molecules → 4 H each

Missing product: C₂H₄ (ethene). Check: 10 + 4 = 14 C, 22 + 8 = 30 H ✓

You can spot the alkene: it fits CₙH₂ₙ (twice as many H as C), not CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Cracking always makes at least one, because there are not enough hydrogens to make every fragment saturated.

Why cracking is necessary — the 2024 Edexcel 2C script (any three)

1. There is a surplus of long-chain fractions / not enough supply of short-chain fractions to meet demand 1

2. Alkenes are produced, which are needed to make polymers 1

3. Shorter alkanes are needed for petrol 1

✗ "Cracking separates crude oil into fractions." — that is fractional distillation. Cracking breaks molecules.
✗ "Catalyst: iron, 450 °C." — that is the Haber process. Cracking is silica or alumina, 600–700 °C.
✓ "Long-chain alkanes are broken down into shorter alkanes and alkenes, to match supply with demand."

Substitution — alkanes with halogens

Spec 4.6, 4.22 · 2021 Q9c · 2024 Q4c · 2025 Q9d · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q7(f) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q8(b) · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q5(b)

In ultraviolet light, one halogen atom swaps in for one hydrogen atom on an alkane — a substitution reaction.

Alkanes are saturated, so nothing can add on. The only way a bromine atom can get in is to replace a hydrogen:

  1. UV light gives the energy to start the reaction.
  2. One Br atom replaces one H atom on the alkane.
  3. The organic product is a bromoalkane.
  4. The removed H joins the other Br atom as hydrogen bromide, HBr.
Methane + bromineCH₄ + Br₂ → CH₃Br + HBrneeds ultraviolet light · bromomethane + hydrogen bromide
Any alkane — the patternCₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + Br₂ → CₙH₂ₙ₊₁Br + HBre.g. C₂H₆ → C₂H₅Br (bromoethane) · C₄H₁₀ → C₄H₉Br (2023 Jan Edexcel 1C)

Chlorine works the same way: CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl. At this level only one hydrogen is replaced (mono-substitution).

Classifying reactions — the spec's three types: combustion (burning in oxygen), substitution (one atom swaps for another — alkanes with halogens), addition (atoms add across a C=C double bond — alkenes only, so never an alkane).

✗ "Displacement" or "reduction". — 2025: "lots said displacement or reduction". It is substitution.
✗ "Heat" as the condition. — ignored. The condition is ultraviolet light (sunlight accepted).
✗ "HBR" or "hbr". — rejected. Write HBr.
✓ "CH₄ + Br₂ → CH₃Br + HBr, in UV light — a substitution reaction."

03Cheat sheet

The sentences behind most of the marks

The column: heated oil in at the bottom, vapours rise, hotter below than above, each fraction condenses where the column is cooler than its boiling point. Down the column: longer · hotter · darker · thicker.

Boiling point: longer molecules → stronger intermolecular forces → more energy to overcome.

Isomers: same molecular formula, different structural formulae.

Combustion: plenty of air → CO₂ + water; limited air → CO (and soot). CO reduces the capacity of the blood to carry oxygen.

Acid rain: fuel contains sulfur → SO₂ → dissolves in rain. Engine heat → N₂ + O₂ from air react → oxides of nitrogen → nitric acid.

Cracking: long alkanes → shorter alkanes + alkenes, silica/alumina, 600–700 °C, to match supply and demand.

Substitution: CH₄ + Br₂ → CH₃Br + HBr in UV light.

04Understanding it

U1

In the fractionating column, why does bitumen leave at the bottom and refinery gas at the top — walk the physics from "heated crude enters" to both exits.

Show answer

The heated crude enters as a mixture of vapours (all but the heaviest, which stay liquid — bitumen drains straight out at the hot bottom). The vapours rise into progressively cooler regions. A long-chain compound has a high boiling point — the column temperature falls below it almost immediately, so it condenses low down (fuel oil, diesel). Short chains have low boiling points — the column never gets cold enough until near the top (gasoline), and the shortest (refinery gases, bp below room temperature) never condense at all and leave as gas.

Every fraction is executing the same rule — rise until your boiling point beats the local temperature — and the exits are just where that happens for each chain length.

U2

Butane and methylpropane are isomers. State three properties they share and one that differs, and explain why the difference exists.

Show answer

Shared: molecular formula (C₄H₁₀), Mr (58), and general chemistry (both saturated alkanes — same combustion, same substitution reactions).

Different: boiling point. Butane's straight chain lets molecules lie alongside each other, giving more contact and stronger intermolecular forces; branched methylpropane packs less closely, weaker forces, so it boils lower (−12 °C vs −1 °C).

The principle: molecular formula fixes the chemistry; the shape fixes the physics. That is what "same molecular formula, different structure" means in practice.

U3

Why does a Bunsen flame burn blue with the air-hole open and yellow-sooty with it closed — and why is the closed-hole flame the dangerous one indoors?

Show answer

Open hole: plenty of air → complete combustion → CO₂ + H₂O, maximum energy, clean blue flame.

Closed hole: limited oxygen → incomplete combustion → carbon monoxide and unburned carbon (the yellow glow and the soot are hot carbon particles).

The danger: the CO. It binds in the blood and reduces its capacity to transport oxygen — and it is colourless and odourless, so nothing warns you. Every gas appliance question (boilers, heaters in sealed rooms) is this chemistry wearing different clothes.

U4

Acid rain questions offer two different chains — sulfur and nitrogen. For each, state where the element comes from, and identify the step students most often skip.

Show answer

Sulfur chain: the sulfur is an impurity in the fuel itself. Burning it gives SO₂; SO₂ + rain → acid rain. The skipped step: saying the fuel contains sulfur — 2025 made the acid-rain mark dependent on it, so answers that opened with "SO₂ is released" had already lost M1 and with it M3.

Nitrogen chain: the nitrogen is from the air, not the fuel. Car-engine temperatures are high enough to make nitrogen and oxygen react → oxides of nitrogen → acid rain. The skipped step: the high engine temperature — without it there is no reason unreactive N₂ would react.

And keep the chains apart from CO₂/greenhouse: acid rain and global warming are different questions with different gases.

U5

Why do alkanes react with bromine by substitution rather than addition, and why does the reaction only start in ultraviolet light?

Show answer

Substitution, not addition: alkanes are saturated — every carbon already has four single bonds, so there is nowhere for extra atoms to add. The only way in is to swap: a bromine atom replaces a hydrogen, giving CH₃Br and the displaced hydrogen leaves in HBr. (Addition belongs to alkenes, which have a C=C double bond to open up.)

The UV: the light provides the energy to split Br₂ and start the reaction — in the dark, methane and bromine sit together indefinitely. Exam-level answer: "the reaction requires ultraviolet light" — with heat explicitly ignored by the scheme.

U6

An oil company cracks decane (C₁₀H₂₂) into octane and one other product. Write the equation, identify the other product's family, and explain why the company bothers.

Show answer

C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄ — atoms conserved: 10 = 8 + 2 carbons, 22 = 18 + 4 hydrogens.

C₂H₄ (ethene) fits CₙH₂ₙ — an alkene, unsaturated. Cracking a saturated alkane must produce at least one double-bonded product because hydrogens are conserved but the fragments need fewer of them.

Why: demand and supply are mismatched — customers want petrol-length molecules, the ground supplies too many long ones. Cracking (silica/alumina catalyst, 600–700 °C) converts surplus long fractions into short ones — and the alkene by-product feeds polymer manufacture.

U7

Why is the general formula of the alkanes CₙH₂ₙ₊₂? Work it out from the bonds rather than just learning it.

Show answer

Each carbon makes four bonds, so n carbons have 4n bonds to give out.

In a chain of n carbons there are n − 1 C–C bonds joining them. Each of those uses up one bond from two carbons, so they use 2(n − 1) bonds in total.

Every bond left over goes to a hydrogen: 4n − 2(n − 1) = 4n − 2n + 2 = 2n + 2 hydrogens.

Check with butane: 2 × 4 + 2 = 10, so C₄H₁₀ ✓. Branching does not change the count — a branched chain still has n − 1 C–C bonds — which is why isomers share the same molecular formula.

Put a C=C double bond in and it uses up two more bonds, leaving 2n hydrogens — the alkene formula you see in cracking products.

U8

A student draws pentane as a straight chain, then draws it again with the chain bent into an L-shape, and says these are two isomers. Are they? How can you check any drawing?

Show answer

No — they are the same molecule. Single C–C bonds can rotate, so a chain drawn bent is just the same chain drawn a different way on paper. Nothing about which atom is bonded to which has changed.

The check: put your pencil on one end carbon and trace the longest continuous chain of carbons without lifting. In the L-shape you still go through all five carbons in a row, so it is still pentane.

A genuine isomer has a different connection: in 2-methylbutane the longest chain is only four carbons, with the fifth carbon hanging off carbon 2 as a branch.

This matters because exam questions give pentane and ask for "the other two isomers" — drawing pentane bent scores nothing.
