02How it actually works

This module has two halves. Energetics is about the heat a reaction gives out or takes in: how to measure it, how to calculate it, and where it comes from (bonds). Equilibria is about reactions that go both ways, and how temperature, pressure and catalysts affect them.

Exothermic and endothermic

Spec 3.1 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q2(b)(i)

An exothermic reaction gives out heat energy to the surroundings; an endothermic reaction takes in heat energy from the surroundings.

TypeHeat energyTemperature of surroundingsSign of ΔHExamples
ExothermicGiven outRisesNegativeCombustion, neutralisation, displacement, thermite, respiration
EndothermicTaken inFallsPositiveThermal decomposition, dissolving ammonium nitrate, photosynthesis

The "surroundings" are the water or solution the thermometer is in. When a reaction gives out heat, the water warms up — the chemicals themselves have lost energy. That is why ΔH is negative when the thermometer goes up.

2024 Edexcel 1C Q2(b)(i) mark scheme: heat energy is given out — "IGNORE energy on its own".
✗ "Energy is released."
✓ "Heat (thermal) energy is given out to the surroundings."

Calorimetry 1 — burning a fuel

Spec 3.2, 3.8 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q8 · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q7

Burn a fuel under a copper can of water; the heat that warms the water is the heat the fuel gave out.

  1. Weigh the spirit burner and fuel (with its cap on).
  2. Measure, say, 100 cm³ of water into a copper can and clamp it above the burner.
  3. Record the initial temperature of the water.
  4. Light the wick. Stir the water with the thermometer.
  5. When the temperature has risen by about 20–35 °C, put the flame out (cap on).
  6. Record the final temperature and immediately reweigh the burner. The mass lost is the mass of fuel burned.

Why stir? So the heat is spread evenly through the water and the thermometer reads the true temperature. Why reweigh straight away, with the cap on? The fuel is volatile — it evaporates from the wick, which would make the mass loss too big. Why copper? It is a good conductor, so heat passes quickly into the water.

2022 Edexcel 2C Q7(a): the bottom of the can turns black. That is carbon (soot) from incomplete combustion.
✗ "Copper oxide" or "smoke".
✓ "Carbon."

Calorimetry 2 — reactions in solution

Spec 3.2, 3.8 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q10 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q10

For neutralisation, displacement and dissolving, the reaction happens in the water itself, so you do it in an insulated polystyrene cup with a lid.

  1. Measure the solution (e.g. 25 cm³ of acid) into a polystyrene cup with a measuring cylinder or pipette. Stand the cup in a beaker so it does not tip.
  2. Record its starting temperature.
  3. Add the second reactant — 25 cm³ of alkali, a weighed amount of metal powder, or a weighed amount of a salt.
  4. Put on the lid, stir, and record the highest temperature (or the lowest, for an endothermic change).

The calculation — Q = mcΔT, then ΔH

Spec 3.3, 3.4 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q10(c) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q8(c) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q10(b)

First find the heat energy the water gained, Q; then divide by the moles that reacted and give ΔH in kJ/mol with a sign.

Heat energy changeQ = m × c × ΔTQ in J · m = mass of the WATER or SOLUTION heated, in g (1 cm³ ≈ 1 g) · c = 4.2 J/g/°C · ΔT = temperature change in °C
Molar enthalpy changeΔH = −Q ÷ nQ in kJ (divide J by 1000) · n = moles of the reactant that was used up · negative if the temperature rose
  1. Work out ΔT = final − initial temperature.
  2. Q = m × 4.2 × ΔT, using the mass of water (not the fuel, not the metal).
  3. Find n of the reactant that was completely used up: the fuel burned, or the limiting reactant (the one not in excess).
  4. Divide Q by n, and convert J to kJ (÷ 1000).
  5. Add the sign: temperature went up → negative. Round as the question asks.

Worked example 1 — metal + acid (2024 Edexcel 1C Q10)

40 cm³ of nitric acid rises from 16.0 °C to 32.4 °C when 0.12 g of magnesium reacts. [Aᵣ Mg = 24]

  1. ΔT = 32.4 − 16.0 = 16.4 °C
  2. Q = 40 × 4.2 × 16.4 = 2755 J
  3. n(Mg) = 0.12 ÷ 24 = 0.005 mol
  4. 2755 ÷ 0.005 = 551 000 J/mol = 551 kJ/mol
  5. ΔH = −550 kJ/mol (2 s.f., with the minus sign)

Worked example 2 — combustion (2023 Edexcel 1C Q8)

Burning pentanol heats 100 cm³ of water by 35.0 °C. The burner goes from 90.11 g to 89.75 g. [Mᵣ = 88]

  1. Q = 100 × 4.2 × 35.0 = 14 700 J
  2. Mass burned = 90.11 − 89.75 = 0.36 g, so n = 0.36 ÷ 88 = 0.0041 mol
  3. 14 700 ÷ 0.0041 ≈ 3 600 000 J/mol = 3600 kJ/mol
  4. ΔH = −3600 kJ/mol

Worked example 3 — displacement: choose the limiting reactant

Excess zinc powder is added to 50 cm³ of 0.20 mol/dm³ copper(II) sulfate. The temperature rises by 10.0 °C.

  1. Q = 50 × 4.2 × 10.0 = 2100 J = 2.1 kJ
  2. The zinc is in excess, so use the copper(II) sulfate: n = 0.20 × 50 ÷ 1000 = 0.010 mol
  3. ΔH = −2.1 ÷ 0.010 = −210 kJ/mol

Worked example 4 — dissolving: an endothermic answer

4.0 g of ammonium nitrate (Mᵣ = 80) dissolves in 50 cm³ of water. The temperature falls by 6.0 °C.

  1. Q = 50 × 4.2 × 6.0 = 1260 J = 1.26 kJ
  2. n = 4.0 ÷ 80 = 0.050 mol
  3. The temperature fell, so ΔH is positive: ΔH = 1.26 ÷ 0.050 = +25.2 kJ/mol
Rearranged for the temperature riseΔT = Q ÷ (m × c)2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q10(b)(i): 2880 ÷ (50 × 4.2) = 13.7 °C, then add the starting 21.0 °C → 34.7 °C

✗ Using the final temperature (50.9 °C) instead of the change (35.0 °C).
✗ "+550 kJ/mol" or "550" — 2024's M4 needed "correct sign" and 2 s.f.

Energy level diagrams (triple only)

Spec 3.5C · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q7(d)(ii)

An energy level diagram shows the reactants and products as two horizontal lines, with ΔH as a vertical arrow from the reactants to the products.

  1. Label the vertical axis energy.
  2. Draw the reactants line (e.g. CH₄ + 2O₂) and label it with formulae.
  3. Exothermic: the products line (CO₂ + 2H₂O) is lower. Endothermic: it is higher.
  4. Draw a vertical arrow from the reactants level to the products level and label it ΔH (e.g. −890 kJ/mol).

The arrow therefore points down for an exothermic reaction and up for an endothermic one. The products are lower in an exothermic reaction because the chemicals have lost energy to the surroundings.

2022 Edexcel 2C Q7(d)(ii): "REJECT arrow pointing from products level to reactants level".
✗ An upward arrow for an exothermic reaction.
✓ Arrow starts at the reactants and ends at the products; a double-headed arrow is also accepted.

Reaction profiles — ΔH, Ea and the catalysed curve (triple only)

Spec 3.14C · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q7(c)(ii) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q5(b)

A reaction profile is an energy level diagram with the hump drawn in: the activation energy is the height of the hump above the reactants. Module 7 (Rates of Reaction) explained why a catalyst speeds a reaction up — an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy. Here you just have to draw it.

  1. Reactants line on the left, labelled (e.g. H₂ + Cl₂).
  2. A curve rising to a peak, then falling to the products line on the right, labelled (e.g. 2HCl).
  3. Ea: vertical arrow up from the reactants level to the top of the peak.
  4. ΔH: vertical arrow from the reactants level to the products level — down if exothermic, up if endothermic.
  5. Catalysed curve: starts at the same reactants level, has a lower peak, ends at the same products level.

A catalyst lowers Ea but does not change ΔH — the reactants and products are the same substances with the same energies. For an endothermic reaction, Ea is always bigger than ΔH, because the peak must be above the products.

2024 Edexcel 2C Q7(c)(ii): Ea arrow "REJECT arrow pointing down"; ΔH arrow for this exothermic reaction "REJECT arrow pointing up".
✗ Ea drawn from the products level, or from the axis.
✗ Catalysed curve that ends at a different products level.
✓ Both arrows start at the reactants line; catalysed curve has the same start and end, lower peak.

Bond breaking and bond making (triple only)

Spec 3.6C · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q7(b)(iv) · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q6(b) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q7(b)

Breaking bonds takes in energy (endothermic); making bonds gives out energy (exothermic). ΔH is the difference between the two.

  • If more energy is released making the new bonds than is taken in breaking the old ones → exothermic, ΔH negative.
  • If more energy is taken in breaking bonds than is released making them → endothermic, ΔH positive.
  • If the same bonds are broken and made → ΔH ≈ 0.

Why is it exothermic? — 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q7(b)(iv), two marks

1. Bond breaking requires (takes in) energy and bond making releases energy 1

2. More energy is released making the bonds than is taken in breaking them 1

The ester reaction is the classic ΔH ≈ 0 case. In CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O, one C–O and one O–H bond are broken, and one C–O and one O–H bond are made. Same bonds broken as made → the energy taken in equals the energy given out → ΔH ≈ 0.

✗ "Breaking bonds releases energy." — the single most common error in this topic.
✓ "Breaking bonds takes in energy; making bonds gives out energy."

Bond energy calculations (triple only)

Spec 3.7C · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q7(c)(i) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q7(b) · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q7(d)(i)

Add up the energy of every bond broken in the reactants, add up every bond made in the products, and subtract: broken minus made.

Enthalpy change from bond energiesΔH = (energy of bonds broken) − (energy of bonds made)all in kJ/mol · a negative answer = exothermic · include the sign
  1. Draw or read the displayed formulae so you can count every bond.
  2. Multiply by the numbers in the balanced equation (3N₂H₄ means three molecules).
  3. Total the bonds broken (reactants).
  4. Total the bonds made (products).
  5. ΔH = broken − made. Keep the sign.

Worked example — H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (2024 Edexcel 2C Q7(c)(i))

H–H 436, Cl–Cl 242, H–Cl 431 kJ/mol

  1. Broken: 436 + 242 = 678 kJ
  2. Made: 2 × 431 = 862 kJ
  3. ΔH = 678 − 862 = −184 kJ/mol

The mark scheme: "(+)184 scores 2" — the sign is worth a whole mark.

Worked example — hydrazine, 3N₂H₄ → 4NH₃ + N₂ (2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q7(b))

N–N 158, N–H 391, N≡N 945 kJ/mol. Each N₂H₄ has one N–N and four N–H.

  1. Broken: 3 × 158 + 12 × 391 = 474 + 4692 = 5166 kJ
  2. Made: 4 NH₃ has 12 N–H, plus one N≡N: 12 × 391 + 945 = 5637 kJ
  3. ΔH = 5166 − 5637 = −471 kJ/mol

Worked example — finding an unknown bond energy (2022 Edexcel 2C Q7(d)(i))

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O, ΔH = −890 kJ/mol. O=O 498, C=O 805, O–H 463. Find C–H.

  1. Broken: 4 × (C–H) + 2 × 498 = 4(C–H) + 996
  2. Made: 2 × 805 + 4 × 463 = 3462
  3. 4(C–H) + 996 − 3462 = −890
  4. 4(C–H) = 1576, so C–H = 394 kJ/mol

✗ "made − broken" — gives the right number with the wrong sign.
✗ Forgetting the big numbers in the equation: 2H₂O has four O–H bonds.
✓ Broken − made, every bond counted, sign shown.

Reversible reactions — copper(II) sulfate and ammonium chloride

Spec 3.17, 3.18 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q12(c) · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q5(c)(ii)

A reversible reaction can go in both directions: the products can react to re-form the reactants. The symbol ⇌ shows this.

Dehydration of copper(II) sulfateCuSO₄·5H₂O(s) ⇌ CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l)blue hydrated → white anhydrous on heating · add water: white → blue, and it gets hot
  • Heating blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate drives off the water of crystallisation. It turns white. This direction takes in heat — endothermic.
  • Adding water to white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate turns it blue again and gives out heat — exothermic.
  • That colour change is the test for water: add the liquid to anhydrous (white) copper(II) sulfate — it turns blue.
Heating ammonium chlorideNH₄Cl(s) ⇌ NH₃(g) + HCl(g)white solid at the hot end breaks down · white solid re-forms in the cooler part of the tube

Heat ammonium chloride at the bottom of a test tube. The white solid seems to disappear as it decomposes into two gases. Higher up, where the tube is cooler, the gases recombine and a white solid forms on the glass again.

2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q12(c)(ii): "Max 1 mark if incorrect starting colour".
✗ "Add copper sulfate; it turns blue." — which copper sulfate? Hydrated is already blue.
✓ "Add the liquid to anhydrous copper(II) sulfate, which turns from white to blue."

Dynamic equilibrium (triple only)

Spec 3.19C, 3.20C · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q6(b) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q7(c)(i) · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q5(c)(iii)

In a sealed container a reversible reaction reaches dynamic equilibrium: the forward and reverse reactions go on at the same rate, so the concentrations of reactants and products stay constant.

  1. At the start there are only reactants, so the forward reaction is fast and the reverse is zero.
  2. As reactants are used up, the forward rate falls. As products build up, the reverse rate rises.
  3. Eventually the two rates are equal.
  4. Now products are made exactly as fast as they are used up, so the concentrations stop changing.

Dynamic means both reactions are still happening — it has not stopped. Sealed container (a closed system) matters: if a gas escapes, the reverse reaction cannot happen and equilibrium is never reached.

The definition — two marks, asked three times

1. The forward and reverse (backward) reactions occur at the same rate 1

2. The concentrations of reactants and products remain constant 1

✗ "The concentrations of reactants and products are equal." — rejected in 2022 Jan and 2023 Jan.
✗ "The reaction has stopped."
✓ "The concentrations remain constant (do not change)."

Catalysts do not move the equilibrium (triple only)

Spec 3.21C · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q7(b) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q7(c)(ii)

A catalyst speeds up the forward and the reverse reactions by the same amount, so the position of equilibrium — and the yield — does not change. Equilibrium is just reached sooner.

The catalyst lowers the activation energy of the pathway in both directions (look at the catalysed reaction profile: the peak is lower whether you climb from the left or the right). Both rates rise equally, so they stay equal to each other at the same concentrations.

The catalyst answer — two marks

1. A catalyst increases the rate of the forward and backward reactions 1

2. equally (by the same amount) — so no effect on the position or the yield 1

✗ "The catalyst increases the yield because the reaction is faster."
✗ "A catalyst speeds up the forward reaction." — only half of it.
✓ "No effect on the yield: it increases the rates of the forward and backward reactions equally."

Changing temperature and pressure (triple only)

Spec 3.22C · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q7(c)

Raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium in the endothermic direction; raising the pressure shifts it towards the side with fewer moles of gas. (Lowering them does the opposite.)

Change madeEquilibrium shifts towards…
Temperature increasedThe endothermic direction
Temperature decreasedThe exothermic direction
Pressure increasedThe side with fewer moles of gas
Pressure decreasedThe side with more moles of gas
Catalyst addedNo shift — equilibrium reached sooner

The ΔH given with the equation is for the forward reaction. The reverse reaction has the same size ΔH with the opposite sign.

Worked example — 2023 Edexcel 2C Q7(c)

CH₄(g) + H₂O(g) ⇌ CO(g) + 3H₂(g)   ΔH = +206 kJ/mol

  1. Temperature lowered (700 °C → 600 °C): the forward reaction is endothermic, so equilibrium shifts to the left (the exothermic direction). The yield of hydrogen decreases.
  2. Pressure lowered (5 atm → 4 atm): left side has 1 + 1 = 2 mol of gas, right side 1 + 3 = 4 mol. Lower pressure shifts to more moles of gas — the right. The yield of hydrogen increases.

Each is marked as a pair: the effect on yield 1, then the reason 1. The reason mark depends on the effect being right.

Count only gas moles, from the numbers in front of each formula. If both sides have the same number, as in H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g), pressure has no effect on the position. Raising the temperature or pressure also makes both reactions faster, but that is a rate effect, separate from the position.

✗ "Increasing the temperature favours the forward reaction." — only if the forward reaction is endothermic.
✗ "Fewer molecules on the right" — count moles of gas from the balanced equation.
✓ "Yield decreases, because the forward reaction is endothermic, so equilibrium shifts to the left."

04Understanding it

U1

In the pentanol experiment, 0.36 g of fuel heated 100 g of water. Why does Q = mcΔT use 100 g and not 0.36 g?

Show answer

Q = mcΔT measures the heat that went into something whose temperature you measured. The thermometer is in the water, and c = 4.2 J/g/°C is the specific heat capacity of water. So m must be the mass of the water.

The fuel comes in later, when you divide by its moles to get the energy per mole. Mass of water goes into Q; mass of fuel goes into n.

U2

The temperature of the solution goes up, yet ΔH is negative. Why is that not a contradiction?

Show answer

ΔH describes the energy of the chemicals, not the water. The reacting chemicals lose energy, and it goes into the water as heat. The water gets hotter because the chemicals got lower in energy.

On an energy level diagram that is why the products line is below the reactants for an exothermic reaction. Thermometer up → chemicals down → ΔH negative.

U3

A student's value for the enthalpy of combustion of ethanol is −910 kJ/mol. The data book says −1367 kJ/mol. Explain why school values are almost always too small, never too big.

Show answer

Every common error makes Q too small or n too big:

  • Heat lost to the air, the can and the clamp → less heat reaches the water → Q too small.
  • Incomplete combustion → some fuel becomes carbon or carbon monoxide, which releases less energy → Q too small.
  • Fuel evaporates from the wick → the mass loss counts fuel that never burned → n too big.

All three push Q ÷ n down. There is no everyday error that adds extra heat to the water, so the answer is nearly always too small.

U4

If breaking bonds always takes in energy, how can any reaction be exothermic?

Show answer

Because a reaction also makes new bonds, and making bonds gives out energy. The overall ΔH is the balance.

In H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, breaking one H–H and one Cl–Cl takes in 678 kJ. Making two H–Cl bonds gives out 862 kJ. More out than in → exothermic, ΔH = −184 kJ/mol.

The "hump" on the reaction profile is the bond-breaking part: energy has to go in first before the new bonds can form. That is the activation energy.

U5

Why is the enthalpy change for making an ester from an acid and an alcohol about 0 kJ/mol?

Show answer

Look at which bonds change. One C–O and one O–H bond are broken in the reactants, and one C–O and one O–H bond are made in the products. Every other bond is untouched.

The same bonds are broken and made, so the energy taken in to break them equals the energy given out making them. Broken − made ≈ 0.

It is only "approximately" 0 because the same bond in different molecules has a slightly different energy — data-book bond energies are averages.

U6

At dynamic equilibrium the colour and pressure of the mixture stay the same for hours. Why is it wrong to say the reaction has stopped?

Show answer

Both reactions are still happening. Reactant particles are still turning into products, and product particles are still turning back into reactants.

They happen at the same rate, so every product molecule made is balanced by one used up. The amounts do not change, so nothing looks different — but it is dynamic, not static.

That is also why the concentrations are constant but not necessarily equal. There could be far more products than reactants, or the other way round.

U7

A nickel catalyst is added to CH₄ + H₂O ⇌ CO + 3H₂. The forward reaction speeds up. Why doesn't the yield of hydrogen go up?

Show answer

The catalyst provides a pathway with a lower activation energy — and it is the same pathway in both directions. So it speeds up the backward reaction by the same amount as the forward one.

Both rates rise equally, so they are still equal at the same concentrations. The position does not move and the yield is unchanged. The only benefit is that equilibrium is reached sooner.

U8

Raising the temperature increases the yield of hydrogen from CH₄ + H₂O ⇌ CO + 3H₂, but decreases the yield of ammonia from N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃. Explain the difference.

Show answer

A rise in temperature always shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction. Which way that is depends on the sign of ΔH.

Methane + steam: ΔH = +206 kJ/mol, so the forward reaction is endothermic. Heating shifts it to the right → more hydrogen.

Ammonia: ΔH is negative, so the forward reaction is exothermic and the reverse is endothermic. Heating shifts it to the left → less ammonia.

Always read the ΔH before answering. "Hotter means more product" is only true for an endothermic forward reaction.
