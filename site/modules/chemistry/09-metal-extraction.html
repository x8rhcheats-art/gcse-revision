Metal Extraction and the Reactivity Series

One list decides everything: how a metal reacts, what displaces what, how a metal is found, and how it is extracted. Add rusting and alloys and you have a topic that turns up in most real Edexcel papers. The reasoning it teaches — carbon as a reducing agent, the more reactive metal wins — is the backbone of redox questions elsewhere.

02How it actually works

Metals differ in how easily they react and how tightly they hold on to their compounds. Rank them once — the reactivity series — and reactions with acid, displacement, rusting protection and the choice of extraction method all read straight off the list.

The reactivity series — learn the order

Spec 2.17 · used in every question in this module

The reactivity series lists metals from most reactive to least reactive, and you must know the order by heart.

The order — most reactive firstK · Na · Li · Ca · Mg · Al · (C) · Zn · Fe · (H) · Cu · Ag · Aupotassium, sodium, lithium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, copper, silver, gold · carbon and hydrogen are non-metal markers

Carbon and hydrogen are slotted in as markers. Carbon's position decides extraction: metals below it can be extracted with carbon. Hydrogen's position decides acids: metals below it do not react with dilute acids. A mnemonic helps (Please Send Lions Cats Monkeys And Cute Zebras Into Hot Countries Signed Gerald) — but the exam rewards the order, not the rhyme.

✗ Putting lithium above sodium. It is K, Na, Li in this spec's list.
✗ Swapping zinc and iron. Zinc is above iron — which is the whole reason galvanising works.
✓ K Na Li Ca Mg Al · C · Zn Fe · H · Cu Ag Au.

Reactions with water and dilute acids

Spec 2.15, 2.21 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q2(a) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q2(b)

The more reactive the metal, the more vigorously it reacts with water and with dilute acid — so the vigour of the reaction ranks the metals.

Metal + watermetal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogene.g. 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂
Metal + dilute acidmetal + acid → salt + hydrogene.g. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ · Fe + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂
MetalCold waterDilute acid
K, Na, LiFizz, float, move around; K even catches fireNever tried — explosive, too dangerous
CaFizzes steadilyToo vigorous — not done
MgVery slow (reacts well with steam)Vigorous fizzing, tube gets hot
ZnNo visible reactionSteady fizzing
FeNo visible reactionSlow fizzing
Cu, Ag, AuNo reactionNo reaction — below hydrogen

The practical (spec 2.21) compares metals fairly:

  1. Put the same volume of the same concentration of dilute acid into separate test tubes, all at the same starting temperature.
  2. Add the same mass of each metal, in pieces of the same size (same surface area).
  3. Observe the rate of bubbling. You can also measure the temperature rise, or collect the gas and measure its volume in a set time.
  4. Rank the metals: fastest bubbling or biggest temperature rise = most reactive.
  5. Test the gas with a lighted splint: a squeaky pop shows hydrogen.

✗ "Potassium should not be added to acid because it is flammable."
✓ "It would react explosively / violently — it is dangerous." That was the 2024 mark.
✗ "Hydrogen relights a glowing splint." That is oxygen.
✓ "A lighted splint gives a squeaky pop."

Placing unknown metals from observations — the method

Spec 2.15, 2.16 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q2(a) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q5(b)

A metal that reacts more vigorously, or displaces more of the others, is higher in the series. The exam gives you letters instead of names so you have to use the evidence, not your memory.

  1. Read every row of the table and mark each metal's reactions: none, slow, steady, vigorous.
  2. If there is a displacement grid, count the ticks for each metal. Each tick means it pushed another metal out.
  3. The metal with the most reactions / most ticks goes at the top. The one with none goes at the bottom.
  4. Check every pair: if A displaces B, A must be above B in your list.
  5. If named metals are included, fit the unknown between them using the order you know. Then name a real metal that sits there.

Worked example — a displacement grid

Three metals A, B and C are each added to solutions of the nitrates of A, B and C. ✓ = a reaction happened.

Metal addedA nitrateB nitrateC nitrate
A—✓✓
B✗—✗
C✗✓—

Step 1. Count ticks: A = 2, C = 1, B = 0.

Step 2. Order: A (most reactive) → C → B (least reactive).

Step 3. Check pairs: A displaces C ✓ (A above C); C displaces B ✓ (C above B). Consistent.

Why the dashes? A metal is never tested with its own salt — a metal cannot displace itself. That was a whole mark in 2023 Jan Q5.

If a question says "metal X is between aluminium and copper", the answer is a real metal from that gap: zinc or iron.

Displacement in solution — observations and ionic equations

Spec 2.16, 2.20 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q2(c)–(d) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q5

A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt; a less reactive metal cannot displace a more reactive one.

Iron in copper(II) sulfate solution:

Word and symbol equationiron + copper(II) sulfate → iron(II) sulfate + copperFe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

What you see: the blue colour fades (turning pale green), and a pink-brown solid — copper — coats the iron. Magnesium or zinc in copper(II) sulfate does the same, but the solution goes colourless and the mixture gets warm.

The reverse — copper in iron(II) sulfate — gives no reaction. Writing "no reaction, because copper is less reactive than iron" is a full answer.

An ionic equation shows only the particles that change. It is the clearest way to see the electrons move. The method:

  1. Write the balanced equation with state symbols: Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
  2. Split every (aq) ionic compound into its ions: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + Cu(s)
  3. Cross out ions that are the same on both sides — the spectator ions. Here, SO₄²⁻.
  4. Write what is left: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)
  5. Check the atoms and the total charge balance: 2+ on the left, 2+ on the right.

Worked example — when the charges are different

Copper in silver nitrate solution. Copper is above silver, so it displaces it.

Full: Cu(s) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2Ag(s)

Ionic: Cu(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s)

Charge check: left 2 × (+1) = +2; right +2. Balanced.

Electrons: one Cu atom loses 2 electrons; each of two Ag⁺ ions gains 1. Two lost = two gained.

Seen: the solution turns blue (Cu²⁺ ions) and silvery-grey crystals of silver grow on the copper.

Worked example — magnesium and aluminium ions (2023 Jan style)

Magnesium displaces aluminium from a solution of an aluminium salt.

Unbalanced: Mg + Al³⁺ → Mg²⁺ + Al — charge +3 on the left, +2 on the right. Wrong.

Balance the electrons: each Mg loses 2e⁻, each Al³⁺ gains 3e⁻. The lowest common number is 6.

So: 3Mg + 2Al³⁺ → 3Mg²⁺ + 2Al — charge +6 = +6.

Mark scheme wording: magnesium is the reducing agent 1; magnesium donates / loses electrons to the Al³⁺ ions 1

✗ "Iron displaces magnesium from magnesium sulfate."
✓ "No reaction: iron is less reactive than magnesium / lower in the reactivity series." (2023 Q2(d))
✗ Leaving the sulfate ions in an "ionic" equation.
✓ Spectator ions are crossed out: Fe(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Fe²⁺(aq) + Cu(s).

Displacement with a metal oxide — the thermite reaction

Spec 2.16, 2.20 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q2(b) · 2024 Q5a

A more reactive metal heated with the oxide of a less reactive metal takes the oxygen away from it.

Thermite2Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + 2Fevery exothermic — the iron is produced molten · balancing numbers are marked

Aluminium is more reactive than iron, so it can remove the oxygen from iron(III) oxide. The reaction gives out so much heat that the iron comes out molten — it is used to weld railway lines. The same idea works on a small scale: magnesium heated with copper(II) oxide glows and leaves pink-brown copper: Mg + CuO → MgO + Cu.

Why it is redox — built step by step:

  1. Aluminium gains oxygen, so aluminium is oxidised.
  2. Iron(III) oxide loses oxygen, so iron(III) oxide is reduced.
  3. Oxidation and reduction happen together, so it is a redox reaction. Aluminium is the reducing agent.

The two-mark script for "explain why this is a redox reaction"

1. Aluminium gains oxygen, so it is oxidised 1

2. Iron(III) oxide loses oxygen, so it is reduced 1

Name the oxide, not "iron". "Oxidation and reduction happen at the same time", with nothing named, scores zero.

✗ "Iron is reduced." — Iron is the product. The thing that lost oxygen is iron(III) oxide.
✗ "Exothermic means it needs heat." — It needs heat to start, but exothermic means heat is given out to the surroundings.
✓ "Aluminium is more reactive than iron, so it removes the oxygen from iron(III) oxide."

Rusting — the conditions

Spec 2.18 · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q2(a) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q2(a) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q2(a) · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q4(a)

Iron rusts only when both oxygen and water are present. Take either one away and it does not rust.

Rusting — word equationiron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxiderust is hydrated iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃·xH₂O · a brown solid · the iron is oxidised

The classic experiment uses three test tubes, each with an iron nail:

  1. Tube 1 — air and water: nail in ordinary tap water, open to the air. Rusts.
  2. Tube 2 — water, no air: nail in boiled water (boiling removes the dissolved oxygen) with a layer of oil on top (stops oxygen dissolving back in). No rust.
  3. Tube 3 — air, no water: nail in dry air with anhydrous calcium chloride, which absorbs water vapour, and a stopper. No rust.
  4. Conclusion: only tube 1 has both, so both oxygen and water are needed.

Rusting is oxidation: the iron gains oxygen. It is not combustion, neutralisation or decomposition — the real papers have offered all three as wrong options. Rusting uses up oxygen, which is why damp iron wool in a sealed tube of air can be used to find the percentage of oxygen in air (about 21%). Iron powder or wool rusts faster than a nail because it has a larger surface area.

✗ "Iron rusts when it gets wet." — Only one of the two. Both marks need oxygen (air is accepted) and water.
✗ "Rust is iron oxide." — Too vague for the name mark.
✓ "Oxygen and water." · "Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide."
✗ "Aluminium and copper rust too." — Only iron (and steel) rusts. Other metals corrode.

Preventing rusting — barriers, galvanising and sacrificial protection

Spec 2.19 · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q2 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q2(b) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q2(a)(iii)

You stop rust either by keeping oxygen and water away from the iron, or by attaching a more reactive metal that reacts instead.

MethodWhat is doneHow it works
BarrierPaint, oil or grease, plastic coating, electroplating (e.g. chromium)Keeps oxygen and water away from the iron. Fails if scratched.
GalvanisingCoat the iron with a layer of zincA barrier and sacrificial: zinc is more reactive, so it corrodes instead of the iron — even if scratched
Sacrificial protectionAttach blocks of a more reactive metal (zinc or magnesium) to ships' hulls, pipesThe more reactive metal oxidises instead of the iron

Sacrificial protection explained as a chain:

  1. Zinc (or magnesium) is more reactive than iron.
  2. So it loses electrons more easily — it is oxidised in preference.
  3. The zinc corrodes instead of the iron. It is "sacrificed".
  4. The iron stays protected as long as some zinc is left, even if the coating is scratched.

The two-mark script for "explain how sacrificial protection / galvanising prevents rusting"

1. Zinc (or a named metal) is more reactive than iron 1

2. so the zinc reacts / oxidises / corrodes instead of the iron 1

"Zinc forms a protective layer" only explains the barrier part. The second mark needs instead of the iron.

✗ "Copper can be used for sacrificial protection." — Copper is below iron. It would make the iron rust faster.
✗ "Paint protects the iron even when scratched." — A scratch exposes the iron to oxygen and water, so it rusts.
✓ "Galvanised iron is still protected when scratched because zinc is more reactive and corrodes instead of the iron."

Ores, and why gold needs no chemistry

Spec 2.22C

Most metals are found combined in ores and must be extracted; unreactive metals such as gold are found as the uncombined element.

An ore is a rock containing enough of a metal compound (often an oxide or sulfide) to be worth extracting. The unreactive metals — gold, and sometimes silver — are found native: they are too unreactive to have formed compounds in the first place. The pattern: the more reactive the metal, the more strongly it holds on to its compound, and the more powerful (and expensive) the method needed to get it out.

Extraction: the carbon line

Spec 2.23C · 2023 Q2b · 2023 Q9b

Metals below carbon are extracted by heating their oxide with carbon; metals above carbon must be extracted by electrolysis.

Extraction is reduction — removing oxygen from the metal oxide. Carbon is cheap; electricity is expensive. So the method follows the position:

  1. Find the metal in the reactivity series.
  2. Below carbon (zinc, iron, copper): carbon is more reactive, so heating the oxide with carbon removes the oxygen. Cheap.
  3. Above carbon (aluminium, magnesium and higher): the metal is more reactive than carbon, so carbon cannot remove its oxygen.
  4. These metals are extracted by electrolysis of the molten compound. It needs a lot of electricity (and heat to melt the ore), so it is expensive.
Iron — carbon extraction2Fe₂O₃ + 3C → 4Fe + 3CO₂carbon (coke) removes oxygen from iron(III) oxide · the blast-furnace details are not needed
Copper — carbon extraction2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂formulae one mark, balancing the second (2023 Q9b)
Aluminium — electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂at the cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al — the electrons do the job carbon cannot

✗ "Magnesium can't be extracted with carbon because it is too expensive." — That is the effect, not the reason.
✓ "Magnesium is more reactive than carbon, so carbon cannot remove the oxygen from magnesium oxide." (2023 Q2b — one mark, exactly that.)
✗ "Aluminium is extracted by electrolysis of aluminium oxide solution." — It is insoluble. Say molten.

Commenting on an unfamiliar extraction

Spec 2.24C · 2021 Q10b · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q6(b)

You are not expected to know the process — the question gives the information, and you apply reactivity and redox to it.

Work through the same checklist every time:

  1. Where is the metal relative to carbon? That tells you whether carbon or something stronger is needed.
  2. What is reduced, and by what? Name the reducing agent and the compound that loses oxygen (or whose ions gain electrons).
  3. Why each condition? An inert gas such as argon stops oxygen in the air reacting with the hot metals. High temperature speeds the reaction.
  4. Cost: electricity, high temperatures, or a reducing agent that was itself expensive to make.
  5. Pollution: for example, roasting sulfide ores gives sulfur dioxide, which causes acid rain.

Worked example — two real processes

Copper pyrites (2021): stage 1 is 2CuFeS₂ + 3O₂ → 2CuS + 2FeO + 2SO₂. The sulfur is oxidised because it gains oxygen (to become SO₂). Stage 2: CuS + O₂ → Cu + SO₂.

Titanium (2022 Edexcel 2C Q6): titanium dioxide is first turned into titanium chloride using chlorine. Then the titanium chloride is heated with magnesium under argon:

TiCl₄ + 2Mg → Ti + 2MgCl₂ — a displacement: magnesium is more reactive than titanium.

Why argon (2 marks): argon is unreactive / inert 1; so oxygen in the air cannot react with / oxidise the magnesium (or titanium) 1

✗ "The sulfur forms sulfur dioxide." — names the product but doesn't apply the definition. Ignored in 2021.
✓ "The sulfur is oxidised because it gains oxygen."
✗ "Argon is used to keep it safe." — Say why: it is unreactive, so the metal is not oxidised.

Alloys — why they are harder

Spec 2.26C, 2.27C · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q6(c)

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements, and the different-sized atoms stop the layers sliding, so the alloy is harder.

An alloy is a mixture of a metal and one or more other elements, usually other metals or carbon. Steel is iron mixed with carbon (stainless steel also contains chromium and nickel).

  1. In a pure metal, all the atoms are the same size and sit in regular layers.
  2. When a force is applied, the layers slide over each other easily — so the pure metal is soft and malleable.
  3. In an alloy, atoms of different sizes are mixed in.
  4. They distort the layers, so the layers cannot slide over each other as easily.
  5. So the alloy is harder / stronger than the pure metal.

The three-mark script (2022 Edexcel 2C Q6(c))

1. In the pure metal the atoms are all the same size / the layers can slide 1

2. The alloy has atoms of different sizes 1

3. which disrupt the layers, so they cannot slide as easily 1

✗ "An alloy is a compound of two metals." — It is a mixture. No chemical bonds between the elements.
✗ "The bonds in an alloy are stronger." — Not the reason the scheme wants.
✓ "Different-sized atoms distort the layers, so the layers cannot slide over each other easily."

04Understanding it

U1

Gold has been used by humans for 6,000 years; aluminium was more expensive than gold until the 1880s. Explain both facts from the reactivity series.

Show answer

Gold sits at the bottom. It is so unreactive that it never formed compounds in the crust. It is found native, ready to pick up and work: no chemistry needed, hence its ancient history.

Aluminium sits above carbon. No fire-and-charcoal method can remove the oxygen from Al₂O₃, because aluminium is more reactive than carbon. Until large-scale electrolysis existed there was no practical way to reduce its ore. The metal was rare not because the ore was rare — it is everywhere — but because the method was missing. Reactivity sets the price of extraction.

U2

Zinc powder is added to blue copper(II) sulfate solution. State the observations, the equation, and why the reverse experiment (copper in zinc sulfate) does nothing.

Show answer

Observations: the blue colour fades to colourless (Cu²⁺ ions leaving the solution), a pink-brown solid (copper) forms, the zinc gets used up, and the mixture warms up. Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu, or as an ionic equation Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu.

Zinc is above copper, so zinc atoms lose electrons more easily. They hand two electrons each to Cu²⁺ ions, which become copper atoms. Copper in zinc sulfate would have to do the opposite — give electrons to Zn²⁺ — and copper holds its electrons more tightly than that. No reaction — and writing exactly that is the expected answer.

U3

Reduction is defined as "loss of oxygen" and also as "gain of electrons". Show, using iron(III) oxide and carbon, that the two definitions agree.

Show answer

In Fe₂O₃ the iron is present as Fe³⁺ ions, held with O²⁻ ions. When carbon removes the oxygen, what is left is iron metal — neutral Fe atoms.

To get from Fe³⁺ to Fe, each iron ion must gain three electrons: Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Fe. So the same event can be described two ways. Following the atoms: iron(III) oxide lost oxygen. Following the electrons: the iron ions gained electrons. Both say "reduced".

The electron version is more general — it also works when no oxygen is involved at all, as in Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, or at the cathode in electrolysis.

U4

Why is iron extracted with carbon but aluminium by electrolysis, when both are common structural metals — and what single cost drives the whole decision?

Show answer

Iron is below carbon in the series. Heat iron(III) oxide with carbon and the carbon removes the oxygen — and carbon (coke) is cheap.

Aluminium is above carbon. Carbon cannot remove oxygen from aluminium oxide, so the electrons must be supplied directly — electrolysis of the molten compound, using huge amounts of electricity, plus the energy to keep it molten.

The driving cost is energy — electricity versus cheap carbon. So an answer about extraction cost must tie back to position: not "electrolysis is expensive" alone, but "aluminium's reactivity forces the expensive method".

U5

2021's copper pyrites question: stage 1 is 2CuFeS₂ + 3O₂ → 2CuS + 2FeO + 2SO₂. Explain why the sulfur is described as oxidised, and why "it forms sulfur dioxide" was not enough for the mark.

Show answer

The sulfur starts combined with metals (no oxygen) and ends as SO₂. It has gained oxygen, which is the definition of oxidation the question wants. Full sentence: "the sulfur is oxidised because it gains oxygen."

"It forms SO₂" was ignored because it names the product without applying the definition. The mark tests whether you connect the observation to "gained oxygen". The schemes do this everywhere: give the definition-level reason (gained oxygen / lost electrons), not just the outcome.

U6

A painted iron gate and a galvanised iron gate both get a deep scratch. Only the painted one rusts at the scratch. Explain the difference.

Show answer

Paint is only a barrier. It works by keeping oxygen and water away from the iron. Scratch it and the iron underneath meets oxygen and water, so it rusts — and the rust can spread under the paint.

Zinc is a barrier and a sacrifice. Zinc is more reactive than iron, so even where the iron is exposed, the zinc next to it is oxidised instead. It gives up electrons more readily than iron, so the iron is not oxidised.

The test question: would a coating of copper protect a scratched gate? No — copper is below iron, so the iron would be the one oxidised. It would rust even faster.

U7

Pure iron is soft and bends easily. Adding a small amount of carbon makes steel, which is much harder. How can adding so little make such a difference?

Show answer

In pure iron every atom is the same size, so the atoms sit in neat, regular layers. Push on the metal and whole layers slide over each other — that is why it bends.

A carbon atom is a different size from an iron atom. Even a few of them scattered through the structure distort the layers, so the layers catch on each other and cannot slide as easily. The metal is harder.

That is also why more carbon gives a harder but more brittle steel (high-carbon steel for cutting tools), while mild steel stays malleable enough to press into car bodies.

U8

In the rusting experiment, one nail sits in boiled water under a layer of oil, and another sits in a stoppered tube with anhydrous calcium chloride. What is each tube testing, and why does each detail matter?

Show answer

Boiled water + oil tests "water but no oxygen". Boiling drives out the dissolved oxygen. The oil layer stops oxygen from the air dissolving back in. Leave out either step and oxygen gets to the nail, so the test fails.

Calcium chloride tests "oxygen but no water". Anhydrous calcium chloride absorbs water vapour, so the air is dry. The stopper stops damp air getting in.

Neither nail rusts, but the nail in ordinary water open to the air does. The only thing it has that both others lack is both substances — so oxygen and water are both needed.
