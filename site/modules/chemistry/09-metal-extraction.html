02How it actually works

Metals differ in how strongly they hold on to their compounds. Rank them once — the reactivity series — and extraction method, displacement, and half the redox questions read straight off the list.

The reactivity series — learn the order

Spec 2.17 · used everywhere

Potassium · sodium · lithium · calcium · magnesium · aluminium · (carbon) · zinc · iron · (hydrogen) · copper · silver · gold.

Carbon and hydrogen are slotted in as non-metal markers: carbon's position decides extraction; hydrogen's decides what happens in acids and at electrodes. A mnemonic helps (Please Send Lions Cats Monkeys And Cute Zebras Into Hot Countries Signed Gerald) — but the exam rewards the order, not the rhyme.

How the series is built: displacement

Spec 2.16 · 2024 Q5a

A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from its compounds — takes its place, in a metal oxide or a salt solution:

  • Metal + metal oxide: the thermite reaction, 2Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe. Aluminium is more reactive, so it seizes the oxygen: aluminium is oxidised (gains oxygen), iron(III) oxide is reduced (loses oxygen) — name the oxide, not the iron.
  • Metal + salt solution: zinc in copper sulfate → zinc sulfate + copper (blue fades, pink-brown copper appears). No reaction the other way — copper cannot displace zinc.

"No reaction" is itself an exam answer: a metal below cannot displace a metal above.

Ores, and why gold needs no chemistry

Spec 2.22C

Most metals are found combined in ores — rocks containing metal compounds (oxides, sulfides). The unreactive metals — gold, and partly silver — are found native (as the uncombined element): they are too unreactive to have formed compounds in the first place. The pattern: the more reactive the metal, the harder it clings to its compound, and the harsher the method needed to win it back.

Extraction: the carbon line

Spec 2.23C · 2023 Q2b · 2023 Q9b

Extraction is reduction — removing oxygen from the oxide. The economics: carbon (coke) is cheap, electricity is expensive. So:

  • Below carbon (zinc, iron, copper): heat the oxide with carbon. Carbon is more reactive than the metal, so it takes the oxygen: 2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂. Iron is the spec's named example (blast furnace, same chemistry).
  • Above carbon (aluminium, magnesium, and everything higher): carbon cannot take their oxygen — the metal is more reactive than carbon (2023's one-marker for magnesium). These need electrolysis of the molten compound: aluminium from molten aluminium oxide, the cathode delivering the electrons that carbon couldn't. Expensive, which is why it is reserved for the metals that force it.

Evaluating an unfamiliar process (spec 2.24C): the exam can describe any extraction and ask for comment. Anchor to the two questions — where is the metal relative to carbon? and what is being reduced, by what? — and the marks follow. 2021's copper pyrites: stage 1 roasts CuFeS₂ in air (the sulfur is oxidised — it gains oxygen → SO₂); stage 2, CuS + O₂ → Cu + SO₂.

The role-of-carbon two-marker — the school's favourite

Spec 2.20, 2.23C · 2024 Q8c · 2025 Q6c — identical question, consecutive years

The two-mark script for "explain the role of carbon"

1. Carbon is the reducing agent 1

2. It removes the oxygen from [calcium phosphate / copper(II) oxide / the named compound] 1

Rejected: "reacts with the oxygen", "displaces the oxygen", or naming only half the compound. The oxygen is removed from something — say what.

04Understanding it

U1

Gold has been used by humans for 6,000 years; aluminium was more expensive than gold until the 1880s. Explain both facts from the reactivity series.

Show answer

Gold sits at the bottom — so unreactive it never formed compounds in the crust. It lies around native, ready to pick up and work: no chemistry required, hence its ancient history.

Aluminium sits above carbon: no fire-and-charcoal method can strip oxygen from Al₂O₃, because aluminium out-competes carbon for it. Until electrolysis existed there was simply no industrial way to reduce its ore — the metal was rare not because the ore was rare (it's everywhere) but because the method was missing. Reactivity sets the price of extraction.

U2

Zinc powder is added to blue copper(II) sulfate solution. State the observations, the equation, and why the reverse experiment (copper in zinc sulfate) does nothing.

Show answer

Observations: the blue colour fades (Cu²⁺ leaving solution) and a pink-brown solid (copper) forms on the zinc; the zinc dissolves away. Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu.

Zinc is above copper, so it displaces it — zinc ionsise more readily, handing its electrons to Cu²⁺. Copper in zinc sulfate has no such leverage: a less reactive metal cannot push a more reactive one out of its compound. No reaction — and writing exactly that is the expected answer.

U3

The same word — reduction — is defined as "loss of oxygen" in this module and "gain of electrons" in electrolysis. Show, using iron(III) oxide + carbon, that the two definitions agree.

Show answer

In Fe₂O₃ the iron exists as Fe³⁺ ions. When carbon strips the oxygen away, the product is iron metal — atoms, Fe⁰. Getting from Fe³⁺ to Fe means each iron gained three electrons.

So "losing oxygen" and "gaining electrons" are the same event seen from different sides: the oxygen leaves as oxide ions taking their borrowed electrons back out, returning them to the metal. One definition tracks the atoms, the other tracks the electrons — the electron version is just more general (it works when no oxygen is involved at all, as at electrodes).

U4

Why is iron extracted with carbon but aluminium by electrolysis, when both are common structural metals — and what single cost drives the whole decision?

Show answer

Iron is below carbon in the series: heat Fe₂O₃ with coke and the carbon takes the oxygen — the raw material is cheap coal. Aluminium is above carbon: carbon cannot reduce Al₂O₃ at any temperature a furnace reaches, so the electrons must be delivered directly — electrolysis of the molten oxide, at vast electrical cost (melting cryolite mixtures, continuous current).

The driving cost is energy — electricity versus coke. That is why answers about extraction economics must tie back to position: not "electrolysis is expensive" alone, but "aluminium's reactivity forces the expensive method".

U5

2021's copper pyrites question: stage 1 is 2CuFeS₂ + 3O₂ → 2CuS + 2FeO + 2SO₂. Explain why the sulfur is described as oxidised, and why "it forms sulfur dioxide" was not enough for the mark.

Show answer

The sulfur starts combined with metals (no oxygen) and ends as SO₂ — it has gained oxygen, which is the definition of oxidation the question wants. Full sentence: "the sulfur is oxidised because it gains/combines with oxygen."

"It forms SO₂" was ignored because it names the product without applying the definition — the mark tests whether you can connect the observation to "gained oxygen". Same logic the schemes apply everywhere: state the definition-level reason (gained oxygen / lost electrons), not just the outcome.

05Exam drill

D1

Put these metals in order of reactivity, most reactive first: copper, magnesium, iron, potassium, zinc. (1)

Show answer

Potassium · magnesium · zinc · iron · copper 1

D2

A teacher demonstrates the thermite reaction between aluminium powder and iron(III) oxide.

(a) Give the chemical equation for the reaction. (1)
(b) Explain why this is a redox reaction. (2)

Show answer

(a) 2Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe 1 — balancing numbers required

(b) Aluminium is oxidised — it gains oxygen 1; iron(III) oxide is reduced — it loses oxygen 1 — name both species; "both happen at once" alone scores 0; "iron is reduced" loses the mark

D3

Copper can be extracted by heating copper(II) oxide with carbon.

(a) Write the balanced equation. (2)
(b) Explain the role of carbon in this reaction. (2)
(c) Explain why this method cannot be used to extract magnesium from magnesium oxide, and name the method used instead. (2)

Show answer

(a) Formulae correct 1; 2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂ 1

(b) Carbon is the reducing agent 1; it removes the oxygen from the copper(II) oxide 1

(c) Magnesium is more reactive than carbon, so carbon cannot remove its oxygen 1; electrolysis of the molten compound 1

D4

An excess of iron filings is added to a solution of copper(II) sulfate.

(a) Give two observations. (2)
(b) Write the word equation. (1)
(c) State what would be observed if silver were added to the copper(II) sulfate solution instead, and why. (2)

Show answer

(a) The blue colour fades / solution turns (pale) green 1; a pink-brown solid (copper) coats the iron 1

(b) iron + copper(II) sulfate → iron(II) sulfate + copper 1

(c) No reaction / no change 1; silver is less reactive than copper, so it cannot displace it 1

D5

Explain why gold is found in the Earth's crust as the metal itself, but aluminium is only found combined in compounds — and state what this means for how each is obtained. (3)

Show answer

Gold is very unreactive, so it never formed compounds — it occurs native/uncombined 1. Aluminium is very reactive, so all of it is locked in compounds (ores) 1. Gold needs no chemical extraction; aluminium must be extracted by electrolysis of its molten compound because it is above carbon in the reactivity series 1
