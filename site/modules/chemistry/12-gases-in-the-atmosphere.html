02How it actually works

Air is a mixture, and its oxygen is the reactive part. If you let something use up the oxygen in a sealed sample of air, the gas volume shrinks. That shrinkage is the oxygen. The rest of this topic is about what happens when elements react with that oxygen, where extra carbon dioxide comes from, and why it matters.

What dry air is made of

Spec 2.9 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q7(a) · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q2(a) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q3(a) · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q1(a) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q2

Dry air is about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.9% argon and 0.04% carbon dioxide, by volume. Learn the four numbers in that order. They come up every year.

Gas% by volumeHow it is asked
Nitrogen, N₂78% (about four-fifths)"Most abundant gas." In 2024 the multiple-choice answer was "80%".
Oxygen, O₂21% (about one-fifth)"Approximate percentage of oxygen": 21%, not 20% or 35%
Argon, Ar0.9% (about 1%)"Gas that is about 1% of dry air"; "trace gas with the highest percentage"; "least reactive gas"
Carbon dioxide, CO₂0.04%"Lowest percentage" of the four

Dry means water vapour is left out, because the amount of water vapour changes from day to day. Hydrogen is not normally found in the atmosphere (2022 Edexcel 2C Q1(a)(iii)).

✗ "Air is mostly oxygen." ✗ "Carbon dioxide is about 1%."
✓ Nitrogen 78%, oxygen 21%, argon about 1%, carbon dioxide 0.04%. The 1% gas is argon, every time.

The idea behind every "% oxygen" practical

Spec 2.10, 2.14 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q4(a) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q3(b)(ii)

Something reacts with the oxygen and removes it from the gas, so the fall in gas volume equals the volume of oxygen that was there.

  1. Trap a known volume of air, sealed, over water or between gas syringes.
  2. Let a metal (iron or copper) or a non-metal (phosphorus) react with the oxygen. The product is a solid oxide, or it dissolves in water, so it takes up almost no gas volume.
  3. Nitrogen and argon do not react, so they stay as gas.
  4. Wait until the volume stops changing. Then all the oxygen has been used.
  5. Decrease in volume = volume of oxygen. Divide by the starting volume and × 100.

Why argon (or neon) does not react: it has a full outer shell of electrons, so it does not gain, lose or share electrons. Both 2022 Jan and 2023 Jan asked this for 1 mark.

Method 1: iron wool in a test tube over water

Spec 2.10, 2.14 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q3(b)

Wet iron wool rusts and uses up the oxygen, so water rises up the tube to replace it.

  1. Push a ball of wet iron wool to the top (closed end) of a test tube.
  2. Turn the tube upside down in a beaker of water. Measure the length of the column of gas.
  3. Leave it for about a week. Rusting is slow.
  4. The iron reacts with the oxygen. The gas volume falls, so water rises up the tube.
  5. Measure the final length of the gas column once it has stopped changing.
% oxygen from column lengths% oxygen = (initial length − final length) ÷ initial length × 100the tube has the same width all the way, so length stands in for volume

Worked example: 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q3(b)(iii)

Initial column 75 mm, final column 30 mm (a mixture of oxygen and neon).

  1. Decrease = 75 − 30 = 45 mm 1
  2. % oxygen = 45 ÷ 75 × 100 = 60% 1

The mark scheme gave 40% only 1 mark. 40% is 30 ÷ 75, the gas that is left, not the oxygen that was used.

This question used a made-up mixture, so the answer was 60%, not 21%. Always calculate from the numbers you are given. Do not just write 21%.

Method 2: iron and a gas syringe, and the "total volume" trap

Spec 2.10, 2.14 · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q4 · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q2(c)

The starting volume of air is the flask (and tube) plus the air in the syringe, and the oxygen used is the drop in the syringe reading.

  1. Wet iron powder (or filings) sits in a conical flask or glass tube, joined to a gas syringe holding air.
  2. Record the syringe reading at the start. Leave the apparatus until the reading stops changing.
  3. The iron uses up the oxygen from all the air: the flask's air and the syringe's air. The plunger moves in.
  4. Oxygen used = start reading − end reading.
  5. Total air at the start = volume of flask and tube + start syringe reading.
% oxygen with a gas syringe% oxygen = (syringe start − syringe end) ÷ (apparatus volume + syringe start) × 100the bottom line is ALL the air at the start, not just the flask

Worked example: 2022 Edexcel 1C Q4(c), 3 marks

Flask and tube hold 260 cm³. Syringe goes from 90 cm³ to 22 cm³.

  1. Oxygen = 90 − 22 = 68 cm³ 1
  2. Total air at start = 260 + 90 = 350 cm³ 1
  3. 68 ÷ 350 × 100 = 19.4% 1

2023 Edexcel 2C Q2(c) was the same with a 280 cm³ flask and 100 → 27 cm³: 73 ÷ 380 × 100 = 19.2 = 19% to two significant figures (4 marks: 73, 380, 19.2, 19).

Why wet? Water is needed for iron to rust. Why powder? A powder has a larger surface area, so the iron rusts faster and you get the results sooner. The product is rust, Fe₂O₃, a brown coating on the iron.

Iron reacting with oxygen4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide · water is needed too

✗ "68 ÷ 260 × 100 = 26.2%": this leaves the syringe's air out of the total. The 2022 scheme gave it 2 of 3.
✗ The product is "red": the 2022 scheme said "IGNORE red alone".
✓ "68 ÷ (260 + 90) × 100 = 19.4%" · rust is brown (orange-brown and red-brown were allowed).

Method 3: passing air over heated copper

Spec 2.10, 2.14 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q4

Pushing the gas back and forth over hot copper removes the oxygen as black copper(II) oxide, and the syringe reading falls until it stays the same.

  1. Two gas syringes are joined by a glass tube packed with copper powder. One syringe holds a measured volume of air.
  2. Heat the copper. Cold copper does not react with oxygen quickly, so heating increases the rate and gives the particles enough energy to react.
  3. Push the gas from syringe A to B over the hot copper, then back again. Record the reading on syringe A. Repeat many times.
  4. The copper turns from pink-brown to black: copper(II) oxide forms.
  5. When the readings stay the same, all the oxygen has reacted.
  6. Let the apparatus cool to room temperature before the final reading, because hot gas takes up more volume.
Copper reacting with oxygen2Cu + O₂ → 2CuOpink-brown copper → black copper(II) oxide · oxidation

Worked example: 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q4(b)(ii), 3 marks

Syringe A starts at 78 cm³ and ends at a steady 58 cm³. The tube and connecting tubes hold 175 cm³.

  1. Oxygen = 78 − 58 = 20 cm³ 1
  2. Total gas = 175 + 78 = 253 cm³ 1
  3. 20 ÷ 253 × 100 = 7.9% 1

That sample was an oxygen and argon mixture, not air, so 7.9% is correct.

Why the answer may not be accurate (the three the scheme accepted): a leak in the apparatus; the temperature was not the same for all readings; the apparatus was not left to cool.

✗ "Copper(I) oxide". The scheme allowed plain "copper oxide", but said "REJECT copper(I) oxide".
✓ "Copper(II) oxide", which is black.

Method 4: phosphorus, a non-metal

Spec 2.10, 2.14

Phosphorus also reacts with oxygen and removes it, which shows that a non-metal works as well as a metal. The spec names both: iron (metal) and phosphorus (non-metal).

  1. A small piece of phosphorus is placed in a sealed volume of air above water, for example in a bell jar or an upturned tube. This is a teacher demonstration: phosphorus catches fire easily.
  2. The phosphorus reacts with the oxygen to make phosphorus oxide, a white solid that dissolves in the water.
  3. The oxygen is used up, so the water rises to replace it, by about one-fifth of the volume.
  4. Wait for the apparatus to cool, then measure how far the water has risen and work out the percentage exactly as in Method 1.
Phosphorus reacting with oxygen4P + 5O₂ → P₄O₁₀phosphorus(V) oxide · an acidic oxide, because phosphorus is a non-metal

Whatever reacts with the oxygen, the logic and the calculation are the same. Fall in volume = oxygen, and divide by all the gas you started with.

For air, a good answer is close to 21%. An answer far below that means not all the oxygen reacted, or the final reading was taken while the gas was still warm.

Oxides: basic for metals, acidic for non-metals

Spec 1.20 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q1(a) · 2021 school Q10(c)

Metal oxides are basic and non-metal oxides are acidic. A metal oxide reacts with acids, and the oxides that dissolve give alkaline solutions. A non-metal oxide that dissolves in water gives an acidic solution.

✗ "Sulfur dioxide is an acid." It is an acidic oxide. It forms an acidic solution when it dissolves in water.
✗ "Magnesium oxide is acidic because it contains oxygen." All oxides contain oxygen. The element decides the character.
✓ "Magnesium is a metal, so its oxide is basic. Sulfur is a non-metal, so its oxide is acidic."

Calculating from a carbonate decomposition

Spec 2.12 · 2023 school Q3

The mass lost on heating is the mass of carbon dioxide given off, and one mole of MCO₃ gives one mole of CO₂.

Worked example: 2023 school Q3, identify X in XCO₃

7.40 g of a Group 2 carbonate, XCO₃, gives off 2.20 g of carbon dioxide. [Mᵣ of CO₂ = 44]

  1. n(CO₂) = 2.20 ÷ 44 = 0.05 mol 1
  2. XCO₃ → XO + CO₂ is 1 : 1, so n(XCO₃) = 0.05 mol 1
  3. Mᵣ(XCO₃) = mass ÷ moles = 7.40 ÷ 0.05 1 = 148 1
  4. CO₃ = 12 + (3 × 16) = 60, so Aᵣ(X) = 148 − 60 = 88 1
  5. X = strontium (Aᵣ 88, Group 2) 1

If the question gives the mass of solid left, subtract: mass of CO₂ = mass before − mass after. Heat to constant mass, so that you know the decomposition is complete.

Carbon dioxide, the greenhouse effect and climate change

Spec 2.13 · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q2(d) · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q1(b) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q2(b)

Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas: it traps heat in the atmosphere, so increasing amounts may contribute to climate change.

  1. Burning fuels such as alkanes (complete combustion), and thermal decomposition of carbonates, release carbon dioxide. Respiration releases it too.
  2. CO₂ is a greenhouse gas. It absorbs heat (infrared) given off by the Earth and traps it in the atmosphere.
  3. More CO₂ traps more heat, so the average global temperature rises. This is global warming, and it leads to climate change.
  4. Effects: melting polar ice caps, rising sea levels, flooding, wildfires. Extra CO₂ also dissolves in the sea, so the oceans become more acidic.

Why more CO₂ is a problem: 2 marks (2023 Edexcel 2C Q2(d))

1. Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas (it traps heat in the atmosphere) 1

2. It causes climate change / global warming, or a named effect such as melting ice caps, rising sea levels or flooding 1

Which reactions make CO₂? In 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q2(b)(i), the two ticks were complete combustion of an alkane and thermal decomposition of copper(II) carbonate. The wrong options were cracking (it makes smaller hydrocarbons), magnesium + hydrochloric acid (it makes hydrogen) and rusting (it uses up oxygen).

✗ "CO₂ makes a hole in the ozone layer." This was rejected in 2022 and 2023.
✗ "CO₂ causes acid rain." This was ignored. Acid rain comes from SO₂ and oxides of nitrogen (module 10).
✓ "CO₂ is a greenhouse gas, so more of it causes climate change, e.g. rising sea levels."

04Understanding it

U1

In the iron-and-syringe experiment, why do you divide by the flask volume plus the syringe reading, and not by the flask volume alone?

Show answer

The flask and the syringe are joined, so they hold one sample of air. The iron takes oxygen from all of it, including the air that started in the syringe. That is why the plunger moves in.

So the fall in the syringe reading is the oxygen from the whole sample, and it has to be divided by the whole sample. Divide by the flask alone and the bottom number is too small, so the answer is too big: about 26% instead of 19–21%. That mistake cost one mark in both 2022 and 2023.

U2

In the heated-copper experiment, why do you keep pushing the gas back and forth until the reading stops changing, and why must you let it cool before the last reading?

Show answer

Each pass only removes some of the oxygen. Only the gas touching the hot copper reacts. When the reading stays the same on several passes, there is no oxygen left to remove. That is the 2022 Jan answer: "results are the same".

Gas expands when it is hot. A reading taken while the gas is warm is too large, so the decrease looks smaller and the % oxygen comes out too low. Cooling to room temperature makes the start and end readings a fair comparison.

U3

Why does a test tube of wet iron wool over water take about a week, when heated copper does the same job in minutes?

Show answer

Rusting at room temperature is a slow reaction. The particles have little energy, and only the surface of the iron is exposed.

Heating the copper gives the particles much more energy, so far more collisions succeed and the oxygen is removed quickly. This is the same collision theory as in module 7. That is also why iron powder is better than lumps: a larger surface area gives more collisions per second, so the reaction is faster.

U4

Iron, copper and phosphorus are very different elements. Why do all three give the same answer for the % oxygen in air?

Show answer

Each one only has to do one job: react with all the oxygen and turn it into something that is not a gas, such as a solid oxide or an oxide that dissolves in the water.

Nitrogen and argon are left behind in every method. So the fall in gas volume always measures the same thing, the oxygen, whatever removed it. The spec asks you to know that both a metal and a non-metal can do this.

U5

Magnesium oxide makes universal indicator turn blue. Sulfur dioxide solution turns it red. Explain the difference in terms of what the elements are.

Show answer

Magnesium is a metal, so its oxide is basic. It reacts with water to form magnesium hydroxide, which gives an alkaline solution with OH⁻ ions, so the pH is above 7.

Sulfur is a non-metal, so its oxide is acidic. It dissolves in water to form an acid, which gives H⁺ ions, so the pH is below 7.

This is one of the two tests the spec gives for classifying an element. The other is electrical conductivity.

U6

When copper(II) carbonate is heated, the solid gets lighter. When copper is heated in air, the solid gets heavier. Both make black copper(II) oxide. How?

Show answer

Carbonate: CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂. The carbon dioxide is a gas and escapes, so the solid loses the mass of the CO₂.

Copper: 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO. Oxygen from the air joins onto the copper, so the solid gains the mass of that oxygen.

Mass is conserved both times. The difference is whether a gas leaves the solid or joins it.

U7

Carbon dioxide is only 0.04% of the air. How can such a small amount affect the climate?

Show answer

Nitrogen and oxygen are not greenhouse gases, so they do not trap heat in this way. Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas: it absorbs heat given off by the Earth and traps it in the atmosphere.

So even a small percentage matters, and a small rise in that percentage is a large increase in the amount of this particular gas. More CO₂ traps more heat. That is why burning fuels may contribute to climate change. The spec says "may contribute", so write it as a cause, not a certainty about the size of the effect.

U8

A student calculates the % oxygen in air as 16%. Give two likely reasons, and explain why a mistake in this experiment usually makes the answer too low rather than too high.

Show answer

Reasons: not all the oxygen had reacted (not left long enough, or not enough iron or copper), or the final reading was taken while the gas was still warm and expanded.

Why usually low: the method depends on the volume falling as far as it can. Anything that stops the reaction from finishing, or makes the gas take up extra room at the end, makes the fall smaller, so the percentage comes out lower.

The main way to get too high an answer is a calculation slip, such as leaving the syringe volume out of the total.
