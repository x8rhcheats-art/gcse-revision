From the June 2024 Paper 2C mark scheme, on explaining sacrificial protection:

"REJECT a more reactive metal rusts instead of iron"

Only iron rusts. Zinc and magnesium corrode, react or are oxidised instead of the iron. The January 2022 scheme says the same about galvanising: "REJECT references to zinc rusting".

02How it actually works

This module builds on the reactivity series you met in Module 9 (Metal Extraction). There the list was used to decide how a metal is extracted. Here you see the evidence for the order — reactions with water, acids and other metals' compounds — then what reactivity means for iron in the real world: it rusts, and we stop it with barriers, zinc and magnesium. The module ends with why we use alloys rather than pure metals.

The order, and the two kinds of evidence

Spec 2.15, 2.17 · recap of Module 9 · used in every question below

The more vigorously a metal reacts with water or acid, the higher it sits in the reactivity series.

Reactivity series (spec 2.17)K · Na · Li · Ca · Mg · Al · (C) · Zn · Fe · (H) · Cu · Ag · Aumost reactive on the left · carbon and hydrogen are markers, not metals

There are two ways to put metals in order:

  1. How fast they react with water or dilute acid — watch the bubbles (hydrogen) and the temperature rise. Faster = more reactive.
  2. Displacement — a more reactive metal pushes a less reactive one out of its compound. If A displaces B, A is above B.

Hydrogen's place matters: metals above hydrogen react with dilute acids to make hydrogen; metals below hydrogen (copper, silver, gold) do not react at all.

Why aluminium looks less reactive than it is

Spec 2.15, 2.25C

Aluminium is covered by a thin, tough layer of aluminium oxide that stops water and acid reaching the metal underneath.

That is why aluminium reacts slowly with acid at first and then speeds up once the acid has eaten through the layer. It is also why aluminium is used outdoors without rusting away: the oxide layer protects it from corrosion. On the series it is still above zinc and iron — thermite proves it.

Practical: metals in dilute acid

Spec 2.21 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q2(b)

To compare magnesium, zinc and iron fairly, change only the metal and compare how fast each makes hydrogen.

  1. Put the same volume (e.g. 20 cm³) of the same concentration of dilute hydrochloric (or sulfuric) acid into three test tubes.
  2. Measure the starting temperature of the acid.
  3. Add the same mass of each metal, in the same form (all powder, or pieces of the same size) so the surface area is the same.
  4. Observe the rate of bubbling, and record the temperature rise — or collect the hydrogen in a gas syringe and measure the volume in a set time.
  5. The most bubbles / biggest temperature rise / most gas = the most reactive.

Expected order: magnesium > zinc > iron. Copper can be added as a comparison: no bubbles at all.

Mark script — describe how to compare three metals with acid

1. Same volume and concentration of acid 1

2. Same mass and surface area (form) of each metal 1

3. Measure something: rate of bubbles, temperature rise or volume of gas in a set time 1

4. The fastest reaction / greatest rise shows the most reactive metal 1

Displacement from salt solutions as evidence

A metal coats itself with a less reactive metal from a salt solution; if nothing happens, the metal in the solution is more reactive (or is the same metal).

Displacement and electrons: the reducing agent

Spec 2.20 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q5(c)

In every displacement the more reactive metal loses electrons, so it is oxidised and is the reducing agent.

Magnesium and aluminium ions3Mg(s) + 2Al³⁺(aq) → 3Mg²⁺(aq) + 2Al(s)each Mg loses 2e⁻ · each Al³⁺ gains 3e⁻
  1. Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻: magnesium loses electrons — it is oxidised.
  2. Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al: aluminium ions gain electrons — they are reduced.
  3. Magnesium donates the electrons that reduce Al³⁺, so magnesium is the reducing agent. Al³⁺ is the oxidising agent.

Mark script — "explain which substance is the reducing agent"

1. Magnesium is the reducing agent 1

2. Because it donates / loses electrons (to the aluminium ions) 1

Full definitions of oxidation, reduction and the agents in electron terms are in Module 8 (Electrolysis and Redox).

Displacement from oxides: thermite

Spec 2.20 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q2(b) · 2024 school Q5(a)

Aluminium takes the oxygen from iron(III) oxide because aluminium is more reactive than iron; the reaction is very exothermic.

Thermite reaction2Al + Fe₂O₃ → 2Fe + Al₂O₃started with a burning magnesium fuse · the iron comes out molten
  • Exothermic: heat (thermal energy) is given out to the surroundings. Enough to melt the iron — used to weld railway lines.
  • Why aluminium displaces iron: aluminium is more reactive (higher in the reactivity series) than iron.
  • Redox: aluminium gains oxygen so it is oxidised; iron(III) oxide loses oxygen so it is reduced.
  • Aluminium is the reducing agent; iron(III) oxide is the oxidising agent.

✗ "Exothermic means energy is released" — the 2024 scheme ignores "energy" on its own.
✓ "Heat / thermal energy is given out to the surroundings."

✗ "Oxidation and reduction happen at the same time." — no species named, no marks.
✓ "Aluminium gains oxygen and is oxidised; iron(III) oxide loses oxygen and is reduced."

Rusting: what it needs and what it makes

Spec 2.18 · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q2(a) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q2(a) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q2(a) · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q4(a)

Iron rusts only when both water and oxygen are present; rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide.

Rusting — word equationiron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxidesimplified formula Fe₂O₃ · a brown / orange-brown solid
Rusting — symbol equation4Fe + 3O₂ + 2xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂Ox varies — that is why rust has no single formula
  • Rusting is oxidation: the iron gains oxygen (and loses electrons, Fe → Fe³⁺).
  • It is slow, and it is exothermic. Hand warmers use fast rusting of iron powder.
  • Iron powder rusts faster than a nail: larger surface area, so a faster reaction. That is why 2022's oxygen-in-air experiment used wet iron powder.
  • Only iron and steel rust. Other metals corrode.

✗ "Rust is iron oxide" — ignored. ✗ "Iron(II) oxide" — rejected.
✓ "(Hydrated) iron(III) oxide" or Fe₂O₃.

✗ Colour of rust: "red" alone — the 2022 scheme ignores it.
✓ "Brown", "orange-brown" or "red-brown".

The three-tube experiment

Spec 2.18 · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q4(a)(i)

Each tube removes one condition; only the tube with both air and water shows rust.

TubeSet-upResultWhat it shows
AIron nail in tap water, open to the airRustsWater + oxygen → rust
BNail in boiled water with a layer of oil on topNo rustOxygen is needed
CNail in dry air with anhydrous calcium chloride, tube stopperedNo rustWater is needed
  1. Boiling the water drives out the dissolved air (oxygen).
  2. The oil layer stops air dissolving back into the water.
  3. Anhydrous calcium chloride is a drying agent — it absorbs water vapour from the air.
  4. The stopper stops moist air getting in.

Conclusion: iron needs both water and oxygen to rust. Remove either one and it does not rust.

Preventing rust: barrier methods

Spec 2.19 · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q2(a)(ii) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q2(b)(iii)

A barrier method coats the iron so that water and oxygen cannot reach its surface.

  • Painting — bridges, cars, railings.
  • Oiling or greasing — bike chains, moving machine parts (paint would crack off).
  • Plastic coating — garden wire, fridge shelves.
  • Electroplating with another metal, e.g. chromium plating.

The weakness: if the barrier is scratched or chipped, water and oxygen reach the iron and it rusts at that point.

✗ Asked for a barrier method: "sacrificial protection" — the 2024 scheme rejects it. It is a different method.
✓ "Painting", "oiling", "greasing" or "coating with plastic".

Galvanising and sacrificial protection

Spec 2.19 · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q2(b) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q2(b) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q2(a)(iii)

A more reactive metal in contact with iron is oxidised instead of the iron, so the iron does not rust.

Sacrificial protection: blocks of magnesium or zinc are bolted to ship hulls, oil rigs and underground pipes. The block corrodes away and must be replaced from time to time; the iron stays intact.

Galvanising: iron or steel is coated with a layer of zinc (e.g. dipped in molten zinc). It protects in two ways:

  1. The zinc is a barrier that keeps water and oxygen off the iron.
  2. If the zinc is scratched, zinc is more reactive than iron, so the zinc reacts / is oxidised instead of the iron — sacrificial protection keeps working.
Sacrificial zincZn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻zinc loses electrons in place of the iron (Fe → Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻ does not happen)

Mark script — explain how sacrificial protection works (2)

1. A more reactive metal (e.g. zinc or magnesium) is attached to / coats the iron 1

2. The more reactive metal reacts / is oxidised / corrodes instead of the iron 1

Never "rusts instead". Never just "an element".

Alloys, and why they are harder

Spec 2.26C, 2.27C (triple only) · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q6(c) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q5(a) · 2025 school Q4(a)

In an alloy, atoms of different sizes disrupt the regular layers, so the layers cannot slide over each other easily and the alloy is harder.

Alloy — definitionalloy = a mixture of a metal and one or more other elementsusually other metals or carbon · steel = iron + carbon · brass = copper + zinc
  1. In a pure metal, all the atoms (positive ions) are the same size and sit in regular layers. The layers can slide over one another, so the metal is soft and malleable. (Metallic bonding itself is in Module 2.)
  2. An alloy contains atoms of different sizes.
  3. The different-sized atoms distort / disrupt the layers.
  4. So the layers cannot slide over each other as easily — the alloy is harder and stronger.

Mark script — why is the alloy stronger than the pure metal? (3)

1. In the pure metal all the atoms are the same size, so layers can slide over each other 1

2. The alloy has atoms of different sizes 1

3. This disrupts the structure, so the layers cannot slide as easily 1

A labelled diagram can score all three: a regular grid of equal circles next to a grid with some bigger circles.

✗ "The molecules in the alloy can't slide." — metals have no molecules; the 2022 and January 2023 schemes reject "molecules".
✓ "The layers of atoms / ions cannot slide."

✗ "The alloy has stronger bonds." — not the reason.
✓ "Atoms of different sizes make it harder for the layers to slide."

04Understanding it

U1

Aluminium is above zinc in the reactivity series, yet aluminium foil barely reacts with dilute acid at first and aluminium window frames do not corrode. Explain.

Show answer

Aluminium reacts instantly with oxygen in the air to form a thin, tough layer of aluminium oxide. That layer sticks to the metal and stops water, oxygen and acid reaching the aluminium underneath.

In acid the reaction is slow until the oxide has been dissolved, then it speeds up. Outdoors the layer never goes away, so the frame is protected. The metal is reactive; its coating is not.

U2

Why does copper not react with dilute hydrochloric acid, while magnesium fizzes rapidly?

Show answer

A metal reacts with acid by displacing hydrogen from it. Only metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series can do this.

Magnesium is well above hydrogen, so it gives up electrons easily and makes hydrogen gas fast. Copper is below hydrogen, so it cannot displace it — no bubbles, no salt, no reaction.

U3

In the three-tube rusting experiment, why is the water boiled, why is oil added, and why do you need all three tubes rather than just one?

Show answer

Boiling drives out dissolved air, so there is no oxygen in the water. The oil stops air dissolving back in. That tube has water but no oxygen.

One tube can only show that rusting happens. To prove both water and oxygen are needed you must remove each one separately: no oxygen (boiled water and oil), no water (calcium chloride). Tube A, with both, shows what happens when nothing is removed — the comparison.

U4

A painted steel gate and a galvanised steel gate are both scratched through to the steel. Explain why only one of them starts to rust.

Show answer

Painted gate: paint is only a barrier. Once scratched, water and oxygen reach the bare steel, and it rusts at the scratch.

Galvanised gate: the zinc is also a barrier, but zinc is more reactive than iron. At the scratch the zinc is oxidised instead of the iron — it gives up its electrons first. The iron is protected until the nearby zinc is used up.

U5

Why do the mark schemes reject "the zinc rusts instead of the iron", when zinc is clearly corroding?

Show answer

Rusting has a precise meaning: the oxidation of iron to form hydrated iron(III) oxide. Zinc does not make rust; it forms zinc compounds.

So the correct words for zinc or magnesium are corrodes, reacts or is oxidised. Saying zinc "rusts" shows the examiner you do not know what rust is.

U6

In the January 2023 question, magnesium was put into four sulfate solutions and then metal X was. Why was the magnesium table alone not enough to find the full order?

Show answer

Magnesium displaced aluminium, copper and X. That tells you magnesium is top, but nothing about how Al, X and Cu compare with each other.

Metal X displaced only copper, and not aluminium. So X is below aluminium and above copper. You need one test for each pair you want to compare — the second table supplied the missing pairs: Mg > Al > X > Cu.

U7

In the thermite reaction, why is aluminium called the reducing agent, and why is it also correct to say it loses electrons?

Show answer

Aluminium removes oxygen from iron(III) oxide, so it reduces it: that makes it the reducing agent. Aluminium gains the oxygen, so it is itself oxidised.

In electron terms: aluminium atoms become Al³⁺ ions in Al₂O₃ — they lose electrons (oxidation). The Fe³⁺ ions in Fe₂O₃ become iron atoms — they gain electrons (reduction). The oxygen version and the electron version describe the same change.

U8

Pure iron is softer than steel, and a chisel is made of high-carbon steel but a car door of mild steel. Explain both facts.

Show answer

Softer pure iron: all the iron atoms are the same size, in regular layers that slide easily. In steel the carbon atoms are a different size; they disrupt the layers, so they slide less easily and the steel is harder.

More carbon, more disruption: high-carbon steel is very hard, so it keeps a sharp cutting edge — ideal for a chisel, though brittle. A car door must be pressed into curved shapes, so it needs mild steel, which has little carbon and is still malleable.
