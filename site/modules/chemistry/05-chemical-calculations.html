The mark schemes are unusually blunt about how these marks die:

And the standing rule on every front page: correct answer without working scores full marks, but a wrong answer without working scores zero — show the moles step and the error-carried-forward rules protect you.

02How it actually works

Every calculation on these papers is the same three-step journey: grams → moles → whatever was asked (grams of something else, a volume of gas, a formula, a yield). The mole is the bridge in the middle; the balanced equation is the exchange rate.

The mole, and the one equation that runs the topic

Spec 1.27, 1.28 · every paper

A mole is just a counted amount of particles — 6.0 × 10²³ of them. Its usefulness: one mole of anything weighs its Mr in grams. So:

n = mass ÷ Mr — and rearranged, mass = n × Mr.

Mr (relative formula mass) is the sum of the Ar values from the periodic table: Na₂CO₃ = (2 × 23) + 12 + (3 × 16) = 106. Brackets multiply everything inside: Ca(OH)₂ = 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 74.

2024 extended this to counting particles: 0.081 g of H₂ = 0.081 ÷ 2 = 0.0405 mol → 0.0405 × 6.0 × 10²³ = 2.4 × 10²² molecules (2 sf, standard form — both were demanded).

Using the balanced equation as an exchange rate

Spec 1.29 · 2021 Q6 · 2024 Q3e

The big numbers in a balanced equation are mole ratios. The routine, always in this order:

  1. Moles of what you know: 10.5 g NaHCO₃ ÷ 84 = 0.125 mol
  2. Ratio across the equation: 2NaHCO₃ → CO₂, so ÷2 → 0.0625 mol CO₂
  3. Convert to what was asked: mass 0.0625 × 44 = 2.75 g, or volume 0.0625 × 24,000 = 1500 cm³

Ratios also settle excess questions (2025: 1.5 g Mg = 0.0625 mol needs 0.125 mol of acid, only 0.1 mol present, so the magnesium is in excess) — always compare moles needed against moles available, never masses.

Gas volumes — the molar volume shortcut

Spec 1.35C · 2022 Q8 · 2023 Q6 · 2024 Q3e · 2025 Q3a

One mole of any gas at room temperature and pressure occupies 24 dm³ = 24,000 cm³. So volume = moles × 24 (in dm³), and moles = volume ÷ 24,000 (in cm³). Match the unit to the one printed on the answer line before you calculate.

The school's set-piece (used in 2022 and re-used in 2023): 6.4 tonnes of sulfur → SO₂. 6.4 × 10⁶ g ÷ 32 = 2.0 × 10⁵ mol → × 24 = 4.8 × 10⁶ dm³, in standard form — the third mark was literally for writing it in standard form.

Empirical and molecular formulae

Spec 1.31, 1.32, 1.33 · 2021 Q10a · 2022 Q9 · 2024 Q7 · 2025 Q10

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. From masses or percentages, the drill is fixed:

  1. Divide each element's mass (or %) by its Ar — from the periodic table, the bigger number, never the atomic number.
  2. Divide everything by the smallest to get the ratio.
  3. If the ratio isn't whole (1 : 1.33), multiply up (× 3 → 3 : 4 → Pb₃O₄) — never round 1.33 to 1.

The molecular formula is a whole multiple of the empirical one: divide the given Mr by the empirical mass. 2022: C₂H₄Cl has empirical mass 63.5; Mr = 127; 127 ÷ 63.5 = 2 → C₄H₈Cl₂.

2021's scheme: "Division by atomic numbers or other inappropriate numbers scores 0/3. Fractions upside down scores 0/3." If your first step is % ÷ Ar you are safe; anything else scores nothing at all.

Formulae from experiments — the two set practicals

Spec 1.31, 1.36 (practical) · 2024 Q5b, Q7 · 2021 Q7 · 2022 Q6

Metal oxide by combustion (MgO): weigh crucible empty, with magnesium, and after heating. The gain in mass is the oxygen that combined. The lid is lifted occasionally to let oxygen in; the result improves by reheating and reweighing to constant mass — constant mass is the evidence the reaction (or the drying) is complete.

Metal oxide by reduction (CuO): pass methane/hydrogen over heated oxide; mass falls as oxygen is removed. Mass of metal and mass of oxygen lost → divide each by Ar → ratio (2024: 0.06 : 0.06 = 1 : 1 → CuO).

Water of crystallisation (the crucible/tube practical): hydrated crystals are heated to constant mass. Mass after = anhydrous salt; the loss = water. Then:

  1. moles of anhydrous salt = mass ÷ Mr (2022: 3.18 ÷ 106 = 0.03)
  2. moles of water = mass ÷ 18 (3.78 ÷ 18 = 0.21)
  3. divide by the smaller → 1 : 7 → Na₂CO₃·7H₂O

Every year's twist: why might x come out too low? — not heated long enough, so not all the water was removed. Improve it? — repeat heating to constant mass.

Ar from isotopes, and percentage yield

Spec 1.17, 1.30 · 2023 Q9a · 2025 Q7b

Relative atomic mass from abundances is a weighted mean: Ar = Σ(isotope mass × %) ÷ 100. Copper: (63 × 69.5 + 65 × 30.5) ÷ 100 = 63.61 → 63.6 to 3 sf.

Percentage yield = actual mass obtained ÷ theoretical mass × 100. The theoretical mass always comes from a moles calculation: 2025 gave 0.150 mol of PbBr₂ expected → 0.150 × 367 = 55.05 g theoretical; actual 49.6 g → 49.6 ÷ 55.05 × 100 = 90.1%. The scheme explicitly rejected dividing the actual mass by the moles figure — masses over masses, always.

Percentage by mass of an element (2023 Q6c): (atoms × Ar ÷ Mr) × 100 — nitrogen in (NH₄)₂SO₄ = (2 × 14 ÷ 132) × 100 = 21.2%. Count every atom of the element: the 2 outside the bracket doubles the N.

04Understanding it

U1

Why does one mole of CO₂ (44 g) and one mole of H₂ (2 g) — wildly different masses — occupy exactly the same volume as gases at room temperature and pressure?

Show answer

Because in a gas the particles are so far apart that the volume is almost entirely empty space — the size and mass of the individual molecules barely matter. What sets the volume is the number of particles (and the temperature and pressure), and one mole of anything is the same number of particles: 6.0 × 10²³.

That is the entire justification for the molar volume shortcut: equal moles of any gas → equal volumes → 24 dm³ per mole at rtp. It works for gases only; solids and liquids have their particles touching, so their volumes depend on what they are.

U2

The empirical drill says "divide the percentage by the Ar". What is that step actually calculating, and why does dividing by the atomic number score zero?

Show answer

It converts a mass ratio into a mole ratio — % of mass ÷ mass-per-mole = relative number of moles of atoms. In 100 g of the lead oxide, 90.7 g ÷ 207 g/mol = 0.438 mol of lead atoms. A formula is a ratio of atoms counted, not of grams, so you must get to moles before comparing.

The atomic number is the count of protons — nothing to do with mass. Dividing by it produces a number with no meaning, which is why the scheme kills the whole question (0/3) rather than just one mark: the method itself is wrong, not the arithmetic.

U3

In the water-of-crystallisation practical, the value of x comes out too low even though every weighing was accurate. What happened, how would you detect it, and how do you fix it?

Show answer

What happened: the crystals were not heated for long enough, so not all the water of crystallisation was driven off. Some water is still in the "anhydrous" mass, making the salt look heavier and the water lighter — the ratio of water to salt shrinks, so x shrinks.

Detection and fix are the same idea: heat again, cool, reweigh — and repeat until the mass no longer changes. Constant mass is the proof that all the water is gone; a mass still falling means there was more water to lose.

The trap in reverse: if the question says x came out too HIGH, look for the salt decomposing or spitting out of the crucible — mass lost that wasn't water.

U4

A question gives you 1.5 g of magnesium and 0.1 mol of hydrochloric acid, and asks you to "show that the magnesium is in excess" (Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂). Why can't you just compare 1.5 g with the acid's mass — and what is the correct comparison?

Show answer

Grams of different substances aren't comparable — a gram of magnesium contains a different number of particles from a gram of HCl, and the equation trades in particles, not grams.

The correct route: 1.5 ÷ 24 = 0.0625 mol Mg. The ratio is 1 : 2, so reacting all of it needs 2 × 0.0625 = 0.125 mol of acid. Only 0.1 mol is available — the acid runs out first, so the magnesium is in excess.

The template for every excess/"show that" question: moles of both → scale one by the ratio → compare needed vs available, in moles.

U5

Why is the percentage yield of a real preparation always less than 100% — and why does the exam's yield formula use two masses rather than two amounts in moles?

Show answer

Less than 100%: product is physically lost at every step — some stays dissolved in the solution after crystallisation, some is left on the filter paper or washed away, side reactions consume reactant. (In 2025's PbBr₂ preparation, ~10% went missing largely in filtering and washing.)

Why masses: actual ÷ theoretical is only meaningful when both are the same kind of quantity. You weigh the actual product, so the theoretical value must be converted all the way to a mass (moles expected × Mr) before dividing. The 2025 scheme specifically rejected 49.6 ÷ 0.150 — a mass divided by an amount of moles is a nonsense number.

U6

Chlorine's Ar is 35.5 — not a whole number, though every single chlorine atom has a whole-number mass. Explain, and state what a 75% ³⁵Cl / 25% ³⁷Cl mixture gives.

Show answer

Ar is a weighted average over the isotopes as they occur naturally, not the mass of any one atom. Individual atoms are ³⁵Cl or ³⁷Cl; the average of a big natural sample sits in between, pulled towards the more abundant one.

(35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100 = (2625 + 925) ÷ 100 = 35.5.

Sanity check that catches sign-of-life errors: the answer must lie between the isotope masses, nearer the more abundant isotope. 2023's copper answer (63.6, close to 63 at 69.5%) passes exactly that check.

U7

2023 gave a solid metal carbonate XCO₃ that decomposed to give 0.05 mol of CO₂ from 7.40 g. Walk the route from those two numbers to the identity of X, and say where the periodic table enters.

Show answer

Three steps, each one mark:

  1. Ratio: XCO₃ → XO + CO₂ is 1 : 1, so moles of XCO₃ = moles of CO₂ = 0.05.
  2. Mr from the definition of moles: Mr = mass ÷ n = 7.40 ÷ 0.05 = 148.
  3. Subtract the known part: CO₃ contributes 60, so Ar(X) = 148 − 60 = 88. The periodic table's Group 2 metal with Ar 88 is strontium.

This is the pattern for every identify-the-element question: get moles, get Mr, strip away what you know, look up what remains — using the question's own constraint (a Group 2 metal) to pick the match.

05Exam drill

D1

Sodium hydrogencarbonate decomposes on heating: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂

(a) Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced by completely decomposing 10.5 g of NaHCO₃. [Mr of NaHCO₃ = 84; Mr of CO₂ = 44] (2)
(b) Calculate the volume of this carbon dioxide at rtp, in cm³. [1 mol of gas = 24,000 cm³ at rtp] (2)

Show answer

(a) n(NaHCO₃) = 10.5 ÷ 84 = 0.125 mol → ratio 2 : 1 → n(CO₂) = 0.0625 mol 1; mass = 0.0625 × 44 = 2.75 g 1

(b) n(CO₂) = 0.0625 mol 1; volume = 0.0625 × 24,000 = 1500 cm³ 1 — error carried forward from (a) is allowed if the working is shown

D2

Hydrated iron(II) sulfate is FeSO₄·xH₂O. A sample is heated to constant mass in a tube: mass of tube 11.96 g, tube + crystals 17.56 g, tube + contents after heating 15.76 g.

(a) State why the crystals are heated to constant mass. (1)
(b) Calculate x, to the nearest whole number. [Mr of FeSO₄ = 152; Mr of H₂O = 18] (3)

Show answer

(a) To make sure all the water (of crystallisation) has been removed 1

(b) mass FeSO₄ = 15.76 − 11.96 = 3.80 g and mass H₂O = 17.56 − 15.76 = 1.80 g → n(FeSO₄) = 3.80 ÷ 152 = 0.025 1; n(H₂O) = 1.80 ÷ 18 = 0.10 1; x = 0.10 ÷ 0.025 = 4 1

D3

A compound of lead and oxygen is 90.7% lead and 9.3% oxygen by mass. Show by calculation that its empirical formula is Pb₃O₄. [Ar: Pb = 207, O = 16] (4)

Show answer

Pb: 90.7 ÷ 207 = 0.438 1; O: 9.3 ÷ 16 = 0.581 1; divide by the smaller: 1 : 1.33 1; multiply up: × 3 → 3 : 4 → Pb₃O₄ 1

Divide by Ar, never the atomic number; never round 1.33 down to 1 — a ratio near a third or a quarter is the question telling you to multiply up.

D4

A mass of 6.4 tonnes of sulfur is burned: S + O₂ → SO₂. Calculate the maximum volume, in dm³, of sulfur dioxide produced at rtp. Give your answer in standard form. [Ar of S = 32; molar volume = 24 dm³ at rtp; 1 tonne = 10⁶ g] (3)

Show answer

n(S) = 6.4 × 10⁶ ÷ 32 = 2.0 × 10⁵ mol 1; ratio 1 : 1, volume = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 24 = 4,800,000 dm³ 1; in standard form: 4.8 × 10⁶ dm³ 1

The last mark is only for the standard form — 4 800 000 written out scored 2 of 3. This exact question has appeared on two papers.

D5

A compound G is 37.8% carbon, 6.3% hydrogen and 55.9% chlorine by mass, and has Mr = 127.

(a) Show that the empirical formula of G is C₂H₄Cl. [Ar: C = 12, H = 1, Cl = 35.5] (3)
(b) Determine the molecular formula of G. (2)

Show answer

(a) 37.8 ÷ 12 = 3.15; 6.3 ÷ 1 = 6.3; 55.9 ÷ 35.5 = 1.57 1 1; divide by smallest → 2 : 4 : 1 → C₂H₄Cl 1

(b) empirical mass of C₂H₄Cl = 63.5; 127 ÷ 63.5 = 2 1; molecular formula = C₄H₈Cl₂ 1

D6

A preparation of lead(II) bromide is expected to produce 0.150 mol of PbBr₂. The actual mass obtained is 49.6 g. Show that the percentage yield is about 90%. [Mr of PbBr₂ = 367] (2)

Show answer

Theoretical mass = 0.150 × 367 = 55.05 g 1; % yield = 49.6 ÷ 55.05 × 100 = 90.1% 1

Convert the expected moles to a mass first — the scheme rejected 49.6 ÷ 0.150. Actual mass over theoretical mass, always.

D7

The reaction of zinc with dilute nitric acid produces 0.081 g of hydrogen gas. One mole of hydrogen gas contains 6.0 × 10²³ molecules. Calculate the number of molecules of hydrogen produced. Give your answer to two significant figures. [Mr of H₂ = 2] (3)

Show answer

n(H₂) = 0.081 ÷ 2 = 0.0405 mol 1; molecules = 0.0405 × 6.0 × 10²³ = 2.43 × 10²² 1; to 2 sf: 2.4 × 10²² 1

Both instructions carry marks: standard form for the big number, and the stated 2 sf. Read the question twice — that is where 2024's marks went.
