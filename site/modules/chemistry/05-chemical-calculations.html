The mark schemes are unusually blunt about how these marks die:

"Division by atomic numbers or other inappropriate numbers scores 0/3. Fractions upside down scores 0/3." — 2021, empirical formula. Edexcel's 2022 scheme said the same.

"Did not read the question so did not leave the final answer to 2 sf. Large numbers should always be left in standard form." — 2024.

And the standing rule on every front page: correct answer without working scores full marks, but a wrong answer without working scores zero — show the moles step and the error-carried-forward rules protect you.

02How it actually works

Every calculation on these papers is the same journey: what you're given → moles → whatever was asked (grams of something else, a volume of gas, a concentration, a formula, a yield). The mole is the bridge in the middle; the balanced equation is the exchange rate. Each section below ends in a worked example laid out step by step — copy the layout in the exam.

Relative formula mass, Mr

Spec 1.26 · 2024 Q8 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q4(a) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q6(a)

Mr is the sum of the relative atomic masses of every atom in the formula.

Take the Ar values from the periodic table — the larger number in each box, not the atomic number.

Worked example — Mr of ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄

  1. Count every atom (the bracket doubles everything inside): N 2, H 8, S 1, O 4.
  2. Multiply each by its Ar: N 2 × 14 = 28; H 8 × 1 = 8; S 1 × 32 = 32; O 4 × 16 = 64.
  3. Add: 28 + 8 + 32 + 64 = 132.

More: Ca(OH)₂ = 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 74. Pb(NO₃)₂ = 207 + 2 × (14 + 48) = 331. Mr has no units.

The mole — n = mass ÷ Mr

Spec 1.27, 1.28 · every paper · 2024 Q10a

One mole of any substance has a mass equal to its Mr in grams, so dividing a mass by Mr gives the number of moles.

moles = mass ÷ Mr
moles in mol · mass in grams (g) · Mr from the periodic table

Rearranged: mass = moles × Mr. A mole is a counted amount: 6.0 × 10²³ particles. So number of particles = moles × 6.0 × 10²³ (the value is given in the question).

Worked example — how many molecules in 0.081 g of hydrogen? (2024)

  1. Mr of H₂ = 2 × 1 = 2.
  2. Moles = 0.081 ÷ 2 = 0.0405 mol.
  3. Molecules = 0.0405 × 6.0 × 10²³ = 2.43 × 10²².
  4. Round as asked (2 sf) and keep standard form: 2.4 × 10²².

Tonnes to grams: × 10⁶. Kilograms to grams: × 1000. Convert before dividing by Mr.

Reacting masses — using the equation

Spec 1.29 · 2021 Q6 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q7(c) · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q6(b)

The big numbers in a balanced equation are mole ratios, so you convert to moles, use the ratio, then convert back.

  1. Moles of what you know = mass ÷ Mr.
  2. Use the ratio from the balanced equation to get moles of what you want.
  3. Convert to what was asked: mass = moles × Mr.

Worked example — mass of CO₂ from 10.5 g of NaHCO₃ (2021)

2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂  ·  Mr: NaHCO₃ = 84, CO₂ = 44

  1. Moles of NaHCO₃ = 10.5 ÷ 84 = 0.125 mol.
  2. Ratio 2NaHCO₃ : 1CO₂, so moles of CO₂ = 0.125 ÷ 2 = 0.0625 mol.
  3. Mass of CO₂ = 0.0625 × 44 = 2.75 g.

Worked example — mass of Cl₂ to make 26.7 g of AlCl₃ (2023 Edexcel)

2Al + 3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃  ·  Mr: AlCl₃ = 133.5, Cl₂ = 71

  1. Moles of AlCl₃ = 26.7 ÷ 133.5 = 0.200 mol.
  2. Ratio 3Cl₂ : 2AlCl₃, so moles of Cl₂ = 0.200 × 3 ÷ 2 = 0.300 mol.
  3. Mass of Cl₂ = 0.300 × 71 = 21.3 g.

Which reactant is in excess?

Spec 1.29 · 2025 Q4c · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q10(a) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q11(a)

Compare the moles you need with the moles you have — never compare masses.

  1. Moles of each reactant you have.
  2. Use the ratio to find how many moles of one would be needed to react with all of the other.
  3. Compare: if you need more than you have, that reactant runs out (it is limiting); the other is in excess.

Worked example — 1.5 g of Mg with 0.1 mol of HCl (2025)

Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂  ·  Ar: Mg = 24

  1. Moles of Mg = 1.5 ÷ 24 = 0.0625 mol.
  2. Acid needed = 2 × 0.0625 = 0.125 mol.
  3. Compare: only 0.1 mol of acid is there, so the acid runs out and magnesium is in excess.

How much is left over? (2023 January Edexcel) 0.0250 mol of HNO₃ reacts with 0.0250 ÷ 2 = 0.0125 mol of Mg = 0.0125 × 24 = 0.30 g. Started with 0.75 g, so 0.45 g is left.

Gas volumes — 24 dm³ per mole

Spec 1.35C · 2022 Q8 · 2023 Q6 · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q6(a) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q4(c)

One mole of any gas takes up 24 dm³ (24 000 cm³) at room temperature and pressure.

volume = moles × 24 (dm³)  or  volume = moles × 24 000 (cm³)
and back again: moles = volume ÷ 24 (dm³) or volume ÷ 24 000 (cm³)

Check the unit on the answer line before you calculate. 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³.

Worked example — SO₂ from 6.4 tonnes of sulfur (2022 and 2023)

S + O₂ → SO₂  ·  Ar: S = 32

  1. Grams: 6.4 tonnes = 6.4 × 10⁶ g.
  2. Moles of S = 6.4 × 10⁶ ÷ 32 = 2.0 × 10⁵ mol.
  3. Ratio 1 : 1, so 2.0 × 10⁵ mol of SO₂.
  4. Volume = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 24 = 4 800 000 dm³.
  5. Standard form: 4.8 × 10⁶ dm³ — the third mark was just for this.

Concentration of a solution — mol/dm³

Spec 1.34C · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q6(b) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q7(c)

Concentration is the number of moles dissolved in each dm³ of solution.

concentration = moles ÷ volume
concentration in mol/dm³ · moles in mol · volume in dm³

Rearranged: moles = concentration × volume. Volumes are usually given in cm³, so divide by 1000 first: 25.0 cm³ = 0.0250 dm³.

Worked example — moles of NaBr in 25.0 cm³ of 2.0 mol/dm³ solution (2024 Edexcel)

  1. Volume in dm³ = 25.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0250 dm³.
  2. Moles = 2.0 × 0.0250 = 0.050 mol.

Then carry on as a reacting-mass question: Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2NaBr → PbBr₂ + 2NaNO₃, so PbBr₂ = 0.050 ÷ 2 = 0.025 mol → 0.025 × 367 = 9.175 g.

Worked example — concentration of 5.85 g of NaCl in 500 cm³

  1. Moles = 5.85 ÷ 58.5 = 0.100 mol.
  2. Volume = 500 ÷ 1000 = 0.500 dm³.
  3. Concentration = 0.100 ÷ 0.500 = 0.200 mol/dm³.

Titration calculations

Spec 1.34C · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q4(f) · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q2(c)

Use the solution whose volume and concentration you both know to find moles, then the ratio, then divide by the other volume.

  1. Moles of the known solution = concentration × (volume ÷ 1000).
  2. Ratio from the balanced equation → moles of the unknown.
  3. Concentration of the unknown = moles ÷ (its volume ÷ 1000).

Worked example — barium hydroxide (2022 Edexcel)

25.0 cm³ of barium hydroxide is neutralised by 21.5 cm³ of 0.600 mol/dm³ nitric acid.
2HNO₃ + Ba(OH)₂ → Ba(NO₃)₂ + 2H₂O

  1. Moles of HNO₃ = 0.600 × (21.5 ÷ 1000) = 0.0129 mol.
  2. Ratio 2HNO₃ : 1Ba(OH)₂, so Ba(OH)₂ = 0.0129 ÷ 2 = 0.00645 mol.
  3. Concentration = 0.00645 ÷ (25.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.258 mol/dm³.

The ratio step is where the marks go. Sulfuric acid is the other trap: H₂SO₄ + 2KOH, so moles of KOH = 2 × moles of acid (2023 Edexcel).

Percentage yield

Spec 1.30 · 2025 Q7b

Percentage yield compares the mass you actually made with the most you could have made.

% yield = (actual mass ÷ theoretical mass) × 100
actual mass = what you weighed · theoretical mass = from a reacting-mass calculation

Worked example — lead(II) bromide (2025)

Expected: 0.150 mol of PbBr₂ (Mr 367). Obtained: 49.6 g.

  1. Theoretical mass = 0.150 × 367 = 55.05 g.
  2. Divide actual by theoretical = 49.6 ÷ 55.05 = 0.901.
  3. × 100 = 90.1%.

The scheme rejected 49.6 ÷ 0.150 — a mass divided by moles means nothing. Both numbers must be masses.

Yield is always below 100% in practice: some product stays dissolved, is left on filter paper, or is lost in transfer; the reaction may not finish.

Empirical formula from masses or percentages

Spec 1.32, 1.33 · 2021 Q10a · 2025 Q10 · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q5(a) · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q4(a)

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms, found by turning each mass into moles of atoms.

  1. Divide each element's mass (or %) by its Ar — the relative atomic mass, never the atomic number, and never upside down.
  2. Divide every answer by the smallest one.
  3. If a number isn't close to whole, multiply them all up: ×2 for 1.5, ×3 for 1.33 or 1.67, ×4 for 1.25.
  4. Write the formula from the whole-number ratio.

Worked example — lead oxide, 90.7% Pb and 9.3% O (2025)

StepPbO
1. ÷ Ar90.7 ÷ 207 = 0.4389.3 ÷ 16 = 0.581
2. ÷ smallest (0.438)11.33
3. × 334

Formula: Pb₃O₄. Rounding 1.33 down to 1 would give PbO — wrong.

Worked example — C 40.0%, H 6.7%, O 53.3% (2022 Edexcel)

  1. ÷ Ar: C 40.0 ÷ 12 = 3.33; H 6.7 ÷ 1 = 6.7; O 53.3 ÷ 16 = 3.33.
  2. ÷ smallest (3.33): C 1, H 2.01, O 1.
  3. Formula: CH₂O.

2021's scheme: "Division by atomic numbers or other inappropriate numbers scores 0/3. Fractions upside down scores 0/3." If your first step is % ÷ Ar, you are safe; anything else scores nothing at all.

Molecular formula

Spec 1.32, 1.33 · 2022 Q9 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q4(a)

The molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula, found by comparing the Mr with the empirical mass.

  1. Work out the mass of the empirical formula.
  2. Divide the Mr you're given by it — you get a whole number.
  3. Multiply every number in the empirical formula by that.

Worked example — empirical formula C₂H₄Cl, Mr = 127 (2022)

  1. Empirical mass = (2 × 12) + (4 × 1) + 35.5 = 63.5.
  2. 127 ÷ 63.5 = 2.
  3. Molecular formula = C₄H₈Cl₂.

Going the other way (2024 Edexcel): caffeine C₈H₁₀N₄O₂ — every number divides by 2 → empirical formula C₄H₅N₂O.

Formula of a metal oxide by experiment

Spec 1.31, 1.36 (practical) · 2024 Q5b, Q7

Weigh the metal and the oxide; the difference is the oxygen, and the two masses give the formula.

By combustion (magnesium oxide):

  1. Weigh an empty crucible and lid, then with the magnesium ribbon inside.
  2. Heat strongly, lifting the lid now and then to let oxygen in — but not so far that white MgO smoke escapes.
  3. Cool and weigh. Reheat and reweigh until the mass is constant — that shows all the magnesium has reacted.
  4. Mass gained = mass of oxygen.

By reduction (copper(II) oxide): heat the oxide in a stream of hydrogen or methane; the mass falls as oxygen is removed, leaving copper. Heat to constant mass.

Worked example — copper oxide reduced

3.18 g of copper oxide leaves 2.54 g of copper. Ar: Cu = 63.5, O = 16.

  1. Mass of oxygen = 3.18 − 2.54 = 0.64 g.
  2. Moles: Cu 2.54 ÷ 63.5 = 0.040; O 0.64 ÷ 16 = 0.040.
  3. Ratio 1 : 1 → CuO.

Water of crystallisation — finding x

Spec 1.31 · 2021 Q7 · 2022 Q6 · 2025 Q2b · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q12 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q9(b)

Heat the hydrated salt until all the water has gone; the mass lost is the water, and comparing moles of water with moles of salt gives x.

  1. Heat to constant mass — reheat and reweigh until the mass stops changing, so all the water is removed.
  2. Mass of anhydrous salt = mass after heating − mass of the empty container.
  3. Mass of water = mass before heating − mass after heating.
  4. Moles of each: salt ÷ its Mr; water ÷ 18.
  5. x = moles of water ÷ moles of salt.

Worked example — Na₂CO₃·xH₂O (2022)

Anhydrous Na₂CO₃ left = 3.18 g. Water lost = 3.78 g. Mr: Na₂CO₃ = 106, H₂O = 18.

  1. Moles of Na₂CO₃ = 3.18 ÷ 106 = 0.0300 mol.
  2. Moles of H₂O = 3.78 ÷ 18 = 0.210 mol.
  3. x = 0.210 ÷ 0.0300 = 7 → Na₂CO₃·7H₂O.

Every year's follow-up: why might x come out too low? — not heated for long enough, so not all the water was removed. How to improve? — heat to constant mass. To check the liquid given off is water: it boils at exactly 100 °C, or turns anhydrous copper(II) sulfate from white to blue.

Ar from isotopes, and percentage by mass

Spec 1.17 · 2023 Q9a · 2023 Q6c · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q10(c)

Both are "weighted" sums: Ar weights each isotope by its abundance; percentage by mass takes one element's share of the Mr.

Ar = Σ(isotope mass × % abundance) ÷ 100
Copper: (63 × 69.5 + 65 × 30.5) ÷ 100 = 63.61 → 63.6. Module 1 has the full method.

Worked example — % of nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃ (2022 Edexcel)

  1. Mr = 14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = 80.
  2. Mass of nitrogen in the formula = 2 × 14 = 28. Count every N atom.
  3. % N = 28 ÷ 80 × 100 = 35%.

Same method for (NH₄)₂SO₄: 28 ÷ 132 × 100 = 21.2% — the 2 outside the bracket doubles the N.

04Understanding it

U1

Why does one mole of CO₂ (44 g) and one mole of H₂ (2 g) — wildly different masses — occupy exactly the same volume as gases at room temperature and pressure?

Show answer

Because in a gas the particles are so far apart that the volume is almost entirely empty space — the size and mass of the individual molecules barely matter. What sets the volume is the number of particles (and the temperature and pressure), and one mole of anything is the same number of particles: 6.0 × 10²³.

That is the whole reason the molar volume shortcut works: equal moles of any gas → equal volumes → 24 dm³ per mole at rtp. It works for gases only; solids and liquids have their particles touching, so their volumes depend on what they are.

U2

The empirical formula method says "divide the percentage by the Ar". What is that step actually calculating, and why does dividing by the atomic number score zero?

Show answer

It converts a mass ratio into a mole ratio — % of mass ÷ mass-per-mole = relative number of moles of atoms. In 100 g of the lead oxide, 90.7 g ÷ 207 g/mol = 0.438 mol of lead atoms. A formula is a ratio of atoms counted, not of grams, so you must get to moles before comparing.

The atomic number is the count of protons — nothing to do with mass. Dividing by it produces a meaningless number, which is why the scheme gives 0/3 rather than just losing one mark: the method itself is wrong, not the arithmetic.

U3

In the water-of-crystallisation practical, the value of x comes out too low even though every weighing was accurate. What happened, how would you detect it, and how do you fix it?

Show answer

What happened: the crystals were not heated for long enough, so not all the water of crystallisation was driven off. Some water is still in the "anhydrous" mass, making the salt look heavier and the water lighter — the ratio of water to salt shrinks, so x shrinks.

Detection and fix are the same idea: heat again, cool, reweigh — and repeat until the mass no longer changes. Constant mass is the proof that all the water is gone; a mass still falling means there was more water to lose.

The trap in reverse: if the question says x came out too HIGH, look for the salt decomposing or spitting out of the crucible — mass lost that wasn't water.

U4

A question gives you 1.5 g of magnesium and 0.1 mol of hydrochloric acid, and asks you to "show that the magnesium is in excess" (Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂). Why can't you just compare 1.5 g with the acid's mass — and what is the correct comparison?

Show answer

Grams of different substances can't be compared — a gram of magnesium contains a different number of particles from a gram of HCl, and the equation works in particles, not grams.

The correct route: 1.5 ÷ 24 = 0.0625 mol Mg. The ratio is 1 : 2, so reacting all of it needs 2 × 0.0625 = 0.125 mol of acid. Only 0.1 mol is available — the acid runs out first, so the magnesium is in excess.

The template for every excess/"show that" question: moles of both → scale one by the ratio → compare needed with available, in moles.

U5

Why is the percentage yield of a real preparation always less than 100% — and why does the exam's yield formula use two masses rather than two amounts in moles?

Show answer

Less than 100%: product is physically lost at every step — some stays dissolved in the solution after crystallisation, some is left on the filter paper or washed away, and the reaction may not go to completion. (In 2025's PbBr₂ preparation, ~10% went missing, largely in filtering and washing.)

Why masses: actual ÷ theoretical only makes sense when both are the same kind of quantity. You weigh the actual product, so the theoretical value must be converted all the way to a mass (moles expected × Mr) before dividing. The 2025 scheme specifically rejected 49.6 ÷ 0.150 — a mass divided by moles is a nonsense number.

U6

Chlorine's Ar is 35.5 — not a whole number, though every single chlorine atom has a whole-number mass. Explain, and state what a 75% ³⁵Cl / 25% ³⁷Cl mixture gives.

Show answer

Ar is a weighted average over the isotopes as they occur naturally, not the mass of any one atom. Individual atoms are ³⁵Cl or ³⁷Cl; the average of a big natural sample sits in between, pulled towards the more abundant one.

(35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100 = (2625 + 925) ÷ 100 = 35.5.

Sanity check that catches silly errors: the answer must lie between the isotope masses, nearer the more abundant isotope. 2023's copper answer (63.6, close to 63 at 69.5%) passes exactly that check.

U7

2023 gave a solid metal carbonate XCO₃ that decomposed to give 0.05 mol of CO₂ from 7.40 g. Walk the route from those two numbers to the identity of X, and say where the periodic table comes in.

Show answer

Three steps, each one mark:

  1. Ratio: XCO₃ → XO + CO₂ is 1 : 1, so moles of XCO₃ = moles of CO₂ = 0.05.
  2. Mr from the definition of moles: Mr = mass ÷ n = 7.40 ÷ 0.05 = 148.
  3. Subtract the known part: CO₃ contributes 60, so Ar(X) = 148 − 60 = 88. The periodic table's Group 2 metal with Ar 88 is strontium.

This is the pattern for every identify-the-element question: get moles, get Mr, take away what you know, look up what remains — using the question's own clue (a Group 2 metal) to pick the match.

U8

In a titration, 0.0027 mol of sulfuric acid neutralises 25.0 cm³ of potassium hydroxide. A student writes "concentration = 0.0027 ÷ 25.0 = 0.000108 mol/dm³". Find both mistakes.

Show answer

Mistake 1 — the ratio. H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O. Each acid molecule gives two H⁺, so it neutralises two KOH. Moles of KOH = 2 × 0.0027 = 0.0054 mol.

Mistake 2 — the units. Concentration is per dm³, so the volume must be 25.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0250 dm³.

Correct: 0.0054 ÷ 0.0250 = 0.216 mol/dm³ (2023 Edexcel 2C Q4). A sanity check helps: bench solutions are usually between 0.1 and 2 mol/dm³ — an answer of 0.000108 should look wrong straight away.

05Exam drill

D1

Sodium hydrogencarbonate decomposes on heating: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂

(a) Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced by completely decomposing 10.5 g of NaHCO₃. [Mr of NaHCO₃ = 84; Mr of CO₂ = 44] (2)
(b) Calculate the volume of this carbon dioxide at rtp, in cm³. [1 mol of gas = 24,000 cm³ at rtp] (2)

Show answer

(a) n(NaHCO₃) = 10.5 ÷ 84 = 0.125 mol → ratio 2 : 1 → n(CO₂) = 0.0625 mol 1; mass = 0.0625 × 44 = 2.75 g 1

(b) n(CO₂) = 0.0625 mol 1; volume = 0.0625 × 24,000 = 1500 cm³ 1 — error carried forward from (a) is allowed if the working is shown

D2

Hydrated iron(II) sulfate is FeSO₄·xH₂O. A sample is heated to constant mass in a tube: mass of tube 11.96 g, tube + crystals 17.56 g, tube + contents after heating 15.76 g.

(a) State why the crystals are heated to constant mass. (1)
(b) Calculate x, to the nearest whole number. [Mr of FeSO₄ = 152; Mr of H₂O = 18] (3)

Show answer

(a) To make sure all the water (of crystallisation) has been removed 1

(b) mass FeSO₄ = 15.76 − 11.96 = 3.80 g and mass H₂O = 17.56 − 15.76 = 1.80 g → n(FeSO₄) = 3.80 ÷ 152 = 0.025 1; n(H₂O) = 1.80 ÷ 18 = 0.10 1; x = 0.10 ÷ 0.025 = 4 1

D3

A compound of lead and oxygen is 90.7% lead and 9.3% oxygen by mass. Show by calculation that its empirical formula is Pb₃O₄. [Ar: Pb = 207, O = 16] (4)

Show answer

Pb: 90.7 ÷ 207 = 0.438 1; O: 9.3 ÷ 16 = 0.581 1; divide by the smaller: 1 : 1.33 1; multiply up: × 3 → 3 : 4 → Pb₃O₄ 1

Divide by Ar, never the atomic number; never round 1.33 down to 1 — a ratio near a third or a quarter is the question telling you to multiply up.

D4

A mass of 6.4 tonnes of sulfur is burned: S + O₂ → SO₂. Calculate the maximum volume, in dm³, of sulfur dioxide produced at rtp. Give your answer in standard form. [Ar of S = 32; molar volume = 24 dm³ at rtp; 1 tonne = 10⁶ g] (3)

Show answer

n(S) = 6.4 × 10⁶ ÷ 32 = 2.0 × 10⁵ mol 1; ratio 1 : 1, volume = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 24 = 4,800,000 dm³ 1; in standard form: 4.8 × 10⁶ dm³ 1

The last mark is only for the standard form — 4 800 000 written out scored 2 of 3. This exact question has appeared on two papers.

(a) Show that the empirical formula of G is C₂H₄Cl. [Ar: C = 12, H = 1, Cl = 35.5] (3)
(b) Determine the molecular formula of G. (2)

Show answer

(a) 37.8 ÷ 12 = 3.15; 6.3 ÷ 1 = 6.3; 55.9 ÷ 35.5 = 1.57 1 1; divide by smallest → 2 : 4 : 1 → C₂H₄Cl 1

D6

A preparation of lead(II) bromide is expected to produce 0.150 mol of PbBr₂. The actual mass obtained is 49.6 g. Show that the percentage yield is about 90%. [Mr of PbBr₂ = 367] (2)

Show answer

Theoretical mass = 0.150 × 367 = 55.05 g 1; % yield = 49.6 ÷ 55.05 × 100 = 90.1% 1

Convert the expected moles to a mass first — the scheme rejected 49.6 ÷ 0.150. Actual mass over theoretical mass, always.

D7
