02How it actually works

Chemical formulae are not memorised one by one — they are built from ion charges. Equations are then balanced by counting atoms, never by editing formulae. Two rules, used again and again.

Reading a formula — counting the atoms

Spec 1.25, 1.39 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q9(a)

A small number after a symbol counts that atom only; a small number after a bracket counts everything inside; a big number in front counts the whole formula.

  • H₂O — 2 hydrogen, 1 oxygen.
  • Ca(OH)₂ — 1 calcium, and the bracket doubles O and H: 2 oxygen, 2 hydrogen.
  • 3H₂O — three whole water molecules: 6 hydrogen, 3 oxygen.

Worked example — Na₂SO₄·7H₂O

  1. Different elements: Na, S, O, H → 4.
  2. Atoms before the dot: 2 Na + 1 S + 4 O = 7.
  3. Atoms after the dot: 7 × H₂O = 7 × 3 = 21.
  4. Total: 7 + 21 = 28 atoms.

The "·7H₂O" is water of crystallisation — seven water molecules for each Na₂SO₄. That was the 2023 Edexcel multiple choice, both parts.

Ion charges you can read off the table

Spec 1.38 · 2024 Q8a · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q8(a)

For Groups 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 and 7, the group number gives the charge.

Metals lose electrons (positive); non-metals gain them (negative). Module 1 explains why.

Ions you must learn by heart

Spec 1.38 · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q9(a) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q6(a)

Six metal ions and six other ions are on the spec list and cannot be worked out from the table — learn them.

  • Metal ions: Ag⁺ (silver), Zn²⁺ (zinc), Cu²⁺ (copper(II)), Pb²⁺ (lead(II)), Fe²⁺ (iron(II)) and Fe³⁺ (iron(III)).
  • Other ions: H⁺ (hydrogen), OH⁻ (hydroxide), NH₄⁺ (ammonium), NO₃⁻ (nitrate), CO₃²⁻ (carbonate), SO₄²⁻ (sulfate).

The Roman numeral in a name is the charge: iron(III) = Fe³⁺, copper(II) = Cu²⁺, lead(II) = Pb²⁺. Lead oxide, PbO, contains Pb²⁺ because O²⁻ is 2− — the 2022 Edexcel answer.

Building a formula — the charges must cancel

Spec 1.39 · 2023 Q4b · 2024 Q8a · 2025 Q6b · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q8(a)

A compound has no overall charge, so you take just enough of each ion for the positive and negative charges to cancel.

  1. Write the two ions with their charges. Aluminium oxide: Al³⁺ and O²⁻.
  2. Find the lowest number both charges go into. 3 and 2 → 6.
  3. Work out how many of each ion make that. 6 ÷ 3 = 2 Al³⁺; 6 ÷ 2 = 3 O²⁻.
  4. Write the numbers as subscripts. Al₂O₃.
  5. Check: 2 × (+3) = +6 and 3 × (−2) = −6. Total zero. ✓

The shortcut is to "cross" the charge numbers — each ion's charge becomes the other's subscript — then simplify. Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ cross to Mg₂O₂, which simplifies to MgO.

  • Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl
  • Mg²⁺ + Cl⁻ → MgCl₂
  • Mg²⁺ + N³⁻ → Mg₃N₂ (2024's table)
  • Ca²⁺ + P³⁻ → Ca₃P₂ (2025)

Brackets around compound ions

Spec 1.39 · 2023 Q4b · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q10(b) · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q7(d)

If you need more than one of an ion made of several atoms, put it in brackets before writing the number.

  1. Write the ions. Ammonium carbonate: NH₄⁺ and CO₃²⁻.
  2. Balance the charges. Two NH₄⁺ (2+) cancel one CO₃²⁻ (2−).
  3. Bracket the ion you need more than one of. (NH₄)₂CO₃.

Without brackets, NH₄₂CO₃ means 42 hydrogen atoms. Only one of the ion? No brackets: NH₄Cl, NaOH, CuSO₄. More examples: Ca(OH)₂, Mg(NO₃)₂, Al₂(SO₄)₃, Fe(OH)₃.

Naming compounds

Spec 1.39 · 2023 Q4b · 2024 Q8 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q8(a)(ii)

The metal (or ammonium) is named first, unchanged; the non-metal part comes second with its ending changed.

  • -ide = only the one non-metal element: chloride (Cl⁻), oxide (O²⁻), nitride (N³⁻), phosphide (P³⁻).
  • -ate = the element plus oxygen: sulfate (SO₄²⁻), nitrate (NO₃⁻), carbonate (CO₃²⁻).
  • Hydroxide = OH⁻; ammonium = NH₄⁺.
  • Roman numerals for metals with more than one ion: iron(II) chloride FeCl₂, iron(III) chloride FeCl₃.

MgO = magnesium oxide. NH₄Cl = ammonium chloride. Spelling is marked — the 2024 scheme said the name's "spelling must be correct".

Elements that exist as molecules of two atoms

Spec 1.25 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q2(a)

Seven elements are always written as pairs in equations: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂.

Every other element is written as a single symbol: Mg, Fe, C, S, Na. So hydrogen gas is H₂, never H; chlorine is Cl₂, never Cl. A lone "Cl" or "O" in an equation loses the formula mark.

Balancing — change the big numbers, never the formulae

Spec 1.25 · 2024 Q5a · 2025 Q4b · 2023 Q9b · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q7(c)

A balanced equation has the same number of each type of atom on both sides, and you balance it only by putting numbers in front of formulae.

Worked example — magnesium burning: Mg + O₂ → MgO

  1. Write correct formulae. Mg + O₂ → MgO. These are now fixed — never change a subscript.
  2. Count each element on both sides. Mg: 1 left, 1 right. O: 2 left, 1 right. Oxygen is unbalanced.
  3. Fix one element with a big number. Put 2 in front of MgO: Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Now O: 2 = 2.
  4. Recount everything. Mg: 1 left, 2 right. Put 2 in front of Mg.
  5. Final check. 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Mg 2 = 2, O 2 = 2. ✓

Tip: balance elements that appear on their own (O₂, C, Mg) last — changing their number doesn't upset anything else.

The school's set pieces: 2Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe · 2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂ · Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + 8C → Ca₃P₂ + 8CO · Ca₃P₂ + 6H₂O → 3Ca(OH)₂ + 2PH₃. Schemes usually give one mark for formulae and one for balancing, and "ALLOW multiples".

Harder balancing — brackets and burning

Spec 1.25 · 2024 Q8b · 2025 Q6c–d · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q7(c)

Count brackets and water molecules carefully, and leave oxygen to the very end.

Worked example — ethane burning: C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

  1. Carbon first. 2 C on the left → 2CO₂.
  2. Hydrogen next. 6 H on the left → 3H₂O.
  3. Now count the oxygen on the right. 2CO₂ = 4 O, 3H₂O = 3 O. Total 7.
  4. Balance O₂ last. 7 ÷ 2 = 3½ O₂. That gives C₂H₆ + 3½O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O.
  5. Remove the half by doubling everything. 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O. Recount: C 4 = 4, H 12 = 12, O 14 = 14. ✓

Both versions score — Edexcel accepts the ½.

Brackets: in Ca₃(PO₄)₂ there are 2 × 4 = 8 oxygens. All eight leave as CO, so you need 8CO and 8C. The 2024 Edexcel nitric acid equation balances as 4NO₂ + 2H₂O + O₂ → 4HNO₃: N 4 = 4, H 4 = 4, O 8 + 2 + 2 = 12 = 12.

State symbols — and which one is the real test

Spec 1.25 · 2021 Q8a · 2022 Q3b · 2024 Q10a · 2025 Q4b · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q6(a) · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q9(a)

A state symbol says what each substance is physically like in the flask: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water.

The papers' favourite: CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).

  1. Water is (l) — it is the liquid itself, not dissolved in anything.
  2. Acids, alkalis and dissolved salts are (aq). 2024's commentary: many wrote zinc nitrate as (s) or (l); it is a soluble salt in solution. Check the solubility rules in module 4.
  3. Precipitates are (s) — insoluble products are solid even though they form in solution: PbCl₂(s), BaSO₄(s).
  4. Gases are (g): H₂, O₂, CO₂, Cl₂.
  5. Metals and solid oxides are (s): Mg(s), CuO(s), PbO(s).

Dissolving a solid in water is X(s) → X(aq) — the 2023 January Edexcel multiple choice.

Equations for reactions you have never seen

Spec 1.25 · 2025 Q6d · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q5(b)

The question gives you every formula you can't work out, so copy them exactly and just balance.

Worked example — calcium phosphide + water → calcium hydroxide + phosphine (PH₃)

  1. Formulae: Ca₃P₂ (from the charges), H₂O, Ca(OH)₂ (Ca²⁺ with two OH⁻), PH₃ (given).
  2. Balance the element that appears in one place on each side: 3 Ca → 3Ca(OH)₂; 2 P → 2PH₃.
  3. Count H and O on the right: 3Ca(OH)₂ has 6 O and 6 H; 2PH₃ has 6 H. So 6 O and 12 H.
  4. Balance water: 6H₂O gives 6 O and 12 H. ✓
  5. Answer: Ca₃P₂ + 6H₂O → 3Ca(OH)₂ + 2PH₃

2022 Edexcel did the same with methanoic acid (HCOOH) and sodium carbonate: the pattern "acid + carbonate → salt + water + CO₂" gives 2HCOOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2HCOONa + CO₂ + H₂O.

04Understanding it

U1

Why is magnesium chloride MgCl₂ but sodium chloride just NaCl? Explain from the charges, and state what the "crossing trick" is actually doing.

Show answer

Compounds are electrically neutral. Mg²⁺ carries two positives, and each Cl⁻ only one negative — so it takes two chloride ions to cancel one magnesium: MgCl₂. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ cancel one-for-one: NaCl.

The crossing trick (charge of one ion becomes subscript of the other) is just a quick way of making total + equal total −. That is why the result must always be simplified (Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ "cross" to Mg₂O₂ → reduce to MgO).

U2

A student balances Mg + O₂ → MgO by rewriting it as Mg + O₂ → MgO₂. Chemically, what has the student done wrong — and what is the legal fix?

Show answer

They have invented a different substance. MgO₂ is not magnesium oxide — the formula of a compound is fixed by its ion charges (Mg²⁺, O²⁻ → MgO) and cannot change during balancing. Editing subscripts changes what reacts, not how much.

The legal fix uses big numbers only: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO — two Mg and two O on each side, formulae untouched. (With state symbols, 2025's version: 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s).)

U3

In Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + 8C → Ca₃P₂ + 8CO, show the oxygen count that makes 8 the right number for both carbon and CO.

Show answer

Left side oxygen: (PO₄)₂ = 2 × 4 = 8 oxygens. Calcium (3) and phosphorus (2) already match Ca₃P₂ — so all 8 oxygens must leave inside CO, one each: 8CO. Eight CO needs eight carbons: 8C.

Recount: Ca 3 = 3, P 2 = 2, O 8 = 8, C 8 = 8. ✓ The general skill: brackets multiply everything inside, and a careful count of each element beats guessing. This equation appeared two years running (2024, 2025).

U4

The same reaction — zinc + nitric acid → zinc nitrate + hydrogen — gets four state symbols. Give them, and explain the two that students got wrong in 2024.

Show answer

Zn(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Zn(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂(g).

The two casualties: zinc nitrate, widely written (s) or (l). It is a soluble salt (all nitrates are soluble) sitting dissolved in the solution it formed in — (aq). It only becomes (s) after you deliberately crystallise it. And the acid: dilute acid is a solution, always (aq), never (l).

The habit: for every substance ask where is it, physically, in the flask? — not what it could be in a bottle somewhere.

U5

Ammonia reacts with sulfuric acid to give ammonium sulfate. Build the product's formula from its ions, then write and balance the full equation.

Show answer

Formula: NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻ — two ammoniums cancel one sulfate → (NH₄)₂SO₄, brackets because the compound ion is doubled.

Equation: the product contains two NH₄ groups, so two ammonia molecules are needed: 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄. Count: N 2 = 2, H (6 + 2) = 8 = 8, S 1 = 1, O 4 = 4. ✓

(2023 asked exactly this, copied from 2022 Edexcel 1C Q10 — the balancing 2 was required.)

U6

"2H₂O" and "H₄O₂" contain the same atoms. Why is only one of them acceptable in an equation?

Show answer

"2H₂O" means two water molecules, each H₂O. "H₄O₂" claims to be one molecule with four hydrogens and two oxygens — a substance that doesn't exist.

The big number counts how many of a substance; the small numbers say what the substance is. Balancing changes amounts, so it may only touch the big numbers. That's why the formulae are written first and then frozen.

U7

Iron forms two chlorides, FeCl₂ and FeCl₃. Why does iron need a Roman numeral in its name when sodium never does?

Show answer

Sodium is in Group 1 and only ever forms Na⁺, so "sodium chloride" can only mean NaCl.

Iron is a transition metal and forms two ions, Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺. "Iron chloride" could be either. The Roman numeral gives the charge: iron(II) chloride = Fe²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ = FeCl₂; iron(III) chloride = Fe³⁺ + 3Cl⁻ = FeCl₃. Same for copper(II) and lead(II) — learn them with the numeral attached.

U8

Lead(II) nitrate solution and potassium chloride solution are mixed and a solid forms. Explain how you would know which state symbol each substance gets, without being told.

Show answer

Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KCl(aq) → PbCl₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq).

Both reactants are described as solutions, so both are (aq). For the products, use the solubility rules: chlorides are soluble except silver and lead(II), so PbCl₂ is insoluble — the solid that formed, (s). Potassium salts and nitrates are always soluble, so KNO₃ stays dissolved, (aq).

State symbols are chemistry, not guesswork: the solubility rules decide (s) or (aq) for every salt.
