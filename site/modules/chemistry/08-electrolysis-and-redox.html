02How it actually works

Electrolysis is using electricity to break down a compound. Everything on these papers comes down to three questions: can it conduct? — which ions go to which electrode? — which ion wins at each electrode? Each one has a fixed answer pattern, and the headings below take them in order.

Why things conduct — and why solids never do

Spec 1.55C, 1.56C · 2023 Q2b · 2024 Q6a · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q4(c) · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q6(c)

To conduct, a substance needs charged particles that are free to move. Ask which particle carries the charge, and whether it can move.

  • Metals conduct as solids and liquids: their delocalised electrons move through the structure.
  • Ionic compounds conduct only when molten or dissolved. In the solid the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice and cannot move. Melt or dissolve it and the ions are free to move.
  • Covalent compounds do not conduct: they are made of molecules, so there are no ions and no free electrons — no charged particles at all.

Mark script — "Explain why magnesium conducts but magnesium chloride only when molten" (2022 Edexcel 2C Q4, and the school's 2023 Q2)

1. Magnesium has delocalised electrons 1

2. which can move through the structure 1

3. Magnesium chloride conducts only when molten or in solution 1

4. because then its ions are free to move 1

✗ "Solid sodium chloride doesn't conduct because the electrons can't move." — the school's 2024 scheme gave nothing for electron answers about an ionic compound.
✗ "The ions in magnesium move." — the metal conducts by electrons, not ions. The 2023 scheme rejected this too.
✓ "In the solid the ions are in fixed positions and cannot move. When molten, the ions are free to move and carry the charge."

Anions, cations and the two electrodes

Spec 1.57C · 2024 Q6b · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q5(b) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q5(b)(ii)

Opposite charges attract: positive ions go to the negative electrode, negative ions go to the positive electrode.

IonChargeGoes toWhat happens there
Cation — metal ions and H⁺PositiveCathode (negative electrode)Gains electrons — reduction

A memory hook: a cation is "pawsitive" — cats have paws. And PANiC: Positive is the Anode, Negative is the Cathode.

"Explain how a metal forms at the negative electrode" is always the same chain:

  1. The metal ions are positive (cations).
  2. They are attracted to the negative electrode (cathode).
  3. There each ion gains electrons — it is reduced.
  4. It becomes a metal atom.

Mark script — "Explain how copper forms at the negative electrode" (2023 Edexcel 2C Q5(b), 3 marks from 4 points)

1. Copper ions are positive / Cu²⁺ 1

2. They are attracted to the negative electrode 1

3. where they gain (accept) electrons 1

4. and become copper atoms (any three of these four)

✗ "Sodium atoms move to the negative electrode." — they are not atoms until they get there. The 2024 school scheme rejected this.
✓ "Positive sodium ions are attracted to the negative electrode, where each ion gains an electron to form a sodium atom."

OIL RIG — oxidation and reduction at the electrodes

Spec 1.59C, 2.20 · 2022 Q5a · 2023 Q2c · 2025 Q7d · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q5(d)(iii) · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q6(d)(ii)

Oxidation Is Loss of electrons; Reduction Is Gain of electrons — and it is always the ion that gains or loses them.

OIL RIGOxidation Is Loss · Reduction Is Gainof electrons — the cathode always does reduction, the anode always does oxidation

So "why is this reaction an oxidation?" is a one-line answer. Look at the half-equation: electrons on the right means they were lost, so it is oxidation. Electrons on the left means they were gained, so it is reduction.

  1. Find which side the e⁻ is on.
  2. Right side → electrons lost → oxidation. Left side → electrons gained → reduction.
  3. Name the ion that did it: "the bromide ions lose electrons", "the magnesium ion gains electrons".

✗ "Bromine loses electrons." — bromine is the product. Rejected in 2025 (school) and in 2024 Edexcel 2C.
✗ "Magnesium gains electrons." — "The magnesium ION gains electrons, not the magnesium!!"
✓ "It is oxidation because the bromide ions lose electrons."
✓ "It is reduction because the magnesium ions gain electrons."

Building a half-equation, step by step

Spec 1.59C · every school paper · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q5(d)(ii) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q6(b)(ii)

Balance the atoms first, then balance the charge by adding electrons. Follow the same five steps every time and the "fascinating variations" disappear.

  1. Write the start and finish. The ion on the left, the product on the right. Halogens are diatomic (Cl₂, Br₂). Oxygen in a solution comes from water.
  2. Balance the atoms with big numbers in front.
  3. Add up the charge on each side.
  4. Add electrons (e⁻) to the more positive side until the two totals match.
  5. Check: atoms equal, charges equal. Cathode equations have e⁻ on the left; anode equations have e⁻ on the right.

Worked example 1 — bromide ions at the anode

Step 1: Br⁻ → Br₂

Step 2: two Br atoms on the right, so 2Br⁻ → Br₂

Step 3: left charge = 2 × (−1) = −2 · right charge = 0

Step 4: the right is more positive, so add 2e⁻ there: 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻

Step 5: −2 = −2 ✓ · electrons on the right, so it is oxidation ✓

Worked example 2 — lead ions at the cathode

Step 1–2: Pb²⁺ → Pb — one lead atom each side already

Step 3: left charge = +2 · right charge = 0

Step 4: the left is more positive, so add 2e⁻ there: Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb

Step 5: 0 = 0 ✓ · electrons on the left, so it is reduction ✓

Worked example 3 — oxygen from water at the anode (the one everyone drops)

Step 1: H₂O → O₂ + H⁺ — water is what is really there

Step 2a: balance O: 2H₂O → O₂ + H⁺

Step 2b: balance H: 4 H on the left, so 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺

Step 3: left charge = 0 · right charge = +4

Step 4: add 4e⁻ to the right: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻

Step 5: 0 = +4 − 4 ✓ · also accepted: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻

✗ Cl⁻ → Cl + e⁻, or Cl₃, or Cl⁺ — chlorine is diatomic.
✗ 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ in a solution — there are no O²⁻ ions in a solution. The 2023 year group's favourite wrong answer.
✓ 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ · 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻

The Edexcel schemes give two marks for the water equation: one for all the right formulae, one for the balancing. Correct formulae alone still earn a mark.

The half-equations you will actually write

Spec 1.59C · every paper

Only about eight half-equations ever come up — learn them as a bank.

At the cathode (reduction — electrons on the left):

  • Metals: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu · Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb · Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (molten) · Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg
  • Hydrogen from a solution: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂

At the anode (oxidation — electrons on the right):

  • Halogens: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ · 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻
  • Oxygen from a solution: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ (or 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻)

The number of electrons always equals the charge on the ion × the number of ions. Cu²⁺ needs 2; two Cl⁻ give up 2; Na⁺ needs 1.

Molten compounds — lead(II) bromide

Spec 1.58C · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q6(c)–(d) · 2025 Q7d

A molten compound has only two ions, so the metal forms at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode — no choice to make.

  1. Put solid lead(II) bromide in a crucible with two graphite electrodes, connected to a d.c. supply and a bulb or ammeter.
  2. Heat strongly, in a fume cupboard. Nothing happens while it is solid — the bulb stays off.
  3. Once it melts, the ions can move and the bulb lights.
  4. Anode (+): Br⁻ ions lose electrons — brown vapour of bromine: 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻.
  5. Cathode (−): Pb²⁺ ions gain electrons — molten lead, a silvery liquid, collects under the electrode: Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb.

Why graphite? It is inert (does not react with the products), it conducts, and it has a very high melting point, so it survives the heat. Why a fume cupboard? Bromine vapour is toxic.

✗ "Bromine is seen at the positive electrode." — that is a name, not an observation.
✓ "A brown vapour is seen at the positive electrode." — this is what the 2024 Edexcel scheme accepted.

Solutions: how to decide the product at the cathode

Spec 1.58C · 2022 Q5b · 2024 Q6b(iv) · 2025 Q3b · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q5(b)(iv)

At the cathode, the less reactive of the metal and hydrogen is the one that forms. A solution always has H⁺ ions from water as well as the metal ions, so the two compete.

  1. Molten? Then the metal forms. Stop.
  2. In solution? List the positive ions: the metal ion and H⁺ from water.
  3. Find the metal in the reactivity series: K, Na, Li, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, (H) , Cu, Ag, Au.
  4. Metal below hydrogen (copper, silver) → the metal is deposited.
  5. Metal above hydrogen (potassium, sodium, magnesium…) → hydrogen gas forms instead, and the metal ions stay in solution.

That is why sodium chloride solution gives hydrogen, not sodium. The one-mark answer: "sodium is more reactive than hydrogen" (or "hydrogen is less reactive than sodium").

Solutions: how to decide the product at the anode

Spec 1.58C · 2021 Q4 · 2022 Q5 · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q5(d)

At the anode, a halide gives its halogen; anything else gives oxygen from the water.

  1. Molten? Then the non-metal from the negative ion forms (Br₂, Cl₂). Stop.
  2. In solution? Is there a halide ion — chloride, bromide or iodide?
  3. Yes → the halogen forms: Cl₂ (pale green gas), Br₂ (orange-brown solution).
  4. No — sulfate or nitrate → oxygen forms, from the water: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻.

Sulfate and nitrate ions are never discharged at IGCSE. If the question gives you sulfuric acid or copper(II) sulfate, the anode gives oxygen.

Practical: electrolysing a solution and testing the gases

Spec 1.60C · 2021 Q4b · 2022 Q5 · 2025 Q3a · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q6(b)(i)

Collect each gas over its electrode, then identify it with the right test.

  1. Pour the solution into a beaker or electrolysis cell with two inert electrodes — graphite or platinum.
  2. Fill a test tube with the solution and turn it upside down over each electrode.
  3. Switch on the d.c. supply. Gas collects in each tube by displacing the solution.
  4. Note what you see at each electrode: bubbles, a coating, a colour.
  5. Test each gas.

Three follow-ups the papers love:

  • Why add dilute acid to water? Pure water is a very poor conductor; the acid increases its conductivity.
  • Why less oxygen (or chlorine) than expected? Some of it dissolves in the water.
  • Why a fume cupboard for NaCl(aq)? Chlorine is toxic.

Copper(II) sulfate — what you see and how it changes

Spec 1.58C, 1.60C · 2023 Q5 · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q5 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q6(a)

With inert electrodes, copper coats the cathode, oxygen bubbles at the anode, and the blue colour fades as the copper ions are used up.

  • Cathode: a pink / brown solid forms — Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu.
  • Anode: bubbles / effervescence — oxygen. The school's 2023 scheme ignored "oxygen" as an observation.
  • Solution: Cu²⁺ is removed, so the blue fades. H⁺ builds up with SO₄²⁻, so the solution becomes sulfuric acid.

2022 Jan Edexcel 2C gave a graph of the mass of the negative electrode against time. It rises, then goes flat. The two-mark explanation:

  1. The mass increases because copper is deposited on the electrode.
  2. The line goes flat because there are no copper(II) ions left in the solution.

2023 Edexcel 2C opened with a test for the copper(II) ions themselves: add sodium hydroxide solution → a blue precipitate (or a flame test → blue-green flame).

Calculations: moles of gas, not a special formula

Spec 1.35C (molar volume) with 1.59C · 2025 Q3a(v)

Electrolysis calculations on this course use ordinary moles: volume ÷ 24 000 cm³, then the ratio from the half-equations. The 4CH1 spec has no Faraday or charge calculations — do not learn any.

Moles of gas at rtpmoles = volume in cm³ ÷ 24 000or volume in dm³ ÷ 24 · then mass = moles × Mr

Worked example — mass of oxygen from its volume (the 2025 Q3 method)

17.8 cm³ of oxygen is collected at rtp. Calculate its mass.

Step 1: moles O₂ = 17.8 ÷ 24 000 = 0.000 742 mol

Step 2: Mr of O₂ = 2 × 16 = 32

Step 3: mass = 0.000 742 × 32 = 0.0237 g (3 s.f.)

Worked example — why twice as much hydrogen as oxygen

Step 1: anode: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ — 4 electrons make 1 O₂

Step 2: cathode: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ — so 4 electrons make 2 H₂

Step 3: the same electrons flow through both, so moles H₂ : O₂ = 2 : 1

Step 4: equal moles of gas have equal volumes, so if 36 cm³ of hydrogen collects, expect 18 cm³ of oxygen (a little less, because some dissolves).

Redox beyond the electrodes — oxygen and electrons

Spec 2.20 · 2021 Q10b · 2024 Q5a · 2025 Q4b · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q2(b)(iii) · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q9(a)(iii) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q5(c)

A redox reaction is one where oxidation and reduction happen together — and you must say which substance does which. The spec tests two definitions:

Oxygen definitionElectron definition
OxidationGain of oxygenLoss of electrons
ReductionLoss of oxygenGain of electrons
Oxidising agentGives oxygenAccepts electrons — is itself reduced
Reducing agentRemoves oxygenDonates electrons — is itself oxidised

How to answer "explain why this is a redox reaction":

  1. Find the substance that gains oxygen (or loses electrons) — it is oxidised.
  2. Find the substance that loses oxygen (or gains electrons) — it is reduced.
  3. Name both, using the reactant names — "iron(III) oxide", not "iron".

Mark script — thermite, 2Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe (2024 Edexcel 1C Q2(b)(iii), school 2024 Q5a)

1. Aluminium gains oxygen, so it is oxidised 1

2. Iron(III) oxide loses oxygen, so it is reduced 1

The same pattern did 2022 Edexcel 1C: in PbO + H₂ → Pb + H₂O, lead oxide loses oxygen (reduced) and hydrogen gains oxygen (oxidised).

In electron terms: in Mg + Cu²⁺ → Mg²⁺ + Cu, magnesium atoms lose two electrons (oxidised) and copper(II) ions gain them (reduced). Magnesium is the reducing agent because it donates electrons — exactly the two marks in 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q5(c).

✗ "Oxidation and reduction happen at the same time." — scored nothing in 2024. It names nobody.
✗ "Iron is reduced." — iron is the product. The 2024 teacher: many said IRON rather than IRON OXIDE.
✓ "Aluminium is oxidised because it gains oxygen; iron(III) oxide is reduced because it loses oxygen."

04Understanding it

U1

Solid sodium chloride sits between two connected electrodes and nothing happens. The moment it melts, a current flows and products appear. What changed — and why would the same experiment described with "electrons become free to move" score zero?

Show answer

Melting frees the ions. In the solid, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are locked in a lattice by their attractions; they vibrate but cannot travel, so no charge can flow. Molten, the ions move: Na⁺ to the cathode, Cl⁻ to the anode — the moving ions are the current inside the electrolyte.

"Electrons become free" is a different (wrong) conduction story — that is how metals conduct. An ionic compound never conducts by electron flow; the charge carriers are ions, and the mark schemes reject electron answers outright.

U2

Electrolysing concentrated sodium chloride solution gives hydrogen at the cathode, yet molten sodium chloride gives sodium. Explain the difference — and what universal indicator near the cathode of the solution would show.

Show answer

The solution contains a second cation: H⁺ from the water. At the cathode the less reactive species is discharged, and hydrogen is far less reactive than sodium — so H⁺ gains the electrons and H₂ bubbles off, while Na⁺ stays in solution. Molten NaCl has no water, no H⁺, no competition: sodium is the only choice.

Removing H⁺ leaves Na⁺ and OH⁻ behind — sodium hydroxide solution. Universal indicator near the cathode turns blue/purple, because hydroxide ions accumulate there. (2025's marker: most students never realised an alkali was forming, and few could name the OH⁻ ion as the reason.)

U3

Why must the oxygen half-equation in a solution start from water (2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻) when 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ balances perfectly well?

Show answer

Balancing isn't the test — existing is. A half-equation must start from a species actually present, and a solution of copper sulfate or dilute acid contains Cu²⁺, SO₄²⁻, H⁺, OH⁻ and H₂O — but no free O²⁻ ions.

The oxygen made at the anode really does come from water molecules (or equivalently the OH⁻ from water): 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻, or 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ — both accepted, because both start from what is really there. This exact wrong answer, 2O²⁻, was the year group's favourite in 2023.

U4

"The magnesium gains two electrons at the cathode." The teacher's comment on this sentence used two exclamation marks. What exactly is wrong with it, and what does the correct sentence look like?

Show answer

Before the electrons arrive there is no magnesium — there is a magnesium ion, Mg²⁺. The whole event at the cathode is the ion becoming magnesium: Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg. Saying "magnesium gains electrons" describes a neutral atom taking on electrons, which is not what happens and would make Mg²⁻.

Correct: "the magnesium ion gains two electrons (is reduced) to form a magnesium atom." The same precision is demanded at the anode: bromide ions lose electrons; bromine doesn't lose anything — it is the product.

U5

In the water-electrolysis apparatus, twice the volume of gas collects over the cathode as over the anode — but the oxygen tube is always slightly under half. Explain both facts.

Show answer

The 2 : 1 ratio: the anode needs 4 electrons to make one O₂; the same 4 electrons at the cathode make two H₂. Twice the moles of hydrogen means twice the volume, because equal moles of gas occupy equal volumes.

The shortfall: some of the oxygen dissolves in the water (oxygen is slightly soluble; the same reason less chlorine than hydrogen collects from NaCl(aq) — chlorine dissolves too). Not a leak, not an error — the accepted one-marker is "some oxygen dissolves".

Related staple: why add dilute acid to the water first? Pure water is a poor conductor; the acid increases its conductivity. (Not "helps carry the current" — name the property.)

U6

The thermite reaction is 2Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe. The 2024 marker complained that "many just said oxidation and reduction are occurring at the same time" and that "many said IRON was reduced rather than IRON OXIDE". Write the answer that scores both marks.

Show answer

"Aluminium is oxidised because it gains oxygen (→ Al₂O₃), and iron(III) oxide is reduced because it loses oxygen (→ Fe)."

Two named species, two named directions, each tied to oxygen. The two rejected shapes: (1) restating the definition of redox without naming anything; (2) "iron is reduced" — iron is the product; the thing that lost oxygen is the oxide. The electron version also scores: iron(III) ions gain electrons.

Bonus connection: aluminium here is the reducing agent — it removes the oxygen — exactly the role carbon plays in extracting iron in the metal-extraction module.

U7

Copper(II) sulfate solution is electrolysed with inert graphite electrodes. Predict what happens at each electrode, what you would see, and what the solution contains at the end.

Show answer

Cathode: copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu — a pink/brown coating of copper grows on the electrode.

Anode: no halide present, so oxygen from water: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ — you see effervescence/bubbles (that is the observation; "oxygen" is a name, not a sight).

End state: Cu²⁺ is progressively removed, so the blue colour fades; H⁺ accumulates with the SO₄²⁻, leaving dilute sulfuric acid. This is 2021's products-table row and 2023 Q5 in one story.

U8

A student weighs the negative electrode every five minutes while copper(II) sulfate solution is electrolysed. The mass rises steadily, then stops changing even though the current is still on. Why does it stop — and what is still happening in the cell?

Show answer

Why it rises: Cu²⁺ ions gain electrons at the cathode and copper is deposited, so the electrode gets heavier.

Why it stops: there are no copper(II) ions left in the solution — all the copper has been deposited. The blue colour will have gone too. (This is the 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C mark scheme, word for word.)

What carries on: the current still flows, but now the only cations left are H⁺ ions. So hydrogen bubbles off at the cathode, and oxygen keeps forming at the anode — the cell is now just electrolysing the sulfuric acid, which means electrolysing water. Running the cathode flowchart again with "no copper left" gives exactly this answer.

D2

Sodium metal is extracted by electrolysing molten sodium chloride.

(a) Explain why solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity but molten sodium chloride does. (2)
(b) Explain how sodium metal forms at the negative electrode. (2)

Show answer

(a) In the solid the ions are fixed in the lattice / cannot move 1; when molten the ions are free to move (and carry the charge) 1 — no marks for electron-based answers

(b) Positive sodium ions are attracted to the negative electrode 1; each ion gains an electron / is reduced (Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na) 1

(c) Sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so hydrogen is discharged instead 1

D3

Dilute sulfuric acid is electrolysed in a Hofmann voltameter with platinum electrodes.

D4

These aqueous solutions are electrolysed under the same conditions with inert electrodes. Complete the table. (4)

copper(II) sulfate: copper · oxygen · sulfuric acid 1

potassium sulfate: hydrogen · oxygen · potassium sulfate (water is electrolysed) 1

D6

The reaction 2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂ is used to extract copper.

(a) Explain why this is a redox reaction, naming the species oxidised and the species reduced. (2)

D7

When copper(II) sulfate solution is electrolysed with inert electrodes, copper forms at the negative electrode.

(a) Describe a test to show that the solution contains copper(II) ions. (2)

D8

A student electrolyses copper(II) sulfate solution and records the mass of the negative electrode every 10 minutes. The graph of mass against time rises, then becomes horizontal.

D9

Magnesium is a metal. Magnesium chloride is an ionic compound.

(a) Explain why magnesium conducts electricity when solid, but magnesium chloride conducts only when molten or in solution. (4)
(b) When molten magnesium chloride is electrolysed, this reaction happens at the cathode: Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg. Explain why this is a reduction. (1)

D10

Lead(II) bromide is heated until it melts, then electrolysed with graphite electrodes.

D11

Complete and balance each half-equation, then answer part (d).

(a) ......Br⁻ → Br₂ + ......e⁻ (1)
(b) Ag⁺ + ...... → Ag (1)
(c) ......H⁺ + ......e⁻ → H₂ (1)
(d) State which of the half-equations (a), (b) and (c) are reductions, and give the reason. (2)

Show answer

(a) 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ 1

(b) Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag 1

(c) 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ 1

D14

Water containing a little dilute sulfuric acid is electrolysed. 36.0 cm³ of hydrogen is collected at room temperature and pressure. [1 mol of gas occupies 24 000 cm³ at rtp; Mr of O₂ = 32]

(a) Calculate the number of moles of hydrogen collected. (1)
(b) Use the half-equations 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ and 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ to explain what volume of oxygen would be expected. (2)
(c) Calculate the mass of this volume of oxygen. (2)

Show answer

(a) 36.0 ÷ 24 000 = 0.001 50 mol 1

(b) 4 electrons make 2H₂ but only 1 O₂, so the ratio H₂ : O₂ is 2 : 1 1; so 18.0 cm³ of oxygen 1

(c) Moles O₂ = 18.0 ÷ 24 000 = 0.000 750 mol 1; mass = 0.000 750 × 32 = 0.024 g 1

Same method as the school's 2025 Q3(a)(v). There are no Faraday calculations on 4CH1 — only moles.

D15

Concentrated potassium bromide solution is electrolysed using inert electrodes.

(a) Name the product at the negative electrode and explain why it forms. (2)

(c) State and explain how the pH of the solution near the negative electrode changes. (2)

(b) Bromine 1; 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ 1

(c) The pH increases / the solution becomes alkaline 1; because hydroxide ions (OH⁻) are left in solution as H⁺ ions are discharged 1
