02How it actually works

Electrolysis is using electricity to force a compound apart. Everything on these papers reduces to three questions: can it conduct? — what arrives at each electrode? — what wins the discharge? Each has a fixed answer pattern.

Why things conduct — and why solids never do

Spec 1.55C, 1.56C · 2023 Q2b · 2024 Q6a

Conduction needs charged particles free to move. Three cases, and the papers test all three:

  • Metals conduct always: delocalised electrons move through the lattice.
  • Ionic compounds conduct only when molten or dissolved: the ions are freed to move. In the solid, the ions are locked in the lattice — the ions cannot move. Never say "the electrons can't move" for an ionic solid: 2024's scheme gave no marks if the answer was about electrons.
  • Covalent compounds do not conduct: no ions, no free electrons — no charged particles free to move.

The 2023 four-marker asked for magnesium and magnesium chloride in one answer, and the four marks were exactly: delocalised electrons · electrons can move · MgCl₂ only molten/in solution · ions free to move. Say which particle moves in which substance.

The circuit: ions travel, electrodes swap electrons

Spec 1.57C · 2024 Q6b

Cations (+) — metal ions and H⁺ — travel to the cathode (−), where they gain electrons (reduction). Anions (−) travel to the anode (+), where they lose electrons (oxidation).

2024's "explain how sodium forms" is the pattern answer: positive sodium ions are attracted to the negative electrode and each ion gains an electron (is reduced) to become an atom. Both halves, and the word ion throughout — the scheme rejected "sodium atoms/metal moves to the electrode".

OIL RIG — and saying it about the right species

Oxidation Is Loss of electrons, Reduction Is Gain — and it is always the ion that does the gaining or losing. "The bromide ions lose electrons" scores; "bromine loses electrons" was explicitly rejected in 2025, and "magnesium gains electrons" in 2023.

Half-equations — the four you write and the two rules that kill them

Spec 1.59C · every paper

At the cathode (reduction — electrons on the left):

  • Metal deposits: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu · Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (molten) · Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg
  • Hydrogen from solution: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂

At the anode (oxidation — electrons on the right):

  • Halogens: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ · 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻
  • Oxygen from solution: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ (equivalently 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻)

The two rules the commentary begs for: halogens are diatomic (Cl₂, never Cl or Cl₃ or Cl⁺), and the oxygen half-equation starts from water in a solution — 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ was the standard wrong answer in 2023 because a solution contains no O²⁻ ions. Balance atoms first, then make the charges balance with electrons.

Which ion is discharged — reactivity decides

Spec 1.58C · 2022 Q5b · 2024 Q6b(iv) · 2025 Q3b

A solution always contains the compound's ions plus H⁺ and OH⁻ from water. At each electrode only one species is discharged:

  • Cathode: the less reactive of the metal and hydrogen. Copper and silver (below hydrogen) deposit as metal; sodium, potassium, magnesium (above hydrogen) stay in solution and hydrogen bubbles off instead. That is why aqueous NaCl gives H₂, not sodium — "sodium is more reactive than hydrogen" is the one-mark answer.
  • Anode: a halide present → the halogen (Cl₂, Br₂). Otherwise (sulfate, nitrate) → oxygen from water.

The 2021 table question is this rule five times over: CuSO₄ → Cu + O₂ (H₂SO₄ left); K₂SO₄ → H₂ + O₂ (K₂SO₄ left — effectively electrolysing water); AgNO₃ → Ag + O₂ (HNO₃ left); KNO₃ → H₂ + O₂ (KNO₃ left).

What you would see, and what's left behind

2023 Q5 · 2025 Q3b

Observations are bubbles and colours, not chemical names: at the anode of CuSO₄ electrolysis you see "effervescence / bubbles" (the scheme ignored "oxygen"). Gas tests close the loop: lit splint → squeaky pop = hydrogen; glowing splint relights = oxygen; chlorine bleaches damp litmus paper. Lit and glowing are not interchangeable — 2024 rejected the glowing splint for hydrogen.

The solution changes too. Electrolysing NaCl(aq): H⁺ is removed at the cathode, leaving Na⁺ and OH⁻ — the solution near the cathode turns alkaline, universal indicator goes blue/purple, and the particle responsible is the hydroxide ion. In CuSO₄(aq) with inert electrodes the blue fades as Cu²⁺ is removed and the solution becomes sulfuric acid. And where do OH⁻ ions in a copper sulfate solution come from at all? From water (dissociating) — not "because the solution is alkaline"; it isn't.

Redox beyond the electrodes

Spec 2.20 · 2021 Q10b · 2024 Q5a · 2025 Q4b

Redox has two currencies, and the papers use both:

  • Oxygen: oxidation = gaining oxygen, reduction = losing it. Thermite: aluminium is oxidised (gains oxygen), iron(III) oxide is reduced (loses oxygen) — name the whole compound; 2024 penalised "iron is reduced". A reducing agent removes oxygen from something else (carbon in metal extraction); an oxidising agent gives it.
  • Electrons: the electrode definition — loss is oxidation, gain is reduction — and the reason every anode reaction "is an oxidation": the ions lose electrons.

A reaction is redox when both happen at once: in Mg + O₂, magnesium is oxidised and oxygen reduced. Say which species is oxidised and which is reduced — "oxidation and reduction happen at the same time" scored nothing in 2024.

04Understanding it

U1

Solid sodium chloride sits between two connected electrodes and nothing happens. The moment it melts, a current flows and products appear. What changed — and why would the same experiment described with "electrons become free to move" score zero?

Show answer

Melting frees the ions. In the solid, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are locked in a lattice by their attractions; they vibrate but cannot travel, so no charge can flow. Molten, the ions move: Na⁺ to the cathode, Cl⁻ to the anode — the moving ions are the current inside the electrolyte.

"Electrons become free" is a different (wrong) conduction story — that is how metals conduct. An ionic compound never conducts by electron flow; the charge carriers are ions, and the mark schemes reject electron answers outright.

U2

Electrolysing concentrated sodium chloride solution gives hydrogen at the cathode, yet molten sodium chloride gives sodium. Explain the difference — and what universal indicator near the cathode of the solution would show.

Show answer

The solution contains a second cation: H⁺ from the water. At the cathode the less reactive species is discharged, and hydrogen is far less reactive than sodium — so H⁺ gains the electrons and H₂ bubbles off, while Na⁺ stays in solution. Molten NaCl has no water, no H⁺, no competition: sodium is the only choice.

Removing H⁺ leaves Na⁺ and OH⁻ behind — sodium hydroxide solution. Universal indicator near the cathode turns blue/purple, because hydroxide ions accumulate there. (2025's marker: most students never realised an alkali was forming, and few could name the OH⁻ ion as the reason.)

U3

Why must the oxygen half-equation in a solution start from water (2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻) when 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ balances perfectly well?

Show answer

Balancing isn't the test — existing is. A half-equation must start from a species actually present, and a solution of copper sulfate or dilute acid contains Cu²⁺, SO₄²⁻, H⁺, OH⁻ and H₂O — but no free O²⁻ ions. Oxide ions are only found inside solid or molten oxides.

The oxygen made at the anode really does come from water molecules (or equivalently the OH⁻ from water): 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻, or 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ — both accepted, because both start from what is really there. This exact wrong answer, 2O²⁻, was the year group's favourite in 2023.

U4

"The magnesium gains two electrons at the cathode." The teacher's comment on this sentence used two exclamation marks. What exactly is wrong with it, and what does the correct sentence look like?

Show answer

Before the electrons arrive there is no magnesium — there is a magnesium ion, Mg²⁺. The whole event at the cathode is the ion becoming magnesium: Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg. Saying "magnesium gains electrons" describes a neutral atom taking on electrons, which is not what happens and would make Mg²⁻.

Correct: "the magnesium ion gains two electrons (is reduced) to form a magnesium atom." The same precision is demanded at the anode: bromide ions lose electrons; chlorine doesn't lose anything — it is the product.

U5

In the water-electrolysis apparatus, twice the volume of gas collects over the cathode as over the anode — but the oxygen tube is always slightly under half. Explain both facts.

Show answer

The 2 : 1 ratio: water is H₂O — the half-equations transfer four electrons to make 2H₂ for every O₂. Twice the moles of hydrogen means twice the volume, because equal moles of gas occupy equal volumes.

The shortfall: some of the oxygen dissolves in the water (oxygen is slightly soluble; the same reason less chlorine than hydrogen collects from NaCl(aq) — chlorine dissolves too). Not a leak, not an error — the accepted one-marker is "some oxygen dissolves".

Related staple: why add dilute acid to the water first? Pure water is a poor conductor; the acid increases its conductivity. (Not "helps carry the current" — name the property.)

U6

The thermite reaction is 2Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe. The 2024 marker complained that "many just said oxidation and reduction are occurring at the same time" and that "many said IRON was reduced rather than IRON OXIDE". Write the answer that scores both marks.

Show answer

"Aluminium is oxidised because it gains oxygen (→ Al₂O₃), and iron(III) oxide is reduced because it loses oxygen (→ Fe)."

Two named species, two named directions, each tied to oxygen. The two rejected shapes: (1) restating the definition of redox without naming anything; (2) "iron is reduced" — iron is the product; the thing that lost oxygen is the oxide. The electron version also scores: iron(III) ions gain electrons.

Bonus connection: aluminium here is the reducing agent — it removes the oxygen — exactly the role carbon plays in extracting iron in the metal-extraction module.

U7

Copper(II) sulfate solution is electrolysed with inert graphite electrodes. Predict what happens at each electrode, what you would see, and what the solution contains at the end.

Show answer

Cathode: copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu — a pink/brown coating of copper grows on the electrode.

Anode: no halide present, so oxygen from water: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ — you see effervescence/bubbles (that is the observation; "oxygen" is a name, not a sight).

End state: Cu²⁺ is progressively removed, so the blue colour fades; H⁺ accumulates with the SO₄²⁻, leaving dilute sulfuric acid. This is 2021's products-table row and 2023 Q5 in one story.

05Exam drill

D1

Molten lead(II) bromide is electrolysed using inert electrodes.

(a) Suggest a suitable material for the electrodes. (1)
(b) Write the half-equation for the reaction at the cathode. (1)
(c) Write the half-equation for the reaction at the anode, and state why it is an oxidation. (2)

Show answer

(a) Graphite/carbon (or platinum) 1

(b) Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb 1

(c) 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ 1; oxidation because the bromide ions lose electrons 1 — "bromine loses electrons" is rejected

D2

Sodium metal is extracted by electrolysing molten sodium chloride.

(a) Explain why solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity but molten sodium chloride does. (2)
(b) Explain how sodium metal forms at the negative electrode. (2)
(c) Give a reason why sodium metal does not form when a concentrated aqueous solution of sodium chloride is electrolysed. (1)

Show answer

(a) In the solid the ions are fixed in the lattice / cannot move 1; when molten the ions are free to move (and carry the charge) 1 — no marks for electron-based answers

(b) Positive sodium ions are attracted to the negative electrode 1; each ion gains an electron / is reduced (Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na) 1

(c) Sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so hydrogen is discharged instead 1

D3

Dilute sulfuric acid is electrolysed in a Hofmann voltameter with platinum electrodes.

(a) Give the test for the gas collected at the negative electrode. (1)
(b) Give the test for the gas collected at the positive electrode. (1)
(c) Write the half-equation for the formation of the gas at the negative electrode. (1)
(d) Complete the half-equation for the positive electrode: ......H₂O → ......H⁺ + ......O₂ + ......e⁻ (1)

Show answer

(a) A lit splint gives a squeaky pop — hydrogen 1

(b) A glowing splint relights — oxygen 1

(c) 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ 1

(d) 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + O₂ + 4e⁻ 1

D4

These aqueous solutions are electrolysed under the same conditions with inert electrodes. Complete the table. (4)

copper(II) sulfate · potassium sulfate · silver nitrate · potassium nitrate — for each: product at the cathode, product at the anode, substance left in solution.

Show answer

copper(II) sulfate: copper · oxygen · sulfuric acid 1

potassium sulfate: hydrogen · oxygen · potassium sulfate (water is electrolysed) 1

silver nitrate: silver · oxygen · nitric acid 1

potassium nitrate: hydrogen · oxygen · potassium nitrate 1

Cathode = the less reactive of metal vs hydrogen; anode = halogen if a halide is present, otherwise oxygen; whatever wasn't discharged stays and pairs up.

D5

Concentrated sodium chloride solution is electrolysed in the laboratory, and both gases are collected.

(a) State why this electrolysis should be carried out in a fume cupboard. (1)
(b) Suggest why the volume of chlorine collected is less than the volume of hydrogen. (1)
(c) Universal indicator is added near the negative electrode. State the colour seen and identify the ion responsible. (2)

Show answer

(a) Chlorine is toxic/poisonous 1

(b) Some of the chlorine dissolves in the water/solution 1

(c) Blue/purple 1; hydroxide ions (OH⁻), left behind as H⁺ is discharged 1

D6

The reaction 2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂ is used to extract copper.

(a) Explain why this is a redox reaction, naming the species oxidised and the species reduced. (2)
(b) State the role of carbon in this reaction. (1)

Show answer

(a) Carbon is oxidised — it gains oxygen (→ CO₂) 1; copper(II) oxide is reduced — it loses oxygen (→ Cu) 1 — name the oxide, not "copper", and never just "both happen at once"

(b) Carbon is the reducing agent — it removes the oxygen from the copper oxide 1
