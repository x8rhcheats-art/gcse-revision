02How it actually works

Rate is about how fast a reaction goes — never how much product it makes. Everything in this topic is one idea (particles must collide, hard enough, often enough) applied to five factors and read off two kinds of graph.

What rate means, and the three ways it is measured

Spec 3.9 · 2021 Q8 · 2022 Q3 · 2023 Q7

Rate = how much reactant is used up, or product is made, per unit time. The school's papers measure it three ways:

  • Mass loss on a balance — marble chips + hydrochloric acid in a flask with a cotton wool plug. The CO₂ escapes, so the reading falls. The plug stops acid spray escaping so the mass decrease is only due to the gas — it must still let the gas out.
  • Gas collected in a syringe — magnesium + acid, or hydrogen peroxide decomposing. Volume rises with time.
  • Time for a change to happen — in the booklet practicals: time to collect a set volume, or for a cross to disappear.

2022 asked why the cotton wool increases accuracy, and the mark scheme rejected "it stops the gas escaping" — the gas must escape or the mass would not change. The answer is that it stops acid splashing out, so the mass decrease is due to the gas alone.

The graph is the question: reading rate curves

Spec 3.9 · 2023 Q7 · 2025 Q1

Amount of product against time gives the same curve every year:

  • Steepest at the start — reactant concentration is highest, so collisions are most frequent.
  • Flattens as reactant is used up — collisions become less frequent.
  • Horizontal when the reaction has finished — one reactant is completely used up. On a total-mass graph this appears as a falling curve that levels off.

Two set-piece tasks come with the graph, and both have exact rules:

Reading a value: draw a vertical line from the time up to the curve and across to the axis — and show those lines. 2023: "They had to show working-out lines on graph to get the full 2 marks."

Rate at an instant (tangent): draw a tangent touching the curve at that time only, make a large triangle, rate = change in y ÷ change in x, and give the unit the axes demand: cm³/min, g/s. The four marks in 2025 were exactly: tangent drawn · triangle measurements · gradient calculation · correct unit.

Rate at 60 s means the tangent gradient at 60 s, not total change ÷ 60. In 2022 the average-rate answer scored 1 of 3. And the average rate over the first minute is a different number from the rate at 60 s — the curve is always slowing.

Collision theory — the three-mark answer that is always the same

Spec 3.10, 3.11 · every paper 2021–2025

Particles react only when they collide with energy at or above the activation energy. Every factor question is answered by saying what happens to the frequency of successful collisions:

The template

1. What changed about the particles (more per volume / more energy / more exposed surface) 1

2. The word successful and/or energy ≥ activation energy where temperature is involved 1

3. Collisions happen more frequently / more per unit time → rate increases 1

  • Concentration ↑ (or gas pressure ↑): more particles in the same volume → more frequent collisions. Never mention energy here — 2024's scheme capped the answer at 1 mark if kinetic energy appeared in a concentration answer.
  • Temperature ↑: particles have more (kinetic) energy → more collisions have energy above the activation energy (a greater proportion are successful) → and collisions are more frequent. 2023: "They had to say that more/a greater proportion of collisions are successful. Too many merged this point with collision frequency."
  • Surface area ↑ (powder instead of chips/ribbon): more particles exposed → more frequent collisions. Total amount is unchanged, so the final amount of product is unchanged — the plateau is reached sooner but at the same height.
  • Catalyst: see below.

Say the concentration of the acid, not "the concentration of magnesium" — 2023's marker penalised that exact phrase. Solids do not have a concentration.

Catalysts — both halves or no mark

Spec 3.12, 3.13 · 2024 Q3 · 2025 Q1

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate without being chemically changed or used up. How it works is a fixed two-marker:

The two-mark script for "explain how a catalyst increases rate"

1. It provides an alternative reaction pathway 1

2. …with a lower activation energy 1

So a greater proportion of collisions are successful. Saying only "it lowers the activation energy" was the standard way to drop a mark in 2025 — the pathway is the mechanism, the lower Eₐ is the consequence.

The school's example is always manganese(IV) oxide catalysing the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. The catalyst can be recovered unchanged at the end — its mass is the same before and after.

The two named practicals

Spec 3.15, 3.16 (practicals) · 2022 Q3 · 2023 Q7 · 2024 Q3 · 2025 Q1

1 — Marble chips + hydrochloric acid (surface area / concentration). CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g). Flask on a balance, cotton wool plug, record mass every 20–30 s. Change one variable (chip size, or acid concentration), keep everything else the same: same mass of chips, same volume and temperature of acid.

2 — Catalytic decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (catalysts). Measure with a gas syringe; compare different solid catalysts by the initial gradient of the volume–time curve. Same volume and concentration of H₂O₂, same mass of catalyst, same temperature.

When the question says "explain how you would make it a fair test", name the actual variables kept constant — the generic phrase "fair test" scores nothing.

Sketching the second curve

2023 Q7b · 2024 Q3b

Asked almost every year: given the curve at one temperature (or concentration), draw the curve for a hotter / more concentrated / powdered version. Two marks, two rules:

  • Steeper at the start, beginning at exactly (0,0) — 2023: "the curve had to very obviously start at (0,0) — quite a lot missed the axes slightly."
  • Levels off at exactly the same final amount (same mass and moles of reactant → same product), just reached sooner. If the amount of reactant changed, only then does the plateau move.

03Cheat sheet

Graph craft — where the easy marks go

  • Sharp pencil, plots exact to the nearest gridline, visible ×.
  • Best-fit curve: one single smooth line, no feathering, through the origin when the data starts there, level exactly at the plateau.
  • Reading a value or a tangent: show the construction lines on the graph — they carry marks on their own.
  • Second-condition sketch: steeper from (0,0), same plateau height.
  • Ignore an anomalous point when drawing the line — and if asked why an anomaly happened in a rates experiment, "forgot to stir" is the accepted cause on this school's papers.

04Understanding it

U1

A student says: "Doubling the concentration doubles the energy of the collisions, so the reaction is faster." There is one correct claim and one wrong claim in that sentence. Which is which?

Show answer

Correct: the reaction is faster.

Wrong: concentration does not change the energy of anything. The particles move at the same speed at the same temperature; there are simply more of them in the same volume, so collisions happen more frequently.

Energy language belongs only in temperature answers. The 2024 mark scheme capped concentration answers mentioning kinetic energy at 1 mark of 3 — this exact confusion.

U2

The same mass of marble is used in two runs — large chips, then the same mass ground to powder. Why does the powder react faster, and why is the final volume of CO₂ the same in both runs?

Show answer

Faster: grinding exposes far more particles at the surface — greater surface area — so collisions between acid particles and carbonate particles happen more frequently.

Same final volume: the amount of CaCO₃ is unchanged — same mass, same moles — so the same moles of CO₂ are eventually made. Surface area changes how fast you get there, never how much you get.

On the graph: steeper start, same plateau. If your sketch's plateau moves, you have changed the amount, not the rate.

U3

Why does raising the temperature by only 10 °C roughly double the rate, when the collision frequency only rises by a few percent?

Show answer

Because frequency is the small effect. The big effect is that the proportion of collisions with energy above the activation energy rises steeply with temperature — many collisions that used to bounce apart now react.

That is why the mark scheme insists on "more collisions have energy greater than the activation energy / a greater proportion are successful" as its own separate mark, and why merging it into "more collisions" loses it. Two different mechanisms: hitting harder (dominant) and hitting more often (minor).

U4

A catalyst does not appear in the chemical equation and its mass is unchanged at the end. So how can it possibly change the rate?

Show answer

It takes part, then is regenerated. The catalyst offers the reaction an alternative pathway — a different route from reactants to products — whose activation energy is lower.

The particles' energies are unchanged, but the bar they must clear is lower, so a greater proportion of the same collisions are now successful.

Because the catalyst is chemically unchanged overall, a tiny amount works for a large amount of reactant, and it is still there — same mass — at the end. That "chemically unchanged at the end" phrase is the definition mark.

U5

Two identical H₂O₂ experiments run at 20 °C and 40 °C. Sketch both curves in your head: state three things the 40 °C curve does and one thing it must not do.

Show answer

The 40 °C curve:

  1. starts at exactly (0,0), like the original;
  2. rises more steeply from the start;
  3. levels off sooner — but at the same final volume, because the amount of H₂O₂ is the same.

It must not level off at a higher or lower volume. Temperature changes the journey, not the destination — only changing the amount (or concentration and volume) of reactant moves the plateau.

U6

In the marble-chips experiment the reaction eventually stops while chips are still visible in the flask. What ran out, how would the balance tell you the reaction had stopped, and what would you see in the flask?

Show answer

What ran out: the hydrochloric acid — the acid is the limiting reactant. "All the acid has reacted" is the accepted wording.

The balance: the mass stops decreasing — the reading stays constant. (On a graph: the curve levels off.)

In the flask: the fizzing/effervescence stops.

Note the logic: solid left over ⇒ the other reactant finished. This is the same limiting-reactant idea the calculations module uses — the excess is visible, the limiting reagent decides when it ends.

U7

Why is the rate of reaction greatest at the very start — and what is wrong with answering "because the particles have the most energy at the start"?

Show answer

Right answer: at the start the concentration of the acid (the reactants in solution) is at its highest — the most particles per unit volume — so collisions are at their most frequent. As reactant is used up, collisions become rarer and the curve flattens.

The wrong answer: energy has nothing to do with it. The temperature is constant, so the particles' energies are the same at the start as at the end. The 2023 scheme explicitly rejected incorrect references to energy here.

U8

A student compares two catalysts for the decomposition of H₂O₂ by timing how long each run takes to collect 20 cm³ of oxygen. Name the three variables that must be kept the same, and explain which catalyst is better: the 40-second run or the 90-second run.

Show answer

Keep the same: volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, mass of catalyst, and temperature. (Naming the actual variables is what scores — "make it fair" scores nothing.)

Better catalyst: the 40-second one. Less time to make the same volume of gas = faster rate. With time-based measurements the comparison flips: shorter time means faster, and rate ∝ 1/time.

05Exam drill

D1

Manganese(IV) oxide catalyses the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide.

(a) State what a catalyst is. (2)
(b) Explain how a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction. (2)
(c) Describe how you could show the manganese(IV) oxide is chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction. (2)

Show answer

(a) A substance that increases the rate of a reaction 1 but is chemically unchanged / not used up at the end 1

(b) Provides an alternative (reaction) pathway 1 with a lower activation energy 1 — both halves needed; "lowers the activation energy" alone drops the pathway mark

(c) Filter it off, wash and dry it, and weigh it: the mass is the same as at the start 1; (it can then catalyse a fresh run just as well) 1

D2

A student investigates the reaction between magnesium ribbon and dilute hydrochloric acid, collecting the hydrogen in a gas syringe and recording the volume every 15 seconds until the reaction stops.

(a) Explain, in terms of particle collision theory, why the rate of reaction is greatest at the start. (2)
(b) The student repeats the experiment at a temperature 10 °C higher. Explain, in terms of particle collision theory, how this affects the rate. (3)
(c) State two ways the curve for the hotter experiment differs from the original, and one way it is the same. (3)

Show answer

(a) The concentration of the acid is greatest at the start — most particles per unit volume 1; so collisions are most frequent / most collisions per unit time 1 — no energy language

(b) Particles gain (kinetic) energy 1; more collisions have energy greater than the activation energy — a greater proportion are successful 1; collision frequency increases, so rate increases 1

(c) Different: steeper at the start 1; levels off sooner 1. Same: it finishes at the same final volume of hydrogen (same amounts of reactants) 1

D3

Marble chips react with dilute hydrochloric acid in a flask on a balance, with a cotton wool plug in the neck. The mass falls from 124.05 g and becomes constant at 123.07 g.

(a) Explain why using a cotton wool plug increases the accuracy of the results. (2)
(b) Calculate the amount, in moles, of carbon dioxide produced. [Mᵣ of CO₂ = 44] (2)
(c) State how the student knows the reaction has stopped, and why some marble remains. (2)

Show answer

(a) It stops acid spray/splashes escaping 1, so the decrease in mass is due only to the (carbon dioxide) gas 1 — NOT "stops gas escaping": the gas must escape

(b) Mass loss = 124.05 − 123.07 = 0.98 g 1; n = 0.98 ÷ 44 = 0.022 mol 1

(c) The mass stays constant / stops decreasing 1; all the acid has reacted — the acid is the limiting reactant 1

D4

A volume–time graph shows oxygen collected from decomposing hydrogen peroxide. Describe how to find the rate of reaction, in cm³/min, at time 8 minutes. (4)

Show answer

Draw a tangent to the curve touching it at 8 minutes only 1; make a (large) triangle on the tangent and read the changes from the axes 1; rate = change in volume ÷ change in time (change in y ÷ change in x) 1; give the answer with the unit cm³/min 1

Unit discipline: cm³ not cm, min not m — "m is metres". And show the tangent and triangle on the graph; the construction carries marks.

D5

A student compares powdered calcium carbonate with chips of the same mass, reacting each with the same volume and concentration of acid at the same temperature.

(a) Explain why the powder gives a higher rate of reaction. (2)
(b) The student says the powder will also give more carbon dioxide. Explain why the student is wrong. (2)

Show answer

(a) Powder has a greater surface area 1, so more collisions per unit time / more frequent collisions between acid particles and the solid 1

(b) The amount (mass/moles) of calcium carbonate is the same 1, so the same amount of CO₂ is produced — surface area changes the rate, not the amount of product 1

D6

Design an experiment, using marble chips and dilute hydrochloric acid, to investigate how the concentration of the acid affects the rate of reaction. Include what you would measure, and how you would make the comparison valid. (5)

Show answer

Use the flask-on-a-balance (or gas syringe) set-up 1; record the mass (or gas volume) at regular time intervals 1; repeat with different concentrations of acid 1; keep the same: mass and size of marble chips, volume of acid, temperature 1; compare the initial gradients of the graphs (or time to lose a set mass / collect a set volume) 1

Name the controlled variables — "make it a fair test" on its own scores nothing.
