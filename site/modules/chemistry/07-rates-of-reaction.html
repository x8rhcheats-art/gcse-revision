02How it actually works

Rate is about how fast a reaction goes — never how much product it makes. Everything in this topic is one idea (particles must collide, hard enough, often enough) applied to five factors and read off two kinds of graph.

What rate means

Spec 3.9, 3.10 · 2021 Q8 · 2022 Q3 · 2023 Q7

Rate of reaction is how much reactant is used up, or product is made, per unit time. "Per unit time" is the part that makes it a rate. Without it you have only described an amount.

The simplest rate to work out is the mean (average) rate over a stretch of time:

Mean rate of reactionmean rate = amount of product formed (or reactant used) ÷ time takengas in cm³, mass in g · time in s or min · unit = cm³/s, cm³/min, g/s

Worked example — mean rate with units

Magnesium reacts with acid. 48 cm³ of hydrogen is collected in the first 2.0 minutes.

  1. Write the equation: mean rate = volume ÷ time
  2. Substitute: mean rate = 48 ÷ 2.0
  3. Answer: 24 cm³/min
  4. If seconds are wanted: 2.0 min = 120 s, so 48 ÷ 120 = 0.40 cm³/s

Same idea with mass: a flask loses 0.66 g in 300 s → 0.66 ÷ 300 = 0.0022 g/s.

✗ "24 cm/m" — cm is a length and m is metres.
✗ "24" — no unit, no unit mark.
✓ "24 cm³/min" — cm³ for volume, min for minutes, per unit time.

Three ways to measure it

Spec 3.9, 3.15, 3.16 · 2021 Q8 · 2022 Q3 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q9(a) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q9(a)

You measure rate by following something that changes as the reaction goes: the mass of the flask, the volume of gas, or the time for a change to happen.

  • Mass loss on a balance — marble chips + hydrochloric acid in a flask with a cotton wool plug. The CO₂ escapes, so the reading falls.
  • Gas collected in a gas syringe — magnesium or zinc + acid, or hydrogen peroxide decomposing. The volume rises with time.
  • Time for a change to happen — time to collect a set volume of gas, or time to lose a set mass. A shorter time means a faster rate.

Why the mass falls, step by step (2024 Edexcel 1C Q9(a)(i) asked exactly this):

  1. CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
  2. Carbon dioxide is a gas, so it leaves the flask.
  3. The balance reading falls by the mass of CO₂ given off.
  4. When the reaction stops, the mass stays constant.

2022 asked why the cotton wool increases accuracy, and the mark scheme rejected "it stops the gas escaping". The gas must escape or the mass would not change.
✗ "The cotton wool stops the gas escaping."
✓ "It stops acid spray escaping, so the decrease in mass is due only to the gas."

Reading a rate curve

Spec 3.9 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q9(b) · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q6(b) · 2023 Q7 · 2025 Q1

Every rate curve is steepest at the start, gets less steep, and then goes flat when one reactant runs out. The gradient of the curve is the rate: steeper means faster.

2024 Edexcel 1C Q9(b)(i) gave marks for linked pairs — what the curve does, then why:

  1. The curve is steepest at the start → because the acid concentration is highest, so collisions are most frequent.
  2. The curve becomes less steep → because the acid is used up and becomes less concentrated, so collisions are less frequent.
  3. The curve levels off → because one reactant (here the acid) has been used up, so the reaction has stopped.

Reading a value: draw a vertical line from the time up to the curve and across to the axis — and show those lines. 2023: "They had to show working-out lines on graph to get the full 2 marks."

✗ "The rate is fastest at the start because the particles have the most energy."
✓ "The rate is fastest at the start because the concentration of the acid is highest, so there are more collisions per unit time."

The temperature is the same all the way through, so energy has nothing to do with it. 2023's scheme rejected energy here.

Worked example — rate at an instant from a tangent

Spec 3.9 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q11(b) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q9(c)(iv) · 2022 Q3 · 2025 Q1

The rate at one moment is the gradient of a tangent drawn at that moment. A tangent is a straight line that just touches the curve at that one point without cutting through it.

  1. Find the time on the x-axis and go up to the curve.
  2. Draw a tangent touching the curve at that point only. Use a ruler and a sharp pencil.
  3. Make the tangent long, then draw a large triangle under it.
  4. Read the change in y and the change in x from the axes.
  5. Rate = change in y ÷ change in x.
  6. Give the unit from the axes: cm³/s, cm³/min or g/s.

Worked example — hydrogen collected against time

A tangent is drawn at 40 s. It passes through (0 s, 45 cm³) and (80 s, 305 cm³).

  1. Change in volume = 305 − 45 = 260 cm³
  2. Change in time = 80 − 0 = 80 s
  3. Rate = 260 ÷ 80
  4. Rate = 3.25 cm³/s (about 3.3 cm³/s)

2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q11(b) marked this exactly: tangent drawn 1; change in volume ÷ change in time 1; answer in the accepted range, 2.75–3.75 cm³/s 1. The range is wide because everyone's tangent is slightly different. The method is what must be right.

Rate at 60 s means the tangent gradient at 60 s, not total change ÷ 60. In 2022 the average-rate answer (0.015 g/s) scored 1 of 3; the tangent gave about 0.005 g/s.
✗ "rate at 60 s = 88 cm³ ÷ 60 s"
✓ "draw a tangent at 60 s; rate = change in y ÷ change in x = … cm³/s"

Comparing curves and sketching the second one

Spec 3.10 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q10 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q9(b)(ii) · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q6(c)(i) · 2023 Q7(b) · 2024 Q3(b)

The steepness at the start tells you the rate; the height of the plateau tells you how much product — and only the amount of the limiting reactant can change that height.

What changedStart of the curveFinal plateau
Hotter, powder, catalyst, more concentrated acid (same moles of the limiting reactant)Steeper, from (0,0)Same height, reached sooner
Cooler, bigger chipsLess steep, from (0,0)Same height, reached later
Half the moles of the limiting reactant (e.g. same volume of acid at half the concentration, metal in excess)Less steepHalf the height

Worked example — which reactant sets the plateau?

Like 2022 Edexcel 1C Q6: 0.090 g of magnesium with 0.025 mol of HCl. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂

  1. Moles of Mg = 0.090 ÷ 24 = 0.00375 mol
  2. HCl needed = 2 × 0.00375 = 0.0075 mol
  3. 0.0075 mol is less than the 0.025 mol present, so the acid is in excess.
  4. So the magnesium is the limiting reactant. It decides how much hydrogen is made, and so where the curve levels off.

In 2023 Edexcel 1C Q10 it was the other way round: the zinc was in excess, so halving the moles of acid halved the final volume of hydrogen (40 cm³ → 20 cm³) and made the curve less steep.

Two rules for any sketch, from 2023: "the curve had to very obviously start at (0,0) — quite a lot missed the axes slightly." And if the amounts have not changed, the new curve must level off at exactly the same height.

Collision theory — the idea behind every factor

Spec 3.11 · every school paper 2021–2025

Particles only react when they collide with at least the activation energy — so anything that makes those successful collisions happen more often speeds the reaction up.

  1. Reactant particles must collide.
  2. The collision must have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy. Then it is a successful collision.
  3. Rate depends on the frequency of successful collisions — how many happen per unit time.

The three-mark template for any factor

1. What changed about the particles (more per unit volume / more energy / more exposed at the surface) 1

2. The word successful, and energy ≥ activation energy where temperature is involved 1

3. Collisions happen more frequently / more per unit time → rate increases 1

2025: "Several lost M3 by not linking successful collision to time, or missing out successful."
✗ "The particles have more chance of colliding."
✓ "There are more successful collisions per unit time."

Concentration and gas pressure

Spec 3.10, 3.11 · 2021 Q8(b)(iii) · 2024 Q3(c) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q9(d)

A higher concentration (or a higher gas pressure) packs more particles into the same volume, so they collide more often. The particles are no faster and no more energetic.

  1. Concentration of the solution increases (or the gas is at higher pressure).
  2. There are more particles in the same volume.
  3. So there are more frequent collisions — more collisions per unit time.
  4. So more successful collisions per unit time, and the rate increases.

For a gas, higher pressure means the same number of particles squeezed into a smaller volume — the same effect as a more concentrated solution. 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q9(d) showed rate is directly proportional to acid concentration: double the concentration, double the rate.

✗ "More concentrated acid gives the particles more energy." — the 2024 scheme capped concentration answers that mentioned kinetic energy at 1 mark.
✗ "The concentration of the magnesium increases." — solids have no concentration; 2023's marker penalised it.
✓ "More acid particles in the same volume, so more frequent collisions."

2021's scheme also rejected "molecules" or "atoms" for the acid — say particles or ions.

Temperature

Spec 3.10, 3.11 · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q6(c)(ii) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q10(b)(i) · 2023 Q7(b) · 2025 Q1(c)

Heating gives particles more kinetic energy, so a greater proportion of collisions have enough energy to react — and they collide more often too. Temperature is the only factor where energy belongs in the answer.

  1. Temperature increases, so particles gain kinetic energy.
  2. So more collisions have energy greater than the activation energy — a greater proportion are successful.
  3. Particles also move faster, so collision frequency increases.
  4. So there are more successful collisions per unit time, and the rate increases.

Cooler works the same way backwards: less kinetic energy, fewer successful collisions per unit time, slower rate — but, if the amounts are unchanged, the same final amount of product (2023 Edexcel 1C Q10(b)(i)).

2023: "They had to say that more/a greater proportion of collisions are successful. Too many merged this point with collision frequency."
✗ "More energy so more collisions."
✓ "More kinetic energy, so more collisions have energy ≥ activation energy, and collisions are more frequent."

Surface area

Spec 3.10, 3.11 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q9(c) · 2022 Q3(d)

Breaking a solid into smaller pieces exposes more particles on its surface, so acid particles hit it more often. Only the surface particles can react.

  1. Smaller chips, or a powder, are used instead of large lumps.
  2. This gives a greater surface area — more particles exposed.
  3. So there are more collisions per unit time between acid particles and the solid.
  4. So the rate increases.

The total amount of solid is unchanged, so the final amount of product is unchanged. The plateau is reached sooner but at the same height.

✗ "Powder has a bigger surface area so it makes more gas."
✓ "Powder has a greater surface area, so more collisions per unit time; the same mass gives the same volume of gas."

Catalysts — both halves or no mark

Spec 3.12, 3.13 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q10(c) · 2024 Q3(d) · 2025 Q1(a)

A catalyst speeds a reaction up by giving it an easier route, and is not used up.

The definition — two marks

1. A substance that increases the rate of a reaction 1

2. but is chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction 1

How it works, as a chain:

  1. The catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway.
  2. This pathway has a lower activation energy.
  3. So a greater proportion of collisions have enough energy to be successful.
  4. So there are more successful collisions per unit time, and the rate increases.

The school's example is always manganese(IV) oxide catalysing the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. The catalyst can be filtered off, dried and weighed at the end — its mass is the same as at the start.

2025: "A lot of students mentioned lowering activation energy so could not score M2".
✗ "The catalyst lowers the activation energy."
✓ "The catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy."

Reaction profiles — activation energy and ΔH (triple only)

Spec 3.14C · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q5(b)

A reaction profile shows the energy of the reactants, the hump the particles must get over, and the energy of the products. The hump is the activation energy.

  1. Draw the reactants as a flat line on the left.
  2. Draw a hump rising to a peak, then falling to the products line on the right.
  3. Activation energy (Eₐ): a vertical arrow from the reactants line up to the top of the peak.
  4. Enthalpy change (ΔH): a vertical arrow from the reactants line to the products line.
  5. Products lower than reactants = exothermic (ΔH negative). Products higher = endothermic (ΔH positive).

For the catalysed reaction, draw a second curve that starts at the same reactants level, has a lower peak, and ends at the same products level. The catalyst changes Eₐ but not ΔH.

✗ Eₐ drawn from the products line, or from the x-axis, up to the peak.
✗ Catalysed curve ending at a different products level.
✓ Both arrows start at the reactants level; the catalysed curve has a lower peak and the same start and end.

Practical 1 — marble chips and hydrochloric acid

Spec 3.15 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q9 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q9 · 2021 Q8 · 2022 Q3

Follow the mass lost as CO₂ escapes, and change only one thing — chip size or acid concentration.

  1. Put a measured volume of dilute hydrochloric acid in a conical flask on a balance.
  2. Add a measured mass of marble chips, put in the cotton wool plug and start the timer.
  3. Record the mass every 30 seconds until it stays constant.
  4. Repeat with smaller chips (same mass) — or with a different acid concentration.
  5. Plot mass lost (or mass) against time for each run and compare the gradients at the start.

Keep the same: mass of marble chips, volume of acid, temperature — and chip size or concentration, whichever you are not testing. Chips left in the flask at the end mean the acid has all reacted.

✗ "Make it a fair test." — scores nothing.
✓ "Use the same mass of marble chips, the same volume of acid and the same temperature."

Practical 2 — comparing catalysts for hydrogen peroxide

Spec 3.16 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q5(a) · 2024 Q3 · 2025 Q1

Add each solid to the same hydrogen peroxide and measure how fast oxygen is made; the fastest rate shows the best catalyst.

The five marks in 2022 Jan Edexcel 2C Q5(a) came from these steps:

  1. Put hydrogen peroxide in a conical flask connected to a gas syringe, add the catalyst and start the timer.
  2. Record the volume of oxygen at regular times — or the time to collect a set volume.
  3. Repeat with the same mass of each different catalyst.
  4. Use the same volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, at the same temperature.
  5. Plot volume against time and work out the gradient for each catalyst.
  6. The most effective catalyst gives the fastest rate — the steepest initial gradient, or the shortest time.

With a time-based measurement the comparison flips: a catalyst that makes 20 cm³ in 40 s beats one that takes 90 s. Shorter time = faster rate.

03Cheat sheet

The sentences behind most of the marks

Rate questions are answered with frequency of successful collisions. Name the factor's effect on the particles, keep the word successful, and end with more collisions per unit time.

Concentration / pressure: more particles in the same volume. Temperature: more kinetic energy, more collisions ≥ activation energy. Surface area: more particles exposed. Catalyst: alternative pathway, lower activation energy.

Graphs: steeper = faster. Rate at a time = tangent gradient. Plateau height = amount of the limiting reactant.

Graph craft — where the easy marks go

  • Sharp pencil, plots exact to the nearest gridline, visible ×.
  • Best-fit curve: one single smooth line, no feathering, through the origin when the data starts there, level exactly at the plateau.
  • Reading a value or a tangent: show the construction lines on the graph — they carry marks on their own.
  • Second-condition sketch: steeper from (0,0), same plateau height — unless the amount of the limiting reactant changed.
  • Ignore an anomalous point when drawing the line — and if asked why an anomaly happened in a rates experiment, "forgot to stir" is the accepted cause on this school's papers.

04Understanding it

U1

A student says: "Doubling the concentration doubles the energy of the collisions, so the reaction is faster." There is one correct claim and one wrong claim in that sentence. Which is which?

Show answer

Correct: the reaction is faster.

Wrong: concentration does not change the energy of anything. The particles move at the same speed at the same temperature; there are simply more of them in the same volume, so collisions happen more frequently.

Energy language belongs only in temperature answers. The 2024 mark scheme capped concentration answers mentioning kinetic energy at 1 mark of 3 — this exact confusion.

U2

The same mass of marble is used in two runs — large chips, then the same mass ground to powder. Why does the powder react faster, and why is the final volume of CO₂ the same in both runs?

Show answer

Faster: grinding exposes far more particles at the surface — greater surface area — so collisions between acid particles and carbonate particles happen more frequently.

Same final volume: the amount of CaCO₃ is unchanged — same mass, same moles — so the same moles of CO₂ are eventually made. Surface area changes how fast you get there, never how much you get.

On the graph: steeper start, same plateau. If your sketch's plateau moves, you have changed the amount, not the rate.

U3

Why does raising the temperature by only 10 °C roughly double the rate, when the collision frequency only rises by a few percent?

Show answer

Because frequency is the small effect. The big effect is that the proportion of collisions with energy above the activation energy rises steeply with temperature — many collisions that used to bounce apart now react.

That is why the mark scheme insists on "more collisions have energy greater than the activation energy / a greater proportion are successful" as its own separate mark, and why merging it into "more collisions" loses it. Two different mechanisms: hitting harder (dominant) and hitting more often (minor).

U4

A catalyst does not appear in the chemical equation and its mass is unchanged at the end. So how can it possibly change the rate?

Show answer

It takes part, then is regenerated. The catalyst offers the reaction an alternative pathway — a different route from reactants to products — whose activation energy is lower.

The particles' energies are unchanged, but the bar they must clear is lower, so a greater proportion of the same collisions are now successful.

Because the catalyst is chemically unchanged overall, a tiny amount works for a large amount of reactant, and it is still there — same mass — at the end. That "chemically unchanged at the end" phrase is the definition mark.

U5

Two identical H₂O₂ experiments run at 20 °C and 40 °C. Sketch both curves in your head: state three things the 40 °C curve does and one thing it must not do.

Show answer

The 40 °C curve:

  1. starts at exactly (0,0), like the original;
  2. rises more steeply from the start;
  3. levels off sooner — but at the same final volume, because the amount of H₂O₂ is the same.

It must not level off at a higher or lower volume. Temperature changes the journey, not the destination — only changing the amount of the limiting reactant moves the plateau.

U6

In the marble-chips experiment the reaction eventually stops while chips are still visible in the flask. What ran out, how would the balance tell you the reaction had stopped, and what would you see in the flask?

Show answer

What ran out: the hydrochloric acid — the acid is the limiting reactant. "All the acid has reacted" is the accepted wording.

The balance: the mass stops decreasing — the reading stays constant. (On a graph: the curve levels off.)

In the flask: the fizzing/effervescence stops.

Note the logic: solid left over ⇒ the other reactant finished. This is the same limiting-reactant idea the calculations module uses — the excess is visible, the limiting reagent decides when it ends.

U7

Why is the rate of reaction greatest at the very start — and what is wrong with answering "because the particles have the most energy at the start"?

Show answer

Right answer: at the start the concentration of the acid (the reactants in solution) is at its highest — the most particles per unit volume — so collisions are at their most frequent. As reactant is used up, collisions become rarer and the curve flattens.

The wrong answer: energy has nothing to do with it. The temperature is constant, so the particles' energies are the same at the start as at the end. The 2023 scheme explicitly rejected incorrect references to energy here.

U8

A gas syringe collects 88 cm³ of hydrogen in the first 60 s. A student says "so the rate at 60 s is 88 ÷ 60 = 1.5 cm³/s". Why is that wrong, and will the true rate at 60 s be bigger or smaller than 1.5 cm³/s?

Show answer

88 ÷ 60 is the mean rate over the whole first minute. The rate at 60 s is the gradient of the tangent at 60 s — a different number.

It will be smaller. The curve is always slowing down: it was steep at the start, when the acid was most concentrated, and flatter by 60 s. The mean mixes the fast early part with the slower later part, so it is bigger than the rate at the end of that minute.

This is why 2022's average-rate answer scored only 1 of 3.

05Exam drill

D1

Manganese(IV) oxide catalyses the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide.

(a) State what a catalyst is. (2)
(b) Explain how a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction. (2)
(c) Describe how you could show the manganese(IV) oxide is chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction. (2)

Show answer

(a) A substance that increases the rate of a reaction 1 but is chemically unchanged / not used up at the end 1

(b) Provides an alternative (reaction) pathway 1 with a lower activation energy 1 — both halves needed; "lowers the activation energy" alone drops the pathway mark

(c) Filter it off, wash and dry it, and weigh it: the mass is the same as at the start 1; (it can then catalyse a fresh run just as well) 1

D2

A student investigates the reaction between magnesium ribbon and dilute hydrochloric acid, collecting the hydrogen in a gas syringe and recording the volume every 15 seconds until the reaction stops.

(a) Explain, in terms of particle collision theory, why the rate of reaction is greatest at the start. (2)
(b) The student repeats the experiment at a temperature 10 °C higher. Explain, in terms of particle collision theory, how this affects the rate. (3)
(c) State two ways the curve for the hotter experiment differs from the original, and one way it is the same. (3)

Show answer

(a) The concentration of the acid is greatest at the start — most particles per unit volume 1; so collisions are most frequent / most collisions per unit time 1 — no energy language

(b) Particles gain (kinetic) energy 1; more collisions have energy greater than the activation energy — a greater proportion are successful 1; collision frequency increases, so rate increases 1

(c) Different: steeper at the start 1; levels off sooner 1. Same: it finishes at the same final volume of hydrogen (same amounts of reactants) 1

D3

Marble chips react with dilute hydrochloric acid in a flask on a balance, with a cotton wool plug in the neck. The mass falls from 124.05 g and becomes constant at 123.07 g.

(a) Explain why using a cotton wool plug increases the accuracy of the results. (2)
(b) Calculate the amount, in moles, of carbon dioxide produced. [Mᵣ of CO₂ = 44] (2)
(c) State how the student knows the reaction has stopped, and why some marble remains. (2)

Show answer

(a) It stops acid spray/splashes escaping 1, so the decrease in mass is due only to the (carbon dioxide) gas 1 — NOT "stops gas escaping": the gas must escape

(b) Mass loss = 124.05 − 123.07 = 0.98 g 1; n = 0.98 ÷ 44 = 0.022 mol 1

(c) The mass stays constant / stops decreasing 1; all the acid has reacted — the acid is the limiting reactant 1

D4

A volume–time graph shows oxygen collected from decomposing hydrogen peroxide. Describe how to find the rate of reaction, in cm³/min, at time 8 minutes. (4)

Show answer

Draw a tangent to the curve touching it at 8 minutes only 1; make a (large) triangle on the tangent and read the changes from the axes 1; rate = change in volume ÷ change in time (change in y ÷ change in x) 1; give the answer with the unit cm³/min 1

Unit discipline: cm³ not cm, min not m — "m is metres". And show the tangent and triangle on the graph; the construction carries marks.

D5

A student compares powdered calcium carbonate with chips of the same mass, reacting each with the same volume and concentration of acid at the same temperature.

(a) Explain why the powder gives a higher rate of reaction. (2)
(b) The student says the powder will also give more carbon dioxide. Explain why the student is wrong. (2)

Show answer
