Chemical Tests

Year 11 topic. It turns up on almost every paper, often as a quick "give a test for…" worth one or two marks, and sometimes as a five- or six-mark "show that this compound is…". The marks go to the exact reagent, the exact colour and what you see. Knowing roughly what happens is not enough.

The mark schemes spell out exactly where these marks go.

On the lithium chloride test (2022 Edexcel 2C): "REJECT incorrect acid e.g. HCl or H2SO4". Nitric acid goes before silver nitrate. Hydrochloric acid brings in chloride ions of its own.

On the lithium flame colour (2022 and 2023): "REJECT brick red/orange red". That is the calcium colour. Lithium is just red.

On the hydrogen test (2023 Edexcel 1C): "REJECT use of glowing splint". The school's 2024 scheme says the same. Hydrogen needs a lighted splint.

On the limewater and litmus tests (2024 Edexcel 1C): "No M5 and M6 if limewater added directly to the solution." You test the gas, not the liquid.

02How it actually works

Every test here does the same job. It turns an ion or a gas you cannot see into something you can see: a colour, a precipitate, a pop, a cloudy liquid. So every answer has two halves. Say what you do (the reagent), then what you see (the result). Name the substance only after that. If either half is missing, you lose the mark.

Hydrogen and oxygen — two different splints

Spec 2.44 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q2(b)(ii) · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q6(a)(ii) · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q5(d)(i) · 2025 school Q3(a)

Hydrogen burns with a squeaky pop when a lighted splint is held near it; oxygen relights a glowing splint.

GasWhat you doWhat you see
Hydrogen, H₂Hold a lighted (burning) splint at the mouth of the tubeSqueaky pop
Oxygen, O₂Put a glowing splint into the gasThe splint relights

The pop is a tiny explosion: hydrogen reacts with oxygen from the air and makes water. Oxygen does not burn. It helps other things burn, so a splint that is only glowing bursts back into flame.

✗ "Glowing splint — squeaky pop" — the wrong splint. The school's 2024 scheme and the Edexcel scheme both reject it.
✗ "It burns with a squeaky pop" — the 2023 scheme ignores this on its own. Say what you hold near the gas.
✓ "Hold a lighted splint near the gas — it burns with a squeaky pop."
✓ "Put in a glowing splint — it relights."

Carbon dioxide — limewater

Spec 2.44 · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q1(c) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q5(b)

Carbon dioxide turns limewater milky (cloudy).

Limewater is calcium hydroxide solution. Carbon dioxide reacts with it to make calcium carbonate, which is insoluble. The tiny white particles of solid make the liquid look milky.

Limewater turning milkyCa(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)the CaCO₃ is the white precipitate you see

The CO₂ answer — two marks

1. Bubble the gas through limewater 1

2. The limewater turns milky / cloudy 1

"White precipitate" is also allowed. "Turns white" alone is risky. Say milky or cloudy.

✗ "A lighted splint goes out" — lots of gases put out a splint (nitrogen does too). This is not a test for CO₂.
✗ "Add limewater to the solution" — the 2024 scheme gives no marks. It is the gas you test.
✓ "Bubble the gas through limewater; it turns milky."

Ammonia and chlorine — the two litmus tests

Spec 2.44 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q1(b) · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q10(b)(iii)

Ammonia turns damp red litmus paper blue; chlorine bleaches damp litmus paper white.

GasPaperWhat you seeWhy
Ammonia, NH₃Damp red litmusTurns blueAmmonia is the only common alkaline gas
Chlorine, Cl₂Damp blue litmusTurns red, then bleached whiteChlorine forms an acid in water, then bleaches the dye

The paper must be damp. A gas has to dissolve in water before it can change an indicator. Dry litmus paper does nothing.

✗ "Chlorine turns litmus red" — lots of acidic gases do that. The mark is for bleached.
✗ "Red litmus turns blue then bleaches" for chlorine — the 2024 scheme gives only M1 for this.
✓ "Hold damp blue litmus paper in the gas. It is bleached / turns white." The scheme also accepts "turns red and then bleached", and damp universal indicator paper.

The flame test — how to do it

Spec 2.45 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q6(b)(i) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q6(d)

A clean wire carries a little of the solid into a hot Bunsen flame, and the metal ion colours the flame.

  1. Clean a nichrome or platinum wire. Dip it in concentrated hydrochloric acid, then hold it in the flame. Repeat until the wire gives no colour.
  2. Dip the clean wire in the acid again, then into the solid sample so a little sticks to it.
  3. Hold it in the hottest part of a blue (non-luminous) Bunsen flame. That means the air hole is open.
  4. Observe the colour of the flame.

Why clean the wire — the 2024 two-mark answer

1. To remove other ions / impurities left on the wire 1

2. So they do not interfere with / mask the colour of the flame 1

Why a blue flame? A yellow (luminous) flame is already coloured, so it would hide the result. A blue flame is almost colourless and much hotter.

Ammonium ions — warm with sodium hydroxide

Spec 2.47 · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q10(b)(iii) · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q7(d)(ii)

Warming an ammonium compound with sodium hydroxide solution gives off ammonia gas, which turns damp red litmus paper blue.

Ammonium ion with hydroxide — ionicNH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → NH₃(g) + H₂O(l)the hydroxide ion takes an H⁺ off the ammonium ion
Ammonium chloride with sodium hydroxideNH₄Cl + NaOH → NaCl + NH₃ + H₂Oany ammonium salt behaves the same way

The ammonium-ion answer — three marks

1. Add sodium hydroxide solution and warm 1

2. Test the gas with damp red litmus paper 1

3. The litmus turns blue (so the gas is ammonia) 1

✗ "Add red litmus to the ammonium solution" — the 2022 and 2024 schemes both reject testing the solution. Hold the paper in the gas above the tube.
✓ "Warm with NaOH(aq); hold damp red litmus at the mouth of the tube; it turns blue."

Copper(II), iron(II) and iron(III) — coloured precipitates

Spec 2.47 · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q5(a) · 2023 school Q10(a)

Adding sodium hydroxide solution to these metal ions makes an insoluble metal hydroxide, and its colour tells you the ion.

IonAdd NaOH(aq) — what you seeHydroxide formed
Copper(II), Cu²⁺Blue precipitateCu(OH)₂
Iron(II), Fe²⁺Green precipitateFe(OH)₂
Iron(III), Fe³⁺Orange-brown (red-brown) precipitateFe(OH)₃
Copper(II) hydroxide precipitateCu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s)2 OH⁻ to balance the 2+ charge
Iron(II) hydroxide precipitateFe²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₂(s)green solid
Iron(III) hydroxide precipitateFe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s)3 OH⁻ for the 3+ ion · orange-brown solid

The number of OH⁻ always matches the charge on the metal ion. Only the metal ion and hydroxide ion appear in the equation. The sodium ions and sulfate (or chloride) ions are spectator ions.

The school's 2023 scheme, on copper(II) sulfate + sodium hydroxide: "must be what you SEE".

✗ "Copper hydroxide forms" — a name, not an observation.
✗ "It fizzes" — rejected. No gas is made.
✓ "A blue precipitate (blue solid) forms."

Halide ions — acidified silver nitrate

Spec 2.48 · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q3(d)(ii)

Add dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution: chloride gives a white precipitate, bromide cream, iodide yellow.

Halide ionPrecipitateColour
Chloride, Cl⁻Silver chloride, AgClWhite
Bromide, Br⁻Silver bromide, AgBrCream
Iodide, I⁻Silver iodide, AgIYellow
Silver halide precipitateAg⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)same shape for Br⁻ → AgBr and I⁻ → AgI

Why add acid first? Carbonate ions would also react with silver ions and form a precipitate, which would give a false positive. The acid reacts with any carbonate ions and removes them as carbon dioxide.

Why nitric acid, and not hydrochloric? Hydrochloric acid contains chloride ions. They would give a white precipitate whatever was in the sample. Nitric acid adds only nitrate ions, and silver nitrate is soluble.

✗ "Add hydrochloric acid and silver nitrate" — 2022 scheme: "REJECT incorrect acid e.g. HCl or H2SO4".
✓ "Add dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution. A white precipitate shows chloride ions." "Acidified silver nitrate" is allowed for both of the first two marks.

Sulfate ions — acidified barium chloride

Spec 2.48

Add dilute hydrochloric acid, then barium chloride solution: a white precipitate shows sulfate ions.

Barium sulfate precipitateBa²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)barium sulfate is insoluble — white solid

The acid is there for the same reason as before. It removes carbonate ions, which would otherwise form a white precipitate of barium carbonate. Hydrochloric acid is fine this time, because barium chloride is soluble. The chloride ions do not form a precipitate with barium.

✗ "Add sulfuric acid, then barium chloride" — sulfuric acid contains sulfate ions, so you would always get a white precipitate.
✓ "Add dilute hydrochloric acid, then barium chloride solution. A white precipitate forms."

Carbonate ions — acid, fizzing, limewater

Spec 2.48 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q7(d)(ii) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q5(b)

Adding dilute acid to a carbonate gives effervescence, and the gas turns limewater milky, so it is carbon dioxide.

Carbonate ion with acid — ionicCO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂Othe fizzing is the CO₂ escaping
Sodium carbonate with hydrochloric acidNa₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂2HCl because CO₃²⁻ needs two H⁺

The carbonate answer — three marks (2024 M4–M6)

1. Add dilute (hydrochloric) acid 1

2. Bubble the gas through limewater 1

3. The limewater turns milky / cloudy 1

The scheme says acid only. If you add a second, wrong reagent (such as silver nitrate), you lose the mark.

Water — anhydrous copper(II) sulfate

Spec 2.49 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q11(a)(i) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q12(c)(ii)

Water turns white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate blue.

Anhydrous to hydrated copper(II) sulfateCuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l) ⇌ CuSO₄·5H₂O(s)white → blue · heating drives it back to white

"Anhydrous" means "without water". Hydrated copper(II) sulfate crystals are blue. Heat them and the water leaves, so they turn white. Add water back and they turn blue again. That is why 2023 Jan Q12 used this test to show the reaction is reversible.

✗ "Add copper sulfate — it turns blue" — the 2022 Jan scheme ties M2 to saying anhydrous or white copper sulfate. Blue copper sulfate is already hydrated.
✗ "It turns from blue to white" — backwards. The 2023 Jan scheme caps you at one mark for a wrong starting colour.
✓ "Add the liquid to anhydrous copper(II) sulfate. It turns from white to blue."

Pure water — a physical test

Spec 2.50 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q9(b)(iii) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q12(b) · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q9(a)(iv) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q11(a)(ii)

Pure water boils at exactly 100 °C and freezes (melts) at exactly 0 °C.

A pure substance has one fixed boiling point. Dissolved impurities raise the boiling point and lower the freezing point. Impurities also make it boil or melt over a range of temperatures instead of at one sharp value.

The pure-water answer — two marks

1. Measure the boiling point (or the freezing point) 1

2. Pure water boils at 100 °C (or freezes at 0 °C) 1

✗ "Add anhydrous copper sulfate" — that shows water is present, not that it is pure. Salty water turns it blue too. The 2023 scheme ignores any chemical test.
✗ "Evaporate it and see if anything is left" — rejected in 2023.
✓ "Measure the boiling point. If it is exactly 100 °C, the water is pure."

Identifying a compound — putting two tests together

Spec 2.44 – 2.48 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q7(d)(ii) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q5(b) · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q3(d)(ii)

A compound has a cation and an anion, so a "show that this is…" question needs one test for each, and every test needs a reagent and a result.

  1. Split the formula into its two ions, for example lithium chloride → Li⁺ and Cl⁻.
  2. Pick the cation test: a flame test for Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ or Cu²⁺; NaOH(aq) for NH₄⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺.
  3. Pick the anion test: HNO₃ then AgNO₃ for halides, HCl then BaCl₂ for sulfate, acid then limewater for carbonate.
  4. For each test, write the reagent, then the observation, with the exact colour.

2024 six-marker — ammonium carbonate, (NH₄)₂CO₃

Ammonium: add sodium hydroxide solution and warm 1; test the gas with damp red litmus paper 1; it turns blue 1

Carbonate: add dilute hydrochloric acid 1; bubble the gas through limewater 1; it turns milky 1

Use separate samples for each test. Each observation mark depends on the reagent before it, so a wrong reagent can wipe out two marks at once.

04Understanding it

U1

Why does hydrogen need a lighted splint but oxygen a glowing one? What happens if you swap them?

Show answer

Hydrogen is a fuel. A flame sets it alight, and it reacts with oxygen in the air so fast that you hear a small explosion, the squeaky pop.

Oxygen does not burn itself. It makes other things burn better. A splint that is only glowing does not have enough oxygen in air to flame, but in pure oxygen it relights.

Swap them and neither test works. A glowing splint cannot light hydrogen properly. A splint that is already burning just burns a bit brighter in oxygen, which is hard to see. That is why the schemes reject the wrong splint.

U2

Why must you add dilute nitric acid before silver nitrate, and why can the acid not be hydrochloric?

Show answer

Acid first: carbonate ions would also form a precipitate with silver ions. The acid reacts with any carbonate and removes it as CO₂ gas. Then a precipitate can only mean a halide.

Not hydrochloric: HCl contains chloride ions. They would make white silver chloride even if the sample had no chloride in it, so every test would look positive. Nitric acid only adds nitrate ions, and all nitrates are soluble.

U3

For the sulfate test you acidify with hydrochloric acid. Why is that fine here, when it was wrong for the halide test? And why not sulfuric acid?

Show answer

The reagent is barium chloride. Barium ions only precipitate with sulfate (and carbonate, which the acid removes). Barium chloride is soluble, so the extra chloride ions from HCl do nothing.

Sulfuric acid contains sulfate ions. It would make white barium sulfate every time, whatever the sample was. You would be testing your own acid.

U4

Why is the flame-test wire cleaned in concentrated hydrochloric acid, and why use the blue flame and not the yellow one?

Show answer

Cleaning: any ions left from an earlier test would colour the flame too, and could mask the real colour. Sodium is the worst because its bright yellow hides everything else. You dip and heat until the wire gives no colour at all.

Blue flame: the yellow (luminous) flame is already coloured by glowing soot, so it would hide or confuse the result. The blue flame is hotter and almost colourless, so the colour you see comes from the metal ion.

U5

In the ammonium test, why must the litmus paper be damp, and why must it be held in the gas rather than dipped into the solution?

Show answer

Damp: ammonia only acts as an alkali once it dissolves in water and makes OH⁻ ions. Dry paper has no water, so nothing changes.

In the gas: the solution already contains sodium hydroxide, which is an alkali. Dipping red litmus into it turns it blue whether or not ammonium was there. Only the gas given off proves ammonia was made. That is why the schemes give no marks for testing the solution.

U6

Chlorine turns damp blue litmus red and then white. Why both changes, and which one proves it is chlorine?

Show answer

Chlorine reacts with the water on the paper to form acids, so the litmus first turns red. One of those products is a bleach, which destroys the dye, so the paper goes white.

Plenty of gases are acidic and turn litmus red. Only chlorine bleaches it. The bleaching is the mark, so always say it.

U7

Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate turns blue with seawater. Why is it a test for water, but not a test for pure water?

Show answer

The white powder turns blue whenever any water reaches it. It cannot tell whether salt or anything else is dissolved in that water.

Purity needs a physical test. A pure substance has one fixed boiling point. Pure water boils at exactly 100 °C. Seawater boils above 100 °C, because dissolved salt raises the boiling point.

U8

Sodium hydroxide solution gives a gas with ammonium chloride, but a precipitate with copper(II) chloride. Why the difference?

Show answer

With the ammonium ion, OH⁻ takes an H⁺ from NH₄⁺. That makes ammonia, NH₃, which is a gas and escapes when warmed.

With copper(II) ions, OH⁻ joins the metal ion: Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂. Copper(II) hydroxide is insoluble, so it appears as a blue solid, a precipitate. Iron(II) and iron(III) behave the same way, but their hydroxides are green and orange-brown.

D15

A student tests two solutions, X and Y.

Solution X gives a yellow flame in a flame test. When dilute nitric acid and then silver nitrate solution are added, a yellow precipitate forms.

Solution Y gives a green precipitate with sodium hydroxide solution. When dilute hydrochloric acid and then barium chloride solution are added, a white precipitate forms.

(a) Name the compound in X. (2)
(b) Write the ionic equation for the formation of the yellow precipitate. (1)
(c) Name the compound in Y. (2)

Show answer

(a) Sodium (from the yellow flame) 1; iodide (from the yellow precipitate) — so sodium iodide 1

(b) Ag⁺ + I⁻ → AgI 1

(c) Iron(II) (from the green precipitate) 1; sulfate (from the white precipitate) — so iron(II) sulfate 1
