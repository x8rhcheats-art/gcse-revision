02How it actually works

An atom is a tiny, heavy, positive nucleus (protons + neutrons) wrapped in shells of almost weightless electrons. Two numbers on the periodic table encode everything the exam asks. Learn to read them, and every question in this module becomes counting.

Atoms, elements and molecules

Spec 1.14 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q2(a)

An atom is the smallest particle of an element; a molecule is two or more atoms covalently bonded together.

An element contains only one type of atom. A molecule can be of an element or of a compound:

  • Cl₂, O₂, H₂ — molecules of elements (two atoms, but the same kind).
  • HCl, H₂O, CO₂ — molecules of compounds (different elements bonded).
  • O on its own — a single atom, not a molecule.

That is the whole of the 2023 January multiple-choice question: "which is a molecule of an element?" — Cl₂.

The three particles

Spec 1.15 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q5(a)

Protons and neutrons sit in the nucleus and carry almost all the mass; electrons orbit in shells and carry the negative charge.

ParticleRelative massRelative chargeWhere
Proton1+1nucleus
Neutron10nucleus
Electron1/1836 (almost 0)−1shells around the nucleus

Edexcel's version of the table prints the electron's mass as 1/2000 — either is accepted. Atoms are neutral because the number of protons equals the number of electrons: +1 and −1 cancel.

Atomic number and mass number — counting the particles

Spec 1.16 · 2021 Q1 · 2022 Q2 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q7(a)

Atomic number = protons. Mass number = protons + neutrons. Everything else is subtraction.

Atomic number is the number of protons — that alone. The 2022 scheme ignored any mention of electrons. Mass number is protons + neutrons, because those are the particles with mass.

Worked example — ¹¹₅B

  1. Protons = the small bottom number (atomic number) = 5.
  2. Neutrons = mass number − atomic number = 11 − 5 = 6.
  3. Electrons = protons, because the atom is neutral = 5.

For an ion, change only step 3: add one electron for each − charge, remove one for each + charge. I⁻ from iodine-127 (53 protons): 53 p, 127 − 53 = 74 n, 53 + 1 = 54 electrons.

Isotopes

Spec 1.16 · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q7(a) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q1(b) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q5(b)

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

So isotopes have the same atomic number and a different mass number. ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl both have 17 protons; one has 18 neutrons, the other 20.

Isotopes have identical chemical properties. Chemistry is decided by electrons, and both isotopes have 17 electrons arranged 2.8.7. The extra neutrons only change the mass.

✗ "Isotopes have different numbers of protons." — that would make them different elements.
✗ "Isotopes have a different mass." — true, but not the definition; no mark.
✓ "Atoms of the same element (same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons." — two marks, one for each half.

Relative atomic mass — the weighted average

Spec 1.16, 1.17 · 2023 Q9a · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q3(c) · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q7(b) · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q7(a)

Ar is the mean mass of an element's atoms, weighted by how common each isotope is.

Relative atomic mass from isotopesAr = Σ(isotope mass × % abundance) ÷ 100Σ means "add up for every isotope" · isotope mass = the mass number · % abundance = how common that isotope is

Worked example — copper, ⁶³Cu 69.5% and ⁶⁵Cu 30.5%

  1. Multiply each mass by its percentage: 63 × 69.5 = 4378.5 and 65 × 30.5 = 1982.5
  2. Add them: 4378.5 + 1982.5 = 6361
  3. Divide by 100: 6361 ÷ 100 = 63.61
  4. Round as asked: 63.6 to 3 significant figures

Check: the answer must sit between the isotope masses, nearer the more common one. 63.6 is between 63 and 65, near 63. ✓

Three isotopes work the same way. Magnesium (2024 Edexcel): (24 × 79.0 + 25 × 10.0 + 26 × 11.0) ÷ 100 = 2432 ÷ 100 = 24.3. This is why chlorine's Ar is 35.5 even though no chlorine atom weighs 35.5.

Electronic configurations — the 2.8.8 rule

Spec 1.19 · 2023 Q1 · 2025 Q6a · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q1(a) · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q1(a)

Electrons fill shells from the inside out: 2 in the first shell, then 8, then 8, then the rest.

  1. Find the number of electrons. For an atom this is the atomic number.
  2. Fill the first shell with 2.
  3. Fill the second shell with up to 8, then the third with up to 8.
  4. Put whatever is left in the fourth shell (only potassium and calcium in the first 20).
  5. Check the digits add up to the number of electrons.
  • Sodium, 11 electrons → 2.8.1
  • Silicon, 14 electrons → 2.8.4 (2024 Edexcel)
  • Phosphorus, 15 electrons → 2.8.5 (2025's one-marker, and 2023 Edexcel's)
  • Calcium, 20 electrons → 2.8.8.2

In a shell diagram, draw the electrons as dots or crosses on circles, in pairs where you can, and write the symbol or "nucleus" in the centre.

Group and period — reading the table

Spec 1.18, 1.22 · 2022 Q2b · 2023 Q1 · 2024 Q1 · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q1 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q1

Group = number of outer-shell electrons. Period = number of occupied shells.

The periodic table lists elements in order of atomic number. Its layout is the electron structure:

  1. Write the configuration, e.g. 2.8.3.
  2. Group = the last number (outer electrons) → Group 3. The reason mark is "because it has 3 electrons in its outer shell".
  3. Period = how many numbers there are (shells) → Period 3.
  4. Identity = add the numbers to get the atomic number → 13 → aluminium.

Groups run down in columns; periods run across in rows. The exception: Group 0 (also called Group 8), the noble gases, have full outer shells — helium has 2, the rest 8.

✗ "2.8.3 is in period 3 because it has 3 outer electrons." — that is the group reason.
✓ "Group 3: three electrons in the outer shell. Period 3: three occupied shells."

It is a coincidence when group and period match. Calcium, 2.8.8.2, is Group 2, Period 4 — the 2023 January Edexcel answer.

Ions and their charges

Spec 1.22 · 2023 Q1 · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q1(e) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q1(b)

Atoms lose or gain electrons to get a full outer shell, and the charge is the number of electrons moved.

  1. Groups 1, 2, 3 (metals) lose their outer electrons → ions of 1+, 2+, 3+.
  2. Groups 5, 6, 7 (non-metals) gain enough to reach 8 → ions of 3−, 2−, 1−.
  3. The ion's configuration is the atom's, with electrons removed or added. Na 2.8.1 → Na⁺ 2.8. Cl 2.8.7 → Cl⁻ 2.8.8.

Protons never change. An ion with three more protons than electrons has a charge of 3+. Write the charge with its size: +1, not "positive".

Metals and non-metals

Spec 1.20, 1.21 · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q1

Metals are on the left and centre of the table; non-metals are on the right — and two tests tell them apart.

TestMetalNon-metal
Electrical conductivityConducts (as a solid)Does not conduct (graphite is the exception)
Oxide dissolved in waterBasic oxide — alkaline, pH above 7Acidic oxide — pH below 7

Magnesium burns to MgO, a basic oxide. Sulfur burns to SO₂, which dissolves to give an acidic solution. "An element that forms a basic oxide" was a 2024 Edexcel answer — lithium, a metal.

Why a group reacts alike — and why noble gases don't

Spec 1.23, 1.24 · 2023 Edexcel 2C Q1(b) · 2024 Q1c

Elements in the same group have the same number of outer electrons, so they react in the same way.

Reactions happen at the outer shell. Lithium (2.1), sodium (2.8.1) and potassium (2.8.8.1) each lose one outer electron, so all form 1+ ions and similar compounds.

The noble gases (Group 0) have a full outer shell. They do not need to lose, gain or share electrons, so they are unreactive and exist as single atoms. An element with a full outer shell has 0 electrons available for bonding — 2024's answer.

The noble gas answer — two marks

1. It has a full outer shell (8 electrons, or 2 for helium) 1

2. So it does not need to lose, gain or share electrons 1

04Understanding it

U1

An atom has the electronic configuration 2.8.3. Without being told the element, state its group, its period, the charge on its ion, and — using a periodic table — its identity.

Show answer

Group 3 — three outer electrons. Period 3 — three occupied shells. Ion: loses the three outer electrons → 3+ (configuration 2.8).

Identity: 2 + 8 + 3 = 13 electrons = 13 protons → atomic number 13 → aluminium.

Same-numbers-different-meanings is the trap: "3" appears as both group and period here by coincidence. Group counts the outer electrons; period counts the shells. The 2023 cohort mixing them up is why this question keeps returning.

U2

Chlorine has two isotopes, ³⁵Cl (75%) and ³⁷Cl (25%). Explain why the two isotopes react identically, and calculate Ar.

Show answer

Identical chemistry: both have 17 protons and therefore 17 electrons in the same arrangement (2.8.7). Chemical behaviour is decided entirely by the electrons — the extra two neutrons in ³⁷Cl change only the mass.

Ar = (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100 = (2625 + 925) ÷ 100 = 35.5.

Check: between 35 and 37, closer to 35 (the abundant one). ✓

U3

Why does an atom of element X with 14 protons, 14 electrons and 15 neutrons have a mass number of 29 — and why are answers of 14, 15 and 28 each wrong in a different way?

Show answer

Mass number counts what has mass in the nucleus: protons + neutrons = 14 + 15 = 29.

The wrong answers (this was 2022's actual MCQ, distractors and all): 14 is the proton count — the atomic number, not the mass number. 15 is just the neutrons. 28 is protons + electrons — but electrons weigh ≈1/1836 of a proton, effectively nothing, and never contribute to the mass number.

U4

Sodium's ion is Na⁺ but its configuration is 2.8.1. Talk through what the atom loses, what the ion's configuration becomes, and why the exam refuses the answer "positive" for the charge.

Show answer

The single outer electron is lost (it is the easiest route to a full outer shell). The ion's configuration is 2.8 — eleven protons now hold ten electrons, so the net charge is +1.

"Positive" was ignored (scored nothing) in 2023 because it answers a different question — direction without size. Mg loses two electrons and is also "positive", but its ion is 2+. The charge is a number with a sign: +1, 2+, 3−, written either way round, or as the symbol Na⁺.

U5

Element Y is in Group 0 (the noble gases). How many electrons does it have available for bonding, and what does that predict about its chemistry?

Show answer

Zero. A Group 0 element has a full outer shell — no tendency to lose, gain or share anything. (2024's answer line accepted "0".)

Prediction: unreactive. Noble gases form almost no compounds and exist as single atoms. A full shell is the stable arrangement every other atom reaches by ionic or covalent bonding.

U6

Argon (Ar 39.9) comes before potassium (Ar 39.1) in the periodic table, even though it is heavier. Why is that the right place for it?

Show answer

The table is in order of atomic number, not mass. Argon has 18 protons, potassium 19, so argon comes first.

It also has to be that way for the groups to work. Argon (2.8.8) has a full outer shell and belongs with the unreactive noble gases. Potassium (2.8.8.1) has one outer electron and reacts like sodium. Ordering by mass would put each in the wrong group. Argon is heavier only because its common isotope has more neutrons — and neutrons do not affect chemistry.

U7

Lithium, sodium and potassium all react with water in the same way. Explain this using electronic configurations — and say why the number of shells does not change the type of reaction.

Show answer

Li 2.1, Na 2.8.1, K 2.8.8.1 — each has one electron in its outer shell. In a reaction each atom loses that one electron to form a 1+ ion. Same outer shell → same reaction → same type of product (a hydroxide and hydrogen).

The inner shells are full and take no part. More shells change how easily the outer electron is lost (it is further from the nucleus, so less strongly attracted), which changes how fast the reaction goes — but not what kind of reaction it is.

U8

An ion X³⁺ has the electronic configuration 2.8. Identify X, and explain each step of your reasoning.

Show answer

Step 1: the ion has 2 + 8 = 10 electrons.

Step 2: a 3+ charge means the atom lost 3 electrons, so the atom had 10 + 3 = 13 electrons.

Step 3: a neutral atom has protons = electrons, so 13 protons → atomic number 13 → aluminium (2.8.3, Group 3 — which fits a 3+ ion).

The trap is reading 2.8 as neon. The configuration belongs to the ion; the element is set by the protons, which never change.
