Bonding and Structures

Three bond types, four structures, and one iron law: properties follow structure. The killer distinction on every paper is bonds versus intermolecular forces — confuse them once in an answer and the whole explanation scores zero.

One error dominates this topic, and the schemes kill entire answers for it:

2021: "If any reference to/implication of breaking covalent or ionic bonds scores 0/2" — for why hydrogen's boiling point is low.

2025: the ionic melting-point answer scored 0 marks if it mentioned covalent bonds, molecules, or intermolecular forces.

2022: "Mention of intermolecular forces in graphite: no M2 or M5."

Giant structures have bonds (or ionic/metallic attractions) between particles; simple molecular substances have weak forces between molecules. Pick the right story before writing a word.

02How it actually works

Atoms bond three ways: give electrons away (ionic), share them (covalent), or pool them (metallic). But melting points and conductivity are set by the structure — giant or simple molecular — not by the bond type alone. Work out the structure first; everything else follows from it.

Which kind of bond? Look at the elements

Spec 1.37, 1.44, 1.53C · 2025 Q6b

Metal + non-metal → ionic. Non-metal + non-metal → covalent. Metal on its own → metallic.

Every atom is trying to get a full outer shell. How it does that depends on its partner:

ElementsBondWhat the electrons doExample
Metal + non-metalIonicTransferred from metal to non-metalNaCl, MgO, Ca₃P₂
Non-metal + non-metalCovalentShared in pairsH₂O, NH₃, CO₂, diamond
Metal onlyMetallicDelocalised — free to moveMg, Cu, Fe

Decide this first. Explaining an ionic compound with sharing, or a molecule with ions, scores zero — the 2025 Ca₃P₂ scheme gave nothing for any mention of sharing.

Ionic bonding — electron transfer

Spec 1.37, 1.40 · 2021 Q3c · 2023 Q2a · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q4(b) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q8(b)

Metal atoms lose their outer electrons and non-metal atoms gain them, so both end up with full outer shells as ions.

  1. Write both configurations. Mg 2.8.2 and O 2.6.
  2. Say how many electrons the metal loses. Mg loses two → Mg²⁺, 2.8.
  3. Say how many the non-metal gains. O gains two → O²⁻, 2.8.
  4. Balance the numbers. If they don't match, use more atoms: Na loses one, O needs two, so two Na atoms per O → Na₂O.

The 2023 marker: "Needed the idea of 2 electrons to score — several just said electrons moved from magnesium to oxygen without specifying how many." Always give the number.

Drawing an ionic dot-and-cross diagram

1. Draw the metal ion's shell empty (outer electrons gone), or show only the full shell underneath. Put it in square brackets with the charge: [Na]⁺.

2. Draw the non-metal ion with a full outer shell of 8, using a different symbol for the gained electron(s): [Cl]⁻ with 7 dots and 1 cross.

3. Brackets and charges on both ions. Only outer electrons need be shown.

The ionic bond is an attraction

Spec 1.41 · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q5(c) · 2025 Q6b

An ionic bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.

The transfer makes the ions; the attraction is the bond. "Transfer of electrons" alone is not a description of the bond, and "sharing" is always wrong here.

Formula from charges (2025 Q6b): calcium forms Ca²⁺, phosphorus forms P³⁻. The charges must cancel: 3 × (2+) = 6+ balances 2 × (3−) = 6−, so the formula is Ca₃P₂. Module 3 drills this.

Giant ionic lattices — melting point and conductivity

Spec 1.42, 1.43 · 2023 Q6e · 2025 Q6e · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q8(b) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q6

Every ion in a giant ionic lattice is held by strong attractions to all the oppositely charged ions around it, so it takes a lot of energy to melt.

The high-melting-point answer — three marks

1. It has a giant ionic lattice / structure 1

2. With strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions 1

3. So a lot of energy is needed to overcome them 1

Any mention of covalent bonds, molecules or intermolecular forces: 0 marks.

Conductivity: the charge carriers are the ions.

  • Solid: ions are in fixed positions in the lattice, so they cannot move — no conduction.
  • Molten or dissolved in water: the ions are free to move and carry the charge — it conducts.

Electrons never enter this answer. "Electrons can't move in the solid" does not score.

Covalent bonding — a shared pair pulled by two nuclei

Spec 1.44, 1.45 · 2021 Q2c · 2022 Q10a · 2024 Edexcel 2C Q7(a) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q9(a)

A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons, held by the electrostatic attraction between that pair and the nuclei of both atoms.

"Describe the forces in a covalent bond" — two marks

1. (Electrostatic) attraction between the shared pair of electrons 1

2. and the nuclei of both atoms 1

2021 and 2022 both refused the mark for "attracted to the nucleus". The plural is the mark.

Covalent bonds are strong. That matters later: what is weak in a simple molecular substance is never the bond.

Dot-and-cross diagrams for molecules

Spec 1.46 · 2021 Q2d · 2023 Q4a · 2024 Q3a · 2024 Edexcel 1C Q7(b) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q7(a)

Share just enough pairs so that every atom ends with a full outer shell — 2 for hydrogen, 8 for everything else.

  1. Count each atom's outer electrons. H 1, C 4, N 5, O 6, Cl 7.
  2. Work out how many bonds each needs. H needs 1 more, O needs 2, N needs 3, C needs 4.
  3. Draw the shared pairs in the overlap, one dot and one cross per pair.
  4. Add the lone pairs left over on each atom.
  5. Check every atom: H has 2, every other atom has 8 in its shell.

Marks are lost to miscounted lone pairs, not to untidy circles. The schemes accept any mix of dots and crosses.

Simple molecular substances — why strong bonds still boil low

Spec 1.47, 1.51 · 2021 Q2b · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q3(b)

Melting or boiling a simple molecular substance only separates the molecules; it overcomes the weak intermolecular forces and breaks no covalent bonds.

H₂, Cl₂, CO₂, water and alkanes have strong covalent bonds inside each molecule, but only weak intermolecular forces between molecules. That is why they are gases, liquids, or solids with low melting points.

The two-mark script for "strong bonds, low boiling point"

1. There are only weak forces of attraction between the molecules (weak intermolecular forces) 1

2. So little energy is needed to overcome them / separate the molecules 1

Any reference to breaking covalent bonds: 0/2. The bonds are bystanders.

Covalent substances usually do not conduct: the molecules are neutral, so there are no ions and no free electrons to carry charge.

Bigger molecules, higher boiling points

Spec 1.48 · 2024 Q9b · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q9(b)

As relative molecular mass increases, the intermolecular forces get stronger, so more energy is needed to separate the molecules.

  1. The molecules get bigger (higher Mr).
  2. So the intermolecular forces between them get stronger.
  3. So more energy is needed to overcome them — a higher boiling point.

Two exam versions: longer alkanes boil higher (2024 school), and the halogens F₂ → Cl₂ → Br₂ → I₂ go from gas to liquid to solid (2023 January Edexcel). The forces are between molecules, never "between atoms".

Giant covalent structures — diamond and graphite

Spec 1.49, 1.50 · 2022 Q10b · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q8(a) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1C Q9(c)

In a giant covalent structure every atom is covalently bonded into one huge network, so melting means breaking millions of strong covalent bonds.

Here, and only here, "covalent bonds are broken on melting" is the right phrase. That is why diamond, graphite and silicon dioxide have very high melting points.

PropertyDiamondGraphite
Bonding of each carbonBonded to 4 others — rigid 3-D (tetrahedral) networkBonded to 3 others — flat hexagonal layers
HardnessVery hard — strong bonds in every directionSoft, slippery — layers slide over each other
Conducts?No — no free electrons; all 4 outer electrons are in bondsYes — one delocalised electron per carbon moves along the layers
UseCutting toolsLubricant, electrodes

For the Edexcel six-marker, give each point for both forms: bonded to 4 vs 3, no delocalised electrons vs delocalised electrons, hard vs soft.

Edexcel allows "weak forces between the layers" of graphite. The school's 2022 scheme cancelled marks for the word intermolecular in graphite — graphite has no molecules. Safe phrase: "the layers can slide over each other".

C₆₀ fullerene — carbon that is molecular

Spec 1.50 · 2022 Q10c · 2022 Edexcel 1C Q8(b) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1C Q10(b)

C₆₀ is made of separate molecules of 60 carbon atoms, so it is simple molecular and melts far lower than graphite.

  1. Graphite is giant covalent.
  2. Melting graphite breaks covalent bonds.
  3. C₆₀ has a simple molecular structure.
  4. Melting C₆₀ only overcomes the weak intermolecular forces between the molecules.
  5. Breaking covalent bonds needs much more energy than overcoming intermolecular forces.

That list is the mark scheme — "any four from" these five, on both the school's 2022 paper and two Edexcel papers.

Metallic bonding and the properties of metals

Spec 1.52C, 1.53C, 1.54C · 2025 Q4a · 2022 Edexcel 2C Q4(c)

A metal is a lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons, held together by the electrostatic attraction between them.

In the 2-D diagram, draw rows of circles labelled + (the positive ions) with electrons (e⁻) scattered between them. The two labels are positive ions and delocalised electrons — "protons" was rejected.

  • Conduct electricity: the delocalised electrons are free to move through the structure.
  • Malleable: the ions are in layers that can slide over each other without the bonding breaking.
  • High melting point: strong attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons.

2025 rejected intermolecular forces in the malleability answer. Metals have no molecules.

Identifying a mystery substance from its properties

2021 Q3a · 2023 Edexcel 1C Q3 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2C Q2

Two facts — does it conduct, and when? how high is its melting point? — identify the structure every time.

  1. Conducts as a solid? Yes → metal (graphite is the one exception).
  2. Conducts only when molten or dissolved? → giant ionic.
  3. Never conducts, low melting point? → simple molecular.
  4. Never conducts, very high melting point? → giant covalent (e.g. diamond).

2021's grid: mp −101 °C, never conducts → simple molecular (a gas at 20 °C since bp −35 °C); mp 1063 °C, conducts solid → metal; mp 801 °C, conducts molten only → ionic; mp 3550 °C, never conducts → giant covalent (diamond). To say whether something is a gas at a temperature, compare that temperature with its boiling point.

03Cheat sheet

The one distinction everything follows from

Giant structures: strong bonds/attractions are overcome on melting. Simple molecular: weak forces BETWEEN molecules are overcome — the covalent bonds never break. Choose the story before you write; mixing them scores zero.

Conduction: metals and graphite — delocalised electrons. Ionic — ions, only when molten or dissolved. Molecules — nothing.

CompoundA substance containing two or more elements chemically bonded/combined (reject: mixture)
Malleable (metal)Can be hammered into shape because layers of ions slide over one another

04Understanding it

U1

The H–H bond is one of the strongest single bonds known, yet hydrogen boils at −253 °C. Resolve the contradiction — and explain why "some of the covalent bonds break on boiling" scores 0/2 rather than 1/2.

Show answer

No contradiction: boiling and bond-breaking are different events. Boiling only has to separate whole molecules from each other, and between H₂ molecules there are just weak intermolecular forces — very little energy overcomes them, hence −253 °C. The strong H–H bond travels intact inside every molecule of the gas.

It scores 0/2 because the error isn't a missing detail — it is the wrong physical story. An answer built on bond-breaking describes a chemical decomposition, not boiling, so neither marking point is present. That is why the schemes treat one mention of bond-breaking as fatal rather than partial.

U2

Sodium chloride at 801 °C and diamond at 3550 °C both have giant structures. What is being overcome when each one melts, and why is "strong bonds" an incomplete answer for NaCl?

Show answer

NaCl: the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions throughout the lattice. The three-marker wants the structure named (giant ionic), the attraction identified (between oppositely charged ions), and the energy statement (a lot needed to overcome it).

Diamond: actual covalent bonds — every carbon is pinned to four others, and melting means breaking that network. This is the one place "bonds break on melting" is the right answer.

"Strong bonds" alone fails for NaCl because it never says what kind of attraction or between what — and worse, it invites covalent language, which zeroes the answer. Name the particles: ions, oppositely charged.

U3

Graphite and C₆₀ fullerene are both pure carbon with covalent bonds. Why does graphite survive to ~3600 °C while C₆₀ melts far lower — and which single word in a student's answer would destroy the graphite half?

Show answer

Different structures. Graphite is giant covalent: one continuous network, so melting must break covalent bonds — hugely expensive. C₆₀ is simple molecular: separate 60-atom balls, held to each other only by weak intermolecular forces; melting just pulls the balls apart and leaves every bond intact. Covalent bonds need far more energy than IMF — that comparison is its own mark.

The destroying word: "intermolecular" anywhere near graphite. Graphite has no molecules, so it can have no intermolecular forces — the 2022 scheme cancelled both graphite marks when they appeared.

U4

Explain why copper conducts electricity as a solid, sodium chloride only when molten, and chlorine never — naming the moving particle in each case.

Show answer

Copper: delocalised electrons move through the lattice of positive ions — mobile charge exists already, so the solid conducts.

Sodium chloride: the charge carriers are the ions themselves. Solid: locked in the lattice — no conduction. Molten (or dissolved): ions free to move — conducts. Electrons never enter this story.

Chlorine: neutral molecules, no ions, no delocalised electrons — no charged particle free to move, ever.

Conduction answers live or die on naming the right particle: electrons for metals (and graphite), ions for ionic compounds, nothing for covalent substances.

U5

Use the charges rule to explain why calcium phosphide is Ca₃P₂ and not CaP — and why a sharing-based explanation was awarded zero.

Show answer

Calcium (Group 2) forms Ca²⁺; phosphorus (Group 5) gains three electrons to form P³⁻. A formula must be electrically neutral: 2+ and 3− don't cancel one-for-one. The smallest balance is three calciums (6+) against two phosphides (6−) → Ca₃P₂.

Sharing scored zero because this is a metal + non-metal — an ionic compound. Explaining an ionic formula with covalent ideas is the same mistake as intermolecular forces in graphite: the wrong model, not a partial answer. Metal + non-metal → transfer and charges, always.

U6

Metals can be hammered into sheets without shattering; ionic crystals crack. Explain both from their structures.

Show answer

Metal: the lattice is layers of identical positive ions in a shared electron sea. Hammering makes the layers slide — but every new position looks like the old one: positive ions still surrounded by the delocalised electrons that hold them. The bonding survives the slide → the metal changes shape without breaking (malleable).

Ionic crystal: sliding a layer by one position puts like charges next to each other — positive against positive. Attraction flips to repulsion and the crystal shatters.

For the exam: malleability = layers (of ions) slide over each other — and no intermolecular forces anywhere near this answer (2025 rejected them).

U7

Fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine is a liquid and iodine is a solid at room temperature. All four are X₂ molecules with one covalent bond. Explain the trend.

Show answer

All four are simple molecular, so melting and boiling only overcome the intermolecular forces. The covalent bond inside each molecule plays no part.

Down the group the molecules get bigger — Mr rises from 38 (F₂) to 254 (I₂). Bigger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces, so more energy is needed to separate them. Boiling points rise, and by iodine the forces are strong enough to hold a solid at room temperature.

The same argument explains why long-chain alkanes boil higher than short ones.

U8

Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon in giant covalent structures. Why does graphite conduct electricity while diamond does not?

Show answer

Carbon has 4 outer electrons. In diamond each carbon forms 4 covalent bonds, so all four electrons are held in bonds. None are free to move — no conduction.

In graphite each carbon forms only 3 bonds. The fourth electron is delocalised — it can move along the layers and carry charge. So graphite conducts, like a metal.

Conduction always needs a charged particle that is free to move. Ask that question first, whatever the structure.

05Exam drill

D1

Sodium (2.8.1) reacts with chlorine (2.8.7) to form sodium chloride.

(a) Describe the changes in electronic configuration when the atoms form sodium chloride. (3)
(b) Explain why sodium chloride conducts electricity when molten but not when solid. (2)

Show answer

(a) Sodium loses one electron 1; chlorine gains one electron 1; Na becomes 2.8 AND Cl becomes 2.8.8 1 — wrong number of electrons transferred caps at 2; any covalent language caps at 1

(b) Solid: ions are fixed in the lattice / cannot move 1; molten: ions are free to move (and carry charge) 1 — no marks for electrons moving

D2

(a) Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for a molecule of ammonia, NH₃. Show outer electrons only. (2)
(b) Describe the forces of attraction in a covalent bond. (2)

Show answer

(a) Three N–H bonding pairs 1; one lone pair on nitrogen, rest fully correct 1 — any combination of dots and crosses accepted

(b) The shared/bonding pair(s) of electrons 1 are (electrostatically) attracted to the nuclei of both atoms 1 — "nuclei" plural; one nucleus loses the mark

D3

The bonding in a hydrogen molecule is strong, yet the boiling point of hydrogen is −253 °C. Explain why the boiling point of hydrogen is low. (2)

Show answer

There are only weak forces of attraction (weak intermolecular forces) between the molecules 1; so little (heat) energy is needed to overcome the forces / separate the molecules 1

Any reference to breaking covalent (or ionic) bonds: 0/2. Accept correctly named IMF, e.g. van der Waals'.

D4

Magnesium oxide has a melting point of 2852 °C. Explain why magnesium oxide has a high melting point. (3)

Show answer

Giant ionic structure/lattice 1; strong (electrostatic) attraction between oppositely charged ions 1; a lot of energy is needed to overcome the attractions 1

Mention of covalent bonds, molecules or intermolecular forces: 0 marks — the 2025 rule.

D5

Graphite and C₆₀ fullerene both contain covalent bonds but have different structures. Explain why C₆₀ fullerene has a much lower melting point than graphite. Refer to structure and bonding in your answer. (4)

Show answer

Any four from this list of five:

  • graphite is giant covalent 1
  • melting graphite breaks covalent bonds 1
  • C₆₀ is (simple) molecular 1
  • melting C₆₀ overcomes intermolecular forces (between molecules) 1

The fifth point, which can replace any one above: covalent bonds need much more energy to break than intermolecular forces need to overcome. Putting intermolecular forces in graphite cancels the graphite marks.

D6

(b) State what is meant by the term compound. (2)

Show answer

(a) Metal = B (conducts as a solid) 1; diamond = D (giant covalent — huge mp, never conducts) 1; ionic = C (conducts only molten) 1; gas at 20 °C = A (bp below 20 °C — simple molecular) 1

(b) A substance containing two or more elements 1 chemically bonded/combined 1 — "mixture" is rejected

D7

Magnesium is a typical metal.

(a) Name the two types of particle in the metallic structure of magnesium. (2)
(b) Explain why magnesium is malleable. (2)

Show answer

(a) Positive (magnesium) ions 1; delocalised electrons 1 — "protons" rejected

(b) The ions are arranged in layers 1; the layers can slide over one another (without the metallic bonding breaking) 1 — no intermolecular forces here

D8

Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for a molecule of nitrogen, N₂. Show outer electrons only. (2)

Show answer

Three shared pairs (six electrons) between the two nitrogen atoms 1; one lone pair on each nitrogen atom 1

Check: each N has 6 shared + 2 lone = 8 electrons.

D9

The boiling points of the halogens increase from fluorine to iodine. Explain this trend. (3)

Show answer

The molecules get bigger / relative molecular mass increases 1; so the intermolecular forces get stronger 1; so more energy is needed to overcome them / separate the molecules 1

Any mention of breaking covalent bonds scores 0.

D10

Diamond and graphite are giant covalent structures made of carbon atoms. Discuss the structures and properties of diamond and graphite. (6)

Show answer

In diamond each carbon atom is covalently bonded to 4 others / tetrahedral 1

In graphite each carbon atom is bonded to 3 others, in layers 1

Diamond does not conduct electricity but graphite does 1

Diamond has no delocalised electrons; graphite has delocalised electrons that can move 1

Diamond is hard but graphite is soft 1

In diamond many strong bonds must be broken; in graphite the layers can slide over each other 1

From 2022 Edexcel 1C Q8(a). Avoid "intermolecular" for graphite.

D11

Lithium (2.1) reacts with oxygen (2.6) to form lithium oxide, Li₂O. Describe the changes in electronic configuration and explain why the formula contains two lithium atoms. (3)

Show answer

Each lithium atom loses one electron, becoming Li⁺ (2) 1; each oxygen atom gains two electrons, becoming O²⁻ (2.8) 1; so two lithium atoms are needed to supply the two electrons for one oxygen / to balance the charges 1

D12

Calcium bromide melts at 730 °C. Caffeine melts at 235 °C. Explain this difference in terms of structure and bonding. (5)

Show answer

Calcium bromide has a giant ionic lattice 1; with strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions 1

Caffeine has a simple molecular structure 1; with weak intermolecular forces between the molecules 1

More energy is needed to overcome the attractions between the ions than the forces between the molecules 1

From 2024 Edexcel 1C Q4(c). Keep the two stories apart: ions for one, molecules for the other.

D13

Magnesium conducts electricity as a solid. Magnesium chloride conducts electricity only when molten. Explain these observations. (4)

Show answer

Magnesium has delocalised electrons 1; which are free to move through the structure 1

In solid magnesium chloride the ions are in fixed positions / cannot move 1; when molten the ions are free to move 1

D14

Draw dot-and-cross diagrams, showing outer electrons only, for (a) a water molecule, H₂O, and (b) a carbon dioxide molecule, CO₂. (4)

(b) Two shared pairs (a double bond) between C and each O 1; two lone pairs on each oxygen, none on carbon 1

D15

(a) Describe metallic bonding in terms of electrostatic attractions. (2)
