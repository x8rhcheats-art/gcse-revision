Non-religious accounts of the origin of the universe

Spec 1.1 · The Universe and the Place of Human Beings

  • Big Bang, 13.7 billion years ago — matter compressed, then expanded; no designer and no purpose behind it.
  • Friedman 1922, Lemaître 1927 — the Belgian who expanded the theory was a Roman Catholic priest.
  • Three pieces of evidence — redshift, background cosmic radiation, and gravitational waves detected in March 2014.
  • Steady-state and cyclical — the rival models; steady-state (Bondi, Gold and Hoyle, 1948) is almost entirely rejected.
  • Darwin, On the Origin of the Species 1859 — natural selection, the Galapagos finches, the fossil record, Archaeopteryx, DNA.
  • Humans are one animal among many — not the goal of creation, so no special value beyond what we give ourselves.
More detail

Most scientists hold that the universe began in the Big Bang about 13.7 billion years ago, with no designer and no purpose behind it.

The matter of the universe became so compressed that it produced an enormous expansion; stars formed, our solar system formed about 5 billion years ago, and the earth about 4.6 billion years ago from the dust and gas left over after the sun. Simple molecules on the cooling earth developed into the first life about 4,000 million years ago.

The theory assumes matter is eternal — neither created nor destroyed, only changed from one form into another (the law of thermodynamics). That assumption is itself an argument against creation out of nothing, so it is worth a sentence.

Two names earn credit here, and the second one wins arguments. The theory was first proposed in 1922 by Alexander Friedman, a Russian mathematician. In 1927 it was expanded by Georges Lemaître (1894–1966), a Belgian physicist and Roman Catholic priest, who called it "the hypothesis of the primeval atom or cosmic egg" and said the "cosmic egg exploded at the moment of creation". The term "Big Bang" was coined later. A priest proposing the Big Bang is the single most useful fact in any "is science compatible with religion?" answer.

The three pieces of evidence the course wants you to name:

  1. Redshift — light from other galaxies is at the red end of the spectrum, so they are moving away from us. Edwin Hubble's observations support this.
  2. Background cosmic radiation — radio telescopes detect radiation throughout the universe, thought to be the remnant of the Big Bang.
  3. Gravitational waves — detected in deep space in March 2014, believed to have been triggered by the rapid expansion of the universe.

Two older rival models are still worth a mark. The steady-state model was devised by Sir Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold and Fred Hoyle in 1948: the universe expands continually while maintaining a constant average density, so it has no beginning and no end. It has been almost completely rejected by scientists, the vast majority of whom accept the Big Bang. The cyclical model holds that the universe expands and contracts in infinite cycles.

For human origins, scientists accept Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection. Charles Darwin (1809–1882) sailed on HMS Beagle in 1831 as the official government naturalist, surveying the South American coast. On the Galapagos islands, 600 miles west of Ecuador, he found tortoises on different islands that were related but had differing features, and finches that were related but whose beaks differed according to the food they ate; he concluded they had a common ancestor but had developed different physical features in different environments. Organisms best able to cope in an environment are more likely to survive and reproduce — the survival of the fittest — and gradually species change to keep in tune with a changing environment. He published the theory and the evidence in On the Origin of the Species in 1859, and argued it applies to all animals, humans included.

The evidence to name: the fossil record — the older a fossil, the simpler its structure; it contains species now extinct, and transitional species such as Archaeopteryx, which had characteristics of both reptiles and birds — and, more recently, DNA and genetics. Compiling the human genome has shown that modern humans originated in Africa, reproduced with and eventually replaced older human species, and continued to evolve.

On this view humans are not the goal of creation but one well-adapted animal among many, and features such as intelligence, relationality and creativity are genetic adaptations shaped by the environment. Darwin's book had a huge impact on the Christian community, because if humanity shares its ancestry with apes then human life has no special value other than what humans choose to give it.

Christian beliefs and teachings about creation

Spec 1.1 · Creation, imago Dei, the application of scripture

  • God is the source — all Christians agree; they disagree only over how literally to read Genesis.
  • Two accounts kept side by side — Genesis 1 a distant commanding God, Genesis 2 a potter working closely.
  • Ex nihilo or ex materia — created out of nothing, or out of pre-existing matter.
  • Fundamentalist, conservative, liberal — literal, broadly true, or metaphorical; the three readings let you write "some Christians…".
  • Polkinghorne and Armstrong — science asks how and Genesis asks why; the Big Bang as the will of God.
  • Evolution still leaves problems — biblical authority, Original Sin, a cruel mechanism, and the value of human life.

More detail

All Christians believe God is the source of the universe; they disagree about how literally to read the Genesis accounts.

Genesis gives two accounts, and the editors of the Bible kept both without any attempt to iron out their differences — which is itself an argument that they were never meant as history. In Genesis 1 – 2:4 God is the sole source of power and life, unique and entirely distinct from a natural world subordinate to him; he has merely to say the word and chaos is given shape and form. Each day he adds to his creation, showing that it has a design and a purpose, and at each stage he declares the finished product good. The culmination is humanity, made in his image, given dominion over the rest of the natural order and told to be fruitful and multiply. In Genesis 2:4–25 — the second and earlier account — the world is deserted and utterly empty until a spring rises from the earth; God fashions the first man from the earth like a potter working with clay and breathes life into him, then creates a garden for Adam to cultivate and animals for him to name, and finally makes Eve from one of his ribs as a companion. The God of the second account is not distant: he works in close relationship with his creation, and humanity is there to cultivate the land rather than to have dominion over it. Genesis 3 is the Fall and the expulsion from Eden.

One distinction worth a mark: from the opening of Genesis 1 some Christians conclude that God brought the universe into being from nothing — ex nihilo — while others conclude he created it from pre-existing matter — ex materia.

The three readings of those chapters are the most useful thing in this whole section, because they let you write "some Christians... other Christians..." in almost any answer:

Young Earth Creationists hold that the earth and all life as found today were created in six days around 10,000 years ago; Old Earth Creationists accept that the earth is about four billion years old but hold that all life, humanity included, was intentionally and directly created by God in countless acts of creation throughout history. The Catholic Church accepts the Big Bang as compatible with Genesis — the theory was expanded and published by Georges Lemaître, a Catholic priest, after Alexander Friedman first proposed it in 1922. In January 2010 the Manchester Diocese of the Church of England, in its report Special Agenda IV, encouraged the Church of England to affirm the compatibility of belief in God with the understanding of science, and argued that the divine design of the universe is achieved through the way in which the laws of the universe work and the process of evolution. The report's reasoning is quotable: many people are impressed by scientific explanations and assume those theories prove religion wrong, so the Church should show that science and religion are compatible and can work together — and science shows the amazing nature of the world God created. Some Christians compromise with Intelligent Design — evolution happens, but only up to a point.

Evolution still leaves Christians with problems, and naming one of these lifts a 6-marker:

  • It appears to question the authority of the Bible, because it is hard to fit into the Genesis story.
  • If Genesis is read literally, evolution undermines the basis of Original Sin — and therefore the atoning work of Jesus.
  • Natural selection is cruel and wasteful, which is hard to reconcile with a good God.
  • It seems to undermine the intrinsic value of human life — are we really made in the image of God?

The purpose of the universe and of humanity

Spec 1.1 · Religious beliefs and teachings about its purpose

  • The universe is deliberate — God declared it good, so it has intrinsic value and is not chance.
  • Purpose is relationship with God — humanity was made in his image, to know him and love its neighbour.
  • Six scriptural purposes — relationship, be fruitful, stewardship, agape and evangelism, salvation in Christ, Natural Law.
  • Aquinas’s primary precepts — preserve life, reproduce, educate children, live in society, worship God.
  • The Fall adds gendered roles — Genesis 3:16–19, read literally as distinct roles, by liberals as the author’s culture.
  • Non-religious purposes differ — survival (Darwin), flourishing (Aristotle), self-invented (Sartre), or false altogether (Nietzsche).
More detail

For Christians the universe is part of God's deliberate plan, and the purpose of human beings is relationship with God.

The universe is intrinsically valuable because God declared it good (Genesis 1); it is not just chance. Humanity plays a crucial role, and many Christians link creation directly to salvation. The purposes of human beings, drawn from scripture:

  • To be in a relationship with God — they were created in his image.
  • To be fruitful and multiply, and to rule the earth (Genesis 1:28).
  • Stewardship — in Genesis 2 humanity is the caretaker of God's creation: "The LORD God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to till it and keep it" (Genesis 2:15). The world is only on loan, so humanity will have to account for its treatment of it to God at the End Time.
  • Agape and evangelism — Jesus's Two Great Commandments, "Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind" (Matthew 22:37) and love your neighbour, endorse agape within the human community; his Great Commission before the ascension endorses evangelism: "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19).
  • Salvation in Christ — "For God so loved the world that he gave his only son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life." (John 3:16)
  • For Catholics, the primary precepts of Natural Law. St Thomas Aquinas (c.1225–1274) taught that because God created the universe there is a natural order to the world and fundamental laws set by God, which human beings can work out using their reason. The primary precepts are to preserve life, reproduce, educate children, live in society and worship God; secondary precepts such as "do not lie", "do not steal" and "do not break promises" follow from them.

The Fall adds one more layer that is easy to forget: in Genesis 3:16–19 God tells the woman "I will greatly increase your pangs in childbearing… and he shall rule over you" and the man "By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread until you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; you are dust, and to dust you shall return" — read by many Christians as the origin of distinct roles for men and women in society, and by liberals as a reflection of the author's culture.

Non-religious accounts of purpose are quite different: on a Darwinian view the purpose of life is simply to survive; for Aristotle it is to flourish; for Sartre there is no script, so we must invent purpose ourselves; for Nietzsche all meaning is false.

Stewardship, dominion and responsibility for the planet

Spec 1.1 · Why people believe human beings have a responsibility for the planet

  • The world is God’s, and good — so humans hold it in trust rather than own it.
  • Stewardship, not dominion — Genesis 1:28 read as a duty to safeguard the planet for future generations.
  • Revelation threatens judgement — eternal judgement for those who "destroy the earth"; caring for creation is itself worship.
  • Assisi 1986, the Christian Declaration on Nature — interdependence, protection not destruction, and all exploitation rejected.
  • Non-religious urgency can be greater — expecting no afterlife, this world is the only inheritance there is.
  • Premillennialists disagree — expecting the Tribulation and the Rapture, they put evangelism before conservation.
  • How humans control the environment — farming and irrigation, selective breeding, damming rivers, mining, cities and flood defences; "subdue it" is the verse that licenses it.

"The earth and all life on it is a gift from God given to us to share and develop, not to dominate and exploit… We must consider the welfare of future generations in our planning for and utilisation of the earth's resources." — Pope John Paul II, Sollicitudo Rei Socialis, 1988
Used to argue: the Roman Catholic position — the planet is a gift held in trust, and consumption must be balanced against conservation.

"The universe as a whole is a product of God's creative and imaginative will. All its parts are interdependent. Men and women are to be stewards and curators, not exploiters, of its resources — material, animal and spiritual." — Methodist Conference, 1991
Used to argue: a second, non-Catholic denomination says the same thing, which is how you show "Christians" rather than "Catholics" believe it.

More detail

Christian teaching on the environment rests on the doctrine of creation: the world is God's, it is good, and humans are its stewards.

The religious reasons:

  • God is the creator of the planet and "saw that it was good" — it has intrinsic value (Genesis 1).
  • Genesis 1:28 is read by many Christians as stewardship rather than hierarchical dominion: a duty to protect and safeguard the planet for future generations.
  • The book of Revelation makes clear there will be eternal judgement for those who "destroy the earth".
  • Caring for the world is itself a form of worship for many Christians — which is what harvest festivals celebrate.
  • Creation teaches the interdependence of living things. Humanity has been set apart by God, but is still part of his wider creation and reliant on the world for survival, so damage to the world damages humanity.
  • Scripture extends the duty to animals: "A righteous man has regard for the life of his beast" (Proverbs 12:10).
  • The Christian Declaration on Nature (Assisi, 1986) made three points: all creation, with and without humans, has a close interdependence made that way by God, and the harmony of creation is the glory of God; humans have the role of protecting all created things, not abusing or destroying them; and all exploitation of the world, its resources and its creatures is rejected, humans must not do anything that risks damage to the world, including nuclear warfare.
  • All life should be valued because it comes from God, and living beings are interdependent — caring for the natural world secures the survival of humanity.

The non-religious reasons: an obligation to future generations — non-religious people may feel more urgency than believers, because they do not expect an afterlife, so preserving the world for those who come after is the only way life continues; justice and equality, because environmental destruction causes more natural disasters and it is the poorest who suffer most; and the practical point that ecosystems are interdependent, so without other life forms humanity would flounder.

How responsibility is exercised — the 3-mark answer: recycle and use environmentally friendly products; reduce your carbon footprint by thinking about the holidays you take, the transport you use and the produce you buy; conserve resources (energy-efficient lighting, public transport); encourage government to invest in renewable energy and support international agreements such as the Paris Climate Agreement; plant trees; join organisations such as Greenpeace, the WWF or Friends of the Earth, or Christian initiatives such as Shrinking the Footprint and Operation Noah. Christians can also pray and support church conservation work.

Ways human beings control the environment — a separate 3-mark answer, and the opposite side of the same coin: farming, which replaces wild land with chosen crops, together with irrigation and fertilisers; selective breeding and now genetic modification of crops and livestock; damming and diverting rivers for water and hydroelectric power; clearing forests and mining for timber, fuel and minerals; building cities, roads and sea and flood defences; controlling other species through pesticides, culling and conservation programmes; and heating, air-conditioning and medicine, which let humans live where and how nature would not allow. Christians who read Genesis 1:28 as dominion — "subdue it" and "rule over" — treat this control as God-given and legitimate; those who read it as stewardship argue the control brings a duty with it, so the test of any one of these is whether it safeguards the planet or exploits it. The Assisi Declaration is the line to quote against the exploitative reading, and the non-religious case adds that the power to control the climate is exactly why the responsibility now falls on this generation.

More detail

Christians have reasons both to revere animals and to treat humanity as uniquely special — which is why this topic comes up as a 12-marker.

Reasons Christians should treat other creatures with respect: God is the creator of all life, and Albert Schweitzer argued for a "reverence for all life"; Christians are commanded to be stewards; exploitation is against God's will because animals are part of a creation God called good, and the Bible makes cruelty to animals wrong; Christians who accept evolution are open to commonality — we share common ancestors.

Reasons for human distinctiveness: humanity alone is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27); humanity is given the breath of life (Genesis 2:7); God became flesh as a human — the incarnation (John 1:14); and salvation is on offer to humans (John 3:16). Many Christians would add that humans have souls and animals do not, and that revelation — the Bible and the message of Jesus — is given to humans. Most Christians accept that animals should be treated humanely but may be used for food, labour and some medical testing.

Non-religious views split the same way. Some stress commonality — we evolved from a common ancestor and share a high percentage of DNA with higher primates; Peter Singer argues animals have moral worth because they can make choices and feel pain, and that we should not be "speciesist". Others stress dominance — humans are the dominant species by intelligence, reasoning and self-awareness, and animals cannot exercise moral responsibility, so they should not have rights.

Animal rights: what the Churches actually say

Spec 1.1 · How human beings should treat animals; animal rights

  • Different, not worthless — animals are not in God’s image and have no soul, but dominion must be responsible.
  • Catechism 2417 — food and clothing are legitimate; experiment is moral "within reasonable limits" where it saves human lives.
  • Church of England — accepts necessary research but requires careful and sympathetic treatment in life and in dying.
  • The Messianic vision — Isaiah 11:6–8 looks to peace between humans and animals; no sparrow falls unnoticed.
  • Most Christians are broadly utilitarian — against cosmetic testing, for essential vivisection, and warned against sentimentality.

More detail

Christians hold that animals are different from human beings — not made in God's image and without a soul — but that dominion must be exercised responsibly and with compassion.

The reasoning, in order: animals were not created in God's image and do not have a soul, so they cannot have the complex spiritual relationship with God that human beings can; God told Adam and Eve that animals were given to humanity to rule and control (Genesis 1:28); but the Bible also makes humanity the steward and caretaker of creation, the Messianic vision of scripture looks forward to a world in which humans and animals live in harmony, and Jesus taught that God's concern extends to all his creation — even the death of a single sparrow does not go unnoticed by God.

In practice, most Christians support the use of animals for food, taking a broadly utilitarian line — the right action is the one that brings the greatest happiness to the greatest number. Many oppose animal testing for cosmetics while accepting vivisection for essential medical research, provided it is carried out humanely; the Roman Catholic Church explicitly warns its members not to be sentimental about animals or grant them too much importance, because human beings are of greater value and while people suffer every effort should go on relieving that. A minority of Christians oppose all research on animals and all eating of animals, on the ground that animals are as intrinsically valuable as humans because God created them.

More detail

Christians teach that human nature is determined by God: made in his image, composed of body and soul, and fallen.

The Christian picture, straight from Genesis: Genesis 1 — made in God's image, with God-like qualities such as awareness, morality, relationality, rationality, spirituality and creativity; Genesis 2 — composed of body and soul; Genesis 3 — susceptible to temptation, and so we sin. St Paul calls the body the "temple of the Holy Spirit" (1 Corinthians 3:16).

The two named thinkers:

  • St Augustine of Hippo (354–430) — humans are created of body and soul; both are necessary to human nature and both were made by God. Influenced by Plato, he saw the soul as the rational and spiritual component and said it should govern the body, which is driven by instinct and physical urges; he used the analogy of a rider and a horse, the soul steering and the body carrying. Human nature is good, and freewill places humanity higher than animals and plants on the ladder of creation — but that gift was tainted by the Fall, so we are no longer equally free to choose between good and evil and are constantly drawn to excessive satisfaction of our lower desires (concupiscence). His Confessions gives the evidence from his own childhood: "Many times I lied to my tutor, my master, and my parents, and deceived them because I wanted to play games or watch some futile show… I even stole from my parents' larder… Can this be the innocence of childhood?" (Confessions, Book I Chapter 9).
  • St Thomas Aquinas (c.1225–1274) — more positive. Because humans are created in God's image they are essentially good, with a natural ability to distinguish right from wrong and a natural desire to do good instead of evil. But the Fall damaged humanity's ability to reason correctly, so wrongful acts happen because a person has mistaken an apparent good for a real good: "Whatever man desires, he desires it under the aspect of good" (Summa Theologica).

Non-religious accounts: a Darwinian view makes us the product of natural selection interacting with the environment, with no goal beyond survival of the species. Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980), an existentialist, denies a fixed human essence at all — "existence precedes essence" — so we are radically free and can choose anything except not to choose. His three quotable lines: "Man is nothing else but what he makes of himself"; "There is no human nature, since there is no god to conceive it"; "Man is condemned to be free, because once thrown into the world, he is responsible for everything he does." Sigmund Freud divides the mind into the ego (the rational self, balancing the other two), the id (the most basic needs — hunger, self-preservation — amoral and merely seeking to express itself) and the super-ego (the controlling, restraining self, formed in infancy from parents' morals and acting as censorship; it can be too severe, demanding perfectionism and producing guilt and acute self-criticism), so we live in constant internal conflict.

One named thinker the examiner expects on the other side of the body is Plato (c.428–c.348 BC), a dualist. The body is in constant change and belongs to the sensible world — the realm of becoming — which is fluid and so cannot give true or permanent knowledge. The soul is a simple entity that cannot be broken into parts or decay, so it is immortal and has access to the stable realm of being; it is through the soul that a person reaches truth, beauty and knowledge. Plato regarded the body as imprisoning the soul and compared them to a charioteer and a chariot: the charioteer (soul) does all the thinking and controlling, the chariot (body) just follows instructions, and at death the soul is released from its prison. Augustine's rider-and-horse analogy is a Christian adaptation of this — but Christians value the body far more than Plato did, because God created it.

Christians themselves divide over what survives death. Some, influenced by Greek thought, treat the human being as a psychosomatic unity with an immortal soul superior to the body. Others reject that as unbiblical — there are no clear references to a soul and little mention of an afterlife in the Old Testament — and as undermining the power and sovereignty of God and humanity's dependence on him: just as human beings depended on God for life at creation, they depend on him to bring them back for the day of judgement, and cannot assume they will survive death because of some permanent component of their own being. These Christians hold to the resurrection of the body and soul: Jesus returned from the dead with a physical body that showed the scars of his death, could be touched, and could speak and eat food — which is why "doubting" Thomas pokes the wounds, to check he is not a ghost.

Physicalism is the claim that the real world is nothing more than the physical world. Thought, personality, feelings and spirituality are inextricably linked to our physicality: when brains deteriorate, reasoning and self-control wane. There is no soul and no afterlife. Christians and some non-religious people reject it by pointing to God, heaven and souls, to near-death experiences, to self-awareness and identity, to the difficulty of getting consciousness out of brain chemistry alone, and to the problem that pure matter could not be free.

More detail

Christians treat selfishness, greed, ignorance and sin as the roots of human wrongdoing, and trace them back to the Fall.

Selfishness is behaviour performed out of self-interest, based on individual need, without considering the needs of other people. Christians teach it is wrong because it displaces God with one's own interests — it is not possible to pursue your own self-interest and serve God at the same time — and because the story of creation makes all people equal in worth, so no individual's need should come at the expense of others; they are taught stewardship and agape instead. The Golden Rule — "In everything do to others as you would have them do to you; for this is the law and the prophets" (Matthew 7:12) — the parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37, Jesus's answer to "who is my neighbour?") and the parable of the Sheep and the Goats (Matthew 25:31–46 — feed the hungry, clothe the poor, help the sick, visit prisoners) all put others first.

Non-religious views: Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) made survival the most basic aspect of human nature and accepted that we will use other people to achieve it, though he thought people soon realise cooperation serves their own interest and so enter a social contract that curbs their behaviour and everyone else's; J. S. Mill (1806–1873) argued we should act only in ways that do not harm others — "The only freedom which deserves the name, is that of pursuing our own good in our own way, so long as we do not attempt to deprive others of theirs, or impede their efforts to obtain it" (On Liberty); and evolution's survival of the fittest suggests we are predisposed to selfishness, because the organisms best able to seize the available resources pass on their genes.

Greed is an excessive desire for things such as wealth and food which bears no relation to one's actual needs. It is related to selfishness but is specifically about the attainment and use of resources. The statistics to quote come from a United Nations study of global household assets in 2006: the world's richest 1% own 40% of the world's wealth and the richest 10% own 85%, while half the world's population together own just 1%. Those in financial services and the internet sector predominate among the super-rich.

Roman Catholics teach greed is one of the seven deadly sins. Today many Christians donate to charity and work for fairer distribution of wealth, for example through Christian Aid. The counter-case: capitalists argue greed drives competition and efficiency, and funds research — pharmaceutical development, new technology — while many ethicists would say it simply needs regulating for the greater good.

Ignorance here means moral ignorance — not knowing the difference between right and wrong, or not knowing what makes an action wrong. The most likely cause is poor socialisation: the person was never taught right from wrong; a few people lack the capacity to tell the difference at all. Non-religious people treat it as harmful to society and put the duty on parents, schools and lawmakers to make sure everyone knows what makes an action wrong, because a society that knows is more likely to be peaceful, just and harmonious. Christians think most people have the capacity: Aquinas argued God gave humanity the gift of rationality, which is the basis of the conscience and can work out right from wrong by thinking logically, and since we are created in God's image we are naturally inclined to do good — so it is every Christian's duty to avoid moral ignorance. Ignorance is generally not an excuse, although Jesus says on the cross "forgive them Father, for they do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34). Aquinas also conceded that people make honest mistakes, taking an apparent good for a real good.

Sin is any action that goes against the law of God, such as the Ten Commandments. The Greek word for sin, hamartia, means to "miss the mark": God expected humanity to obey him in order to be in a relationship with him, and by turning from God and becoming self-centred humanity has failed to meet his goals. In the Sermon on the Mount Jesus says "be perfect as the heavenly father is perfect" and goes further than the Commandments, condemning sinful thoughts as well as sinful actions: "You have heard that it was said to those of ancient times, 'You shall not murder'… But I say to you that if you are angry with a brother or sister, you will be liable to judgment" (Matthew 5:21–22); he also commends the pure in heart, who "will see God" (Matthew 5:8).

A fundamentalist approach treats the Bible as the inerrant Word of God, applicable to all ages, so everything it calls a sin must be avoided; a liberal or conservative approach accepts that some teachings reflect the values of another age and the struggles of human authors, so these Christians use Christian tradition and their own conscience alongside Scripture. Original Sin is the inherited consequence of the Fall from God's grace (his undeserved love): for some Christians a spiritual inheritance each person is born with, for others a description of the universal human condition — however good our intentions, we are easily influenced into doing wrong. Forgiveness is available: "If we confess our sins, he who is faithful and just will forgive us our sins and cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:8–10) — and because everyone fails, Christians are warned to be slow to judge: "Why do you see the speck in your neighbour's eye, but do not notice the log in your own eye?" (Matthew 7:1–3). Non-religious people often dislike the word "sin" because it implies an objective, religious right and wrong, and would focus instead on consequences or on practical ways of improving society.

Human responsibility for hatred, injustice, violence and war. Greed leads to people wanting more than their fair share, producing social injustice — the desire for cheap consumer goods often results in child exploitation or slave labour in less economically developed countries — and war: the first Gulf War in 1991 was a response to Iraq's invasion and annexation of Kuwait over oil pricing and production disputes. Selfishness produces resentment, violence, discrimination, anti-social behaviour and tax-dodging; moral ignorance is a contributing factor in crime, because children who do not learn right from wrong are more likely to act irresponsibly later; sin produces social ills, because ignoring commandments such as do not kill, do not steal and do not commit adultery leads to hatred, injustice, violence and war.

But not all suffering has these causes: the forces of nature — droughts, famines, earthquakes and diseases — are often beyond human control; addiction to alcohol, drugs or gambling causes its own harm; some wars are started for ideological reasons, such as the Crusades (a clash of religious beliefs) and the Second World War (fuelled by Hitler's notion of an ideal race); and some occur to defend the weak or, as a last resort, to overcome social injustice — the basis of Just War theory.

More detail

Freewill is the ability to make genuine choices and so to be held responsible for them; determinism says our choices are fixed by prior causes.

Non-religious arguments that freewill is an illusion: science sees the world as operating by cause and effect, so every decision has prior causes; genetic determinism says personality and choices are set by our genes (if genes fix eye colour, body shape and appetite, why not character?); psychological determinism says character is determined by upbringing, because children learn by imitation and are impressionable; physical determinism treats the brain as a biological computer whose neurological and chemical processes determine thought and action. Hard determinism says all behaviour is externally determined; soft determinism allows that we can sometimes act against character.

The replies: genes and environment influence without forcing; family members with shared genes differ dramatically; consciousness is complex and not fully understood; and it is still us making the decision, so as long as we are not forced, we can count ourselves free. Libertarians hold that although character is shaped by influences, the moral self is free and we can choose between self-interest and duty. Sartre said being free is fundamental to being human.

The Christian position, and the tension inside it:

Jesus taught that humanity can choose to follow God's commandments, and the Christian belief in a day of judgement at the Eschaton implies we are accountable. The Roman Catholic Church argues that God has made us in his own image and able to control our own actions. The whole Christian narrative — faith in Jesus, or the choice to do good works, in return for salvation — depends on genuine choice.

Against that, Augustine argued that we have freewill but it is so distorted after the Fall that humans only choose evil; and John Calvin (1509–1564) argued for predestination: the Fall left humanity inherently sinful, so no one is in a position to earn a place in heaven and salvation is a completely unmerited gift, given by a God entirely free to give it to whomever he chooses. The elect were chosen before birth and have the Holy Spirit active in their lives, enabling them to do good deeds; the reprobate, also chosen before birth, do not receive the Spirit, so they yield to their sinful nature — which means your behaviour through life is a good indication of which group you are in.

The context is worth a mark, because it explains why a Protestant reformer said something so bleak. Calvin was writing against the Roman Catholic Church of his day, which appeared to control who was admitted to heaven: Christians were taught they needed the sacraments, administered by priests, so excommunication and the denial of the sacraments effectively damned a person, and the selling of indulgences to fast-track the saved through purgatory implied the Church held the keys. Calvin wanted to insist it was entirely up to God. Many Christians nevertheless reject predestination because it does not reflect an omnibenevolent God who loves all people, it makes moral law pointless, and it destroys accountability and therefore judgement day.

The problem of foreknowledge: if God is omniscient he knew before Genesis exactly what we would choose, so we were never free. The three standard solutions:

  1. God knows what we will do but does not force or compel us to do it — he may know all possible outcomes, or the end result but not the route.
  2. God has the capacity for omniscience but limits it, turning off his foresight to allow genuine freedom.
  3. God is outside time, so he does not see it "before" it happens; all things are present to him, and he simply knows what we are freely choosing.

More detail

The determinist case is not just philosophy — the course expects named studies, and naming one is the quickest way out of a vague 12-marker.

  • The DARPP-32 gene (2009). A study of a mutation in the gene DARPP-32, which affects dopamine levels, suggested it could be responsible for people having a quick temper. Genes that regulate feelings such as hunger may likewise be responsible for higher rates of obesity. Evidence for genetic determinism.
  • Thomas Bouchard's twin studies (1970s–1980s). Identical twins, whether raised together or apart, tended to have a similar IQ, favour the same school subjects and share political leanings. Evidence that nature, not choice, sets character.
  • Dr Gerd Kempermann. Against that: identical twins are usually very similar but differences do occur, because genes can be switched on or off by life experiences. At the very least this shows nurture also influences our choices — so genes influence without fixing.
  • Benjamin Libet's experiment (1980s). A volunteer was asked to move a finger whenever they liked and to note the moment of choosing. Libet found the choice to move was registered after the action had already been initiated by the brain, which has been used to argue freewill is an illusion. The standard reply: a simplistic test in which the subject already knows a choice is coming does not replicate real daily decision-making, and knowing a choice is due may trigger the subconscious process that produced it.

Soft determinism accepts that behaviour is caused, but holds that if a person is acting according to their own character and is not compelled, they still have a real semblance of freedom — which is why soft determinism is compatible with freewill, and why it is usually the safest position to defend in a conclusion. Note the general reply that runs through all of this: there is a difference between influence and cause. A propensity to act a certain way does not make it inevitable, and there are many examples of people achieving great things despite a poor start in life.

The belief took shape because the early Christians held two things at once: that there is one sovereign God, and that this God was uniquely present in Jesus the Christ (the Messiah, God's anointed), whose life on earth was begun and sustained by the Holy Spirit.

Non-religious attitudes to ultimate reality: some treat "God" as a human invention. Julian Baggini (b.1968) argues that those who believe in an ultimate reality are either looking backwards for a cause of existence or forwards to a goal of it, when meaning in fact comes from living in the present and enjoying the things that make life worth living now — a meaning we have to create for ourselves, with no lasting impact on the universe. Physicalists reject it because the real world is nothing more than the physical world, with no higher metaphysical realm at all. Richard Dawkins (b.1941), in The Magic of Reality (2011), explains natural phenomena scientifically to show how the elementary components of the planet work together logically and beautifully: "The truth is more magical — in the best and most exciting sense of the word — than any myth or made-up mystery or miracle. Science has its own magic: the magic of reality." Yet some non-religious people still accept ultimate values such as the pursuit of truth, beauty and justice, which may transcend time and be relevant to all ages, or point to the laws of nature as giving order and unity to the cosmos.

Two more facts worth dropping in: by some estimates there are more than 4,000 religions in the world, and the ancient Egyptians, Greeks, Romans and Norse peoples were polytheistic, as are many spiritual traditions native to Africa, Polynesia, central Asia and the indigenous Americas, which focus worship on the divine forces in nature. Polytheistic cultures typically also believe in demonic and ghostly forces, and it is these malevolent beings — not the gods — who are held responsible for the ills of the world, which gives polytheism its own answer to the problem of evil.

More detail

Reasons for believing in God divide into arguments from reason and arguments from revelation.

First, the vocabulary, because it earns credit: deductive arguments use a logical chain of reasoning; inductive arguments rest on premises likely to be true; a priori arguments work from logic alone; a posteriori arguments work from sensory or empirical evidence.

  • The cosmological or first cause argument (Aquinas; a posteriori). Classically expressed in the Summa Theologica as one of Aquinas's Five Ways. Everything that exists, including the universe, must have a prior cause. We cannot follow the chain of causation back forever — that is an infinite regress — so there must be a first cause, rather like someone pushing over the first domino in a line. That first cause must have been different, because it was itself uncaused, and it can only be God. It survives modern cosmology easily: if the universe began in the rapid expansion of a dense nucleus of matter, God could be the cause of that event.
  • The teleological or design argument (William Paley, 1743–1805; a posteriori). The world is complex and appears designed, so it cannot be the product of chance. Paley contrasted finding a stone on a path, which natural processes explain, with finding a watch on the same path: an intricate and complex mechanism must have been made, so it has a designer and a purpose. The universe is like the watch — the human eye is composed of minute parts fitting together in a complex and exact way, and all of them work together for the common purpose of sight — so the universe has been designed, for a purpose, by God. A modern extension is the anthropic principle: the chance of the planet evolving conditions fit for human survival is minute, so the production and survival of humanity looks like a purpose, which Christians use to argue God designed the universe to allow human life.
  • The ontological argument (Anselm; a priori). God is the greatest conceivable being; the greatest conceivable being would exist in reality as well as in the mind; therefore God exists. Descartes, Norman Malcolm and Alvin Plantinga offered later versions.
  • The search for meaning and purpose. If life has meaning and purpose, and it could not without God, then God must exist.
  • The appeal to religious authority — the Bible, the Church and its teaching.
  • Christian nurture and formation. Children take on the religion of their parents; festivals, rites of passage, worship and Christian values stick for life; church teaching, education, parental influence and the religious community all reinforce it, as does cultural pressure to conform.

More detail

The argument from religious experience says that God is known through revelation, not only through reason.

Many people believe because they have felt God's presence — by hearing him, seeing him or feeling his support in everything they do. Such experiences range from visions to healing to an overwhelming sense of love, and may be vividly described or ineffable (beyond description). Rudolf Otto (1869–1937) called them numinous — the sense of wonder and awe we feel in the presence of something greater than ourselves. William James (1842–1910) regarded religious experience as the foundation of religious belief, and Martin Buber (1878–1965) argued that because God is personal he is known only through personal encounters. The three types: direct experience of God (Moses and the burning bush, Exodus 3), conversion (St Paul on the road to Damascus, Acts 9; Augustine; C. S. Lewis; Bishop Hugh Montefiore), and miracles.

A miracle is an inexplicable event in which God dramatically intervenes: a physical or mental healing, a special sign that changes the course of a life, or an act that saves someone from a specific danger. Jesus's ministry was characterised by miracles that overcame the forces of nature, expelled demons, healed, or brought people back from the dead; the incarnation — Jesus being both truly human and truly divine — and the resurrection, Christ overcoming the powers of death, are themselves miracles. Christians believe miracles still happen, and Roman Catholics point to Lourdes, a town in the south of France at the foot of the Pyrenees: in 1858 Bernadette Soubirous had a number of visions of the Virgin Mary in the Grotto of Massabielle and was told to dig nearby, where she found a spring. Pilgrims who bathe in or drink the water claim to have been cured of their ailments.

Why some Christians are cautious about miracles: they can look like God acting arbitrarily, favouring some and not others; many claims have natural or psychological explanations; and some Christians hold that God works through the ordinary laws of the universe rather than breaking them. Atheists explain them as coincidence, misreporting or the placebo effect.

Atheism literally means "without or no God": an atheist believes there is no God or gods. Most atheists regard themselves as realists, facing the world as it really is and giving a naturalistic account of the existence and functioning of the universe. The reasons: there is no reliable empirical evidence for God, and one should only believe what there is good evidence for; everything in the universe can be explained satisfactorily by scientific observation, research and experiment, so God is unnecessary and there need be no great design or ultimate purpose; advances in science and medicine, and human ability to control the environment, remove the need for God; the problem of evil suggests an imperfect world full of design flaws, and God's apparent non-intervention supports the conclusion that he is not there; religious truth claims conflict between religions and within them, so believers cannot say what God is like or what he expects, and that conflict is dangerous — as in Israel/Palestine and Northern Ireland, and in atrocities committed in God's name. As atheists put it, God was once the explanation for what we did not understand, and now it is God who needs explaining.

Two named explanations of why people believe: Ludwig Feuerbach (1804–1872) argued that happiness is our primary desire and only seems secure if God exists to reward the virtuous and punish the wicked, so humanity imagined a God and gave him every quality we admire — intelligence, goodness, love. His conclusion: what man is in need of, he makes his God, so God is no more than a projection of man. Sigmund Freud (1856–1939) argued religion is a mental defence against the threatening aspects of nature: rather than face the terrors of life people believe in a protecting power, which is really a magnification and projection of the child's earliest memory of a parent — so belief in God is a symptom of neurosis, curable when one learns to face the world without illusions. A non-religious upbringing is itself a factor.

Agnosticism is not knowing whether God exists. Agnostics are unconvinced by theistic arguments but equally unconvinced by Freud or Feuerbach, and do not think evil conclusively disproves God. They may feel there is no positive empirical evidence either way, that the philosophical arguments are evenly balanced (first cause against the problem of evil), that it is not irrational to think some unknown cause lies behind the universe, and that both believers and atheists are guilty of intellectual arrogance in claiming a certainty nobody has.

More detail

John Hick defined evil as "physical pain, mental suffering and moral wickedness"; for Christians it is whatever is opposed to God and his purposes.

That definition is worth learning word for word, because it gives you a named scholar in the first line of a 6-marker. Evil is what causes suffering and harm to God's creation.

  • Moral evil — suffering caused by moral agents, deliberately or through negligence: bullying, lying, cheating, murder, rape.
  • Natural evil — suffering caused by events that have nothing to do with people: the natural order of the world producing volcanic eruptions, earthquakes, droughts, floods, disease and death.

The line between them is not as clean as it looks, and saying so earns credit: given human interference in the natural world, many recent natural disasters actually stem from human decisions, so the suffering they cause may really be a form of moral evil — floods and mudslides caused by widespread deforestation are the standard example.

The logical problem of evil, posed by Hume and J. L. Mackie as an inconsistent triad: if God is omnipotent and omniscient he knows evil exists and can stop it; if he is omnibenevolent he will want to stop it; yet evil exists. So either God is not good, or not all-powerful — and either answer destroys the Christian understanding of God. Set out as an argument: if God existed there would be no evil; evil exists; therefore God cannot exist.

Two further forms are worth naming. The evidential problem relies on the amount and often gratuitous nature of evil, which makes an omnipotent, omniscient and wholly good God improbable — the Jewish Holocaust during the Second World War, the Killing Fields of Cambodia and the Soviet Gulag are the standard illustrations. The experiential problem arises when a person meets evil first-hand and suffers, or watches a loved one or innocent children suffer; it is compounded if they pray for help and no response seems to come.

Atheists and humanists cannot believe a good, omnipotent God would design a world containing earthquakes and disease; they find it easier to attribute bad things to evolution or to human beings misusing their freedom. The spec names two specific Christian readings of suffering, and they pull in opposite directions: suffering as what sin deserves, and suffering as the test that proves faith.

Suffering as punishment for sin. The line runs straight out of Genesis 3: Adam and Eve freely disobey, fall from God's grace, and the consequences — pain in childbearing, toil, thorns and thistles, and return to the dust (Genesis 3:16–19) — are imposed as punishment. For some Christians Original Sin is a spiritual inheritance, so all humanity bears the consequence; for others the story describes the universal human condition. The Augustinian theodicy generalises it: natural evil exists because human disobedience ruined the perfection God created, so the order of nature was lost, bringing natural disasters, disease and death. The practical implication is the one the Psalmist draws — the answer to suffering is to obey God, who has given us the Ten Commandments and has even become incarnate to show by example the life he expects (Psalm 119:1–10).

Suffering as proof of faith. The Book of Job gives the alternative. Satan appears there as God's servant, whose role is to test whether Job fears God "for nothing" (Job 1:9–12); God permits the test, Job's faith holds, and when Job demands an explanation God answers only "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?". On this reading suffering is neither deserved nor meaningless but a trial through which faith is proved — which connects directly to the Irenaean theodicy, where evil develops charity and compassion and grows us into the likeness of God, and to Romans 8:28: "in everything God works for good with those who love him."

The 12-mark frame

Paragraph 1 — restate the statement and say what turns on it. One or two sentences: what the claim actually asserts, and which Christian belief it threatens. "The claim that [science has disproved creation / we have no freewill / humans are superior to animals] matters because it bears directly on [the authority of Genesis / accountability at judgement / the imago Dei]."

Paragraph 2 — the strongest case FOR, with teaching and scripture. One named argument, its evidence, then your appraisal of it. "A fundamentalist Christian would argue…" · "Calvin's doctrine of predestination supports this, because…" · "This argument is strong because it rests on Genesis 1:28 itself, but it is weakened by…"

Paragraph 3 — the strongest case AGAINST, with teaching and scripture. Same shape, opposite side. "However, a liberal Christian would respond…" · "Polkinghorne replies that Genesis answers why rather than how…" · "Russell's objection is more damaging still, because…"

Paragraph 4 — where Christians disagree with each other. This is the paragraph that separates level 4 from level 3, and in this module it is always available. "Christians do not speak with one voice here: fundamentalists read Genesis literally, whereas conservative and liberal Christians…" · "The Roman Catholic Catechism (2417) permits what a minority of Christians reject outright…" · "A Christian who follows Natural Law would argue…, but a situation ethicist would respond…"

Paragraph 5 — a conclusion that decides. Weigh the arguments against each other, then state your own view and why. "On balance, the argument from… is the stronger, because…" · "Although the evidential problem of evil remains unanswered, 'proves' is too strong a word, and I therefore disagree with the statement." Never finish with "both sides have good points".

The marks are in the appraisal, not the list. Each argument needs a named source — Aquinas, Paley, Augustine, Irenaeus, Plantinga, Hick, Catechism 2417, the Nicene Creed — and a sentence saying how good that argument actually is.

03Understanding it

These are the "why" questions behind the content. If you can answer them in your own words you can answer any 6- or 12-mark question on this section.

U1

Why do many Christians accept the Big Bang and evolution, when the Bible gives a different account?

Show answer

Because they do not read Genesis as a scientific report. Conservative and liberal Christians treat it as broadly true or as metaphorical — an answer to why there is something rather than nothing, not how it happened. On that reading a "day" could be billions of years, and the Big Bang and natural selection become the means God used.

They can also point to authority: the Catholic Church accepts the Big Bang, which was first proposed by the priest Georges Lemaître, and the Church of England encouraged members to see God's design achieved through the laws of the universe and the process of evolution.

The exam line: science asks how, religion asks why. Fundamentalist Christians reject the compromise and read Genesis literally, explaining fossils by Noah's flood or the Apparent Age theory.

U2

Why do Christians believe they have a responsibility for the planet, and what is the strongest objection to saying that duty is specially theirs?

Show answer

Why: God created the planet and "saw that it was good", so it has intrinsic value; Genesis 1:28 is read as stewardship, a duty to safeguard creation for future generations; Revelation warns of judgement for those who "destroy the earth"; and the Catholic Church calls the planet a gift to be "shared and developed, not to dominate and exploit".

The objection: non-religious people reach exactly the same duty without scripture — obligation to future generations, justice for the poorest, and the practical need to keep the earth habitable. If the conclusion is identical, the religious reasons add motivation rather than obligation.

A second objection comes from inside Christianity: Genesis 1:28 also says "dominion" and "subdue", which some scholars argue has licensed exploitation, and some Christians prioritise the next life over this one.

U3

What is the strongest objection to the claim that human beings have a soul?

Show answer

The interaction problem: if the soul is non-material, it is very hard to explain how it could act on physical matter — how a non-physical thing moves a hand or receives a thought.

Supporting objections: there is no scientific evidence of a soul; the Old Testament makes no clear reference to "souls" and says little about life after death; and the usual evidence — near-death experiences, spiritualism, remembered lives — has naturalistic explanations and cannot be replicated.

The Christian reply: Genesis 2:7 and Genesis 1:27, Ecclesiastes 12:7 and Matthew 10:28, and the Catechism's definition of the soul as "the spiritual principle in man". Some Christians avoid the problem altogether by teaching bodily resurrection rather than an immortal soul.

U4

Why do Christians think people do wrong, when God made them good?

Show answer

Because of the Fall. Genesis 3 describes Adam and Eve freely disobeying God, and that fall from grace produces Original Sin, which affects all humanity: we are born inclined to sin, and the image of God in us is distorted. Paul puts it exactly — "the evil I do not want to do, this I keep on doing" (Romans 7:18–19).

Aquinas gives a gentler version: humans naturally desire the good, but the Fall damaged our reasoning, so we mistake an apparent good for a real good. Augustine's version is harsher — concupiscence, the body pulling against the soul.

The named root causes to list are selfishness, greed, ignorance and sin.

U5

If God is omniscient, how can we be free? Give the Christian answers.

Show answer

The problem: if God knew before Genesis exactly what you would choose, you were never really free — you were always going to do it.

  1. Knowledge is not compulsion. God knows what we will do but does not force us; he may know all possible outcomes, or the end result but not the route we take.
  2. Self-limitation. God has the capacity for omniscience but limits it, so that freedom is genuine.
  3. Timelessness. God is outside time, so he does not know it "before" it happens; all things are present to him, and he knows what we are freely choosing now.

Why it matters: without freewill there is no accountability, no need for the incarnation and crucifixion, and judgement day becomes unjust — which is why most Christians defend freedom even at the cost of a weaker account of foreknowledge.

U6

Why do Christians believe in one God, and why is the Trinity not a contradiction of that?

Show answer

One God: God revealed himself that way. In the Ten Commandments he declares that he alone is God and alone is worthy of worship (Exodus 20:2–3), and Jesus endorsed it in telling his followers to love the Lord God with all their heart, mind and soul. Philosophically, the order and unified laws of the world suggest a single designer, and a perfect being cannot have a rival.

The Trinity: God is one substance, three persons. The three work as one — at Jesus's baptism the Father speaks, the Son is baptised and the Spirit descends as a dove (Matthew 3:13–17). The Nicene Creed states the belief formally.

The objection worth knowing: critics ask whether a religion with a Trinity is genuinely monotheistic at all. The answer is the distinction between substance and person.

U7

What is the strongest objection to Paley's design argument, and to Aquinas's first cause argument?

Show answer

Against design: natural selection explains apparent design without a designer — complexity is built up by a blind process over immense time. Evolution is also cruel and wasteful, which fits a loving designer badly. And the analogy is weak: we know watches are made, but we have no second universe to compare this one with.

Against first cause: the argument stops the chain of causation exactly where it suits itself. If everything must have a cause, so must God; if something can be uncaused, why not the universe? Science can explain the universe's origin and operation without invoking God, and the theory is simpler without him.

The reply: the first cause must be different in essence — uncaused — and a universe arising from nothing by chance is, Christians argue, less plausible than a creator.

U8

Why does the existence of evil challenge belief in God, and which Christian response is strongest?

Show answer

The challenge is the inconsistent triad: an omniscient God knows evil exists, an omnipotent God can remove it, an omnibenevolent God wants to. Evil exists. So God lacks one of those attributes — and any of those conclusions destroys the Christian God.

The responses, appraised: the Freewill Defence (Plantinga) is strong on moral evil — God created the possibility of evil, not evil itself — but says nothing about earthquakes or disease. The Augustinian theodicy covers that gap, tracing natural evil to the corruption of nature after the Fall, but struggles to explain why a good creation would disobey. The Irenaean theodicy makes evil purposeful — soul-making — but asks why God could not build souls through love instead, and reduces the significance of Jesus.

A judgement: the Irenaean theodicy handles both kinds of evil and fits the idea of a world that develops us, which makes it the most complete — though the sheer scale of evil in the evidential problem remains the hardest thing for any of them to answer.

Christian teaching to anchor it: animals are part of a creation God "saw was good", so exploiting them is against God's will and the Bible condemns cruelty to animals 1

Yet most Christians still accept that animals may be used for food, labour and some medical testing, provided they are treated humanely — which is a claim of superiority in practice 1

Conclusion: the biological case for commonality is strong, and the arguments from suffering show that superiority cannot mean animals may be treated as things 1

But the Christian teaching that humanity alone bears the image of God, receives revelation and is offered salvation gives human beings a distinct role and a distinct responsibility; I therefore agree that humans are superior in status, but only in the sense that stewardship makes them accountable for the creatures beneath them 1

"Suffering proves God does not exist."

Arguments for: if God is good and all-powerful he has both the means and the inclination to prevent suffering, yet suffering occurs, which suggests he does not exist 1

Hume and Mackie set this out as the inconsistent triad, and the conclusion follows: if God existed there would be no evil; evil exists; therefore God cannot exist 1

God is the creator, so natural evil — disease, earthquakes, death — traces back to him rather than to human freewill 1

The evidential problem adds weight: the scale and gratuitousness of the Holocaust or the Gulag go far beyond anything a loving purpose could require 1

Arguments against: a great deal of suffering is caused by the misuse of human freewill, so Plantinga's Freewill Defence shows that a good God can co-exist with evil because he created only the possibility of it 1

Augustine argued evil is not a thing God made but a privation — a corruption of good — originating in the free choice of angels and humans to turn from God in Genesis 3 1

Irenaeus argued God deliberately made the world imperfect so that suffering could develop charity and compassion and build us into the likeness of God 1

Some Christians answer with mystery: in Job, God rebukes human demands for an explanation with "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?", and the answer may only come in heaven 1

Christian teaching to anchor it: suffering is not meaningless in Christianity, because Jesus himself suffered, and Christians respond practically with prayer, forgiveness and charity rather than treating suffering as a disproof 1

Appraisal: the Freewill Defence is strong on moral evil but silent on natural evil; the Augustinian theodicy covers natural evil but must explain why a good creation disobeyed; the Irenaean theodicy covers both but makes God the author of suffering 1

Conclusion: suffering is the most serious challenge to belief in God, and the evidential version of the problem is not fully answered by any theodicy 1

But "proves" is too strong: the theodicies show the existence of God and the existence of evil are not logically contradictory, so suffering makes belief harder to hold rather than impossible, and I therefore disagree with the statement 1

Explain religious and non-religious views about the commonality of human beings and other animals. (6)

Many non-religious people stress commonality on the basis of evolutionary theory: humans evolved from a common ancestor with the higher primates and share a high percentage of their DNA 1, a similarity so close that it should guide how we treat other animals, which is why this group tends to favour animal rights 1

Peter Singer goes further, arguing that all animals including humans have the same moral worth because they all make decisions and all feel pain, so it is "speciesist" to think only humans are valuable 1

Christians who accept evolution are also open to commonality, valuing animals both because God created them and declared them good and because of that shared heritage; the concept of stewardship endorses it, and Albert Schweitzer said Christians should have a reverence for all life 1

But most Christians accept commonality only up to a point: humanity alone is created in the image of God, which marks a genuine point of difference, because human beings have a spiritual dimension and can be in a relationship with God 1

That relationship was solidified by the incarnation, when God revealed himself in human form, and the death and resurrection of Jesus point to a salvation offered to humanity alone — which is why some Christians emphasise human dominance over the rest of the animal kingdom 1

Arguments for: animals are part of a creation God made and declared good, so a minority of Christians argue they are as intrinsically valuable as humans and oppose both research on animals and eating them 1

Scripture extends the duty of care to animals: "A righteous man has regard for the life of his beast" (Proverbs 12:10), and Jesus taught that not even the death of a single sparrow goes unnoticed by God 1

The Messianic vision of Isaiah 11:6–8 looks forward to wolves and sheep living together in peace and a child safe with a poisonous snake — God's intended end for creation is harmony, not exploitation 1

Non-religiously, Peter Singer argues animals have the same moral worth because they make choices and feel pain, and evolution shows commonality — a shared ancestor and a high percentage of shared DNA — so the difference is one of degree, not kind 1

Arguments against: Christians hold that animals were not created in God's image and do not have a soul, so they cannot have the spiritual relationship with God that human beings can 1

Genesis 1:28 gives humanity the command to "fill the earth and subdue it" and to "rule over" every living creature, which most Christians read as a real difference in status 1

The Roman Catholic Church warns its members not to be sentimental about animals or grant them too much importance: human beings are of greater value, and while people suffer every effort should go on relieving that suffering 1

Non-religiously, humanity's intelligence, reasoning and self-awareness make it the dominant species, and animals act on instinct and exercise no moral responsibility, so they cannot hold rights in the way humans do 1

Christian teaching to anchor it: the Roman Catholic Catechism (2417) states that God entrusted animals to the stewardship of those made in his image, so it is legitimate to use them for food and clothing and to experiment on them "within reasonable limits" where this saves human lives 1

The Church of England takes the same balance from the other direction, recognising the need for some animal research while affirming that responsible stewardship requires all animals to receive "careful and sympathetic treatment, both during their lives and in the manner of their dying" 1

Conclusion: the arguments from suffering and from commonality are strong enough to rule out treating animals as mere things, and both Churches accept that cruelty is wrong 1

But "as valuable" is too strong for the Christian position: humanity alone bears the image of God, has a soul and is offered salvation, and both Churches permit the use of animals for food and essential medicine — so I disagree with the statement, while holding that stewardship makes humans accountable for how they use that greater status 1
