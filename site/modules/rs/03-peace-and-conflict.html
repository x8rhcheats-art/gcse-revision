More detail

A conflict occurs when there is a serious disagreement between two parties, and large-scale conflicts have five standard causes: politics, resources, history, culture and religion. The spec names those five, so learn them as a list and attach an example to each.

The spec also requires three current areas of conflict. Learn these three with one sentence each — a bare name is not a described example:

  2. Israel / Palestine. Both Israelis and Palestinians claim the same land. Since the annexation of the land in 1948 there have been several wars, most recently in Gaza in May 2021. Consequences are a favourite 3-mark question, and each one has a figure attached if you want the development mark. Death and injury, especially among civilians — in World War II more than 60 million people were killed, and 60% of the deaths were civilians. Mass migration — the Syrian conflict drove more than three million refugees into Turkey and Lebanon and on into Europe, and over 900,000 Rohingya have fled to Bangladesh, often into huge refugee camps. The cost — weapons and military personnel are expensive, and the disruption to a country's economy can be crippling. Damage to infrastructure — roads, hospitals and schools. Disrupted education. And the burden on neighbouring countries, providing shelter, health care and education for refugees.

Christian teachings on war — the Bible cuts both ways

Spec 3.1 · Revision Booklet 3 p.6–7

  • Most Christians oppose war — but a minority allow taking up arms against an oppressor as a last resort.
  • The Bible against war — "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9); Micah 4:3 beats swords into ploughshares.
  • The Bible for war — 1 Samuel 15:3 commands the destruction of the Amalekites; Joel 3:9 inverts Micah exactly.
  • Jesus' example cuts both ways — he died rather than fight, yet overturned the money changers' tables in righteous anger (Mark 11:15).
  • The individual-not-nation move — Jesus tells a person how to answer violence done to them, not a state what to do.
  • Three hundred years of pacifism — the early Church refused to fight at all; leaders condoned war once Rome turned Christian.

The Bible against war

"Blessed are the peacemakers." — Matthew 5:9. Used to argue that peacemaking, not fighting, is the Christian vocation.

"Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you." — Matthew 5:44. Used to argue that even an enemy is owed agape, which rules out killing them.

"If someone strikes you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also." — Matthew 5:39. Used to argue against retaliation of any kind.

"Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you; not as the world gives do I give to you." — John 14:27.

"Nation will not take up sword against nation, nor will they train for war any more." — Micah 4:3, foretelling the time when God's rule is established. Used to argue that God's ideal world is disarmed, so war can never be God's will. Isaiah calls the awaited Messiah the "Prince of Peace" (Isaiah 9:6–7).

Jesus' own example counts as evidence too: he was prepared to die rather than fight, and when arrested in the Garden of Gethsemane he did not resist and stopped his followers resisting. For the first three hundred years of the religion, Christians refused to fight in wars at all.

The Bible for war

"The LORD your God will drive out those nations before you… He will give their kings into your hand, and you will wipe out their names from under heaven." — Deuteronomy 7:22–24. Used to argue that God himself has commanded war.

"Proclaim this among the nations: Prepare for war! Rouse the warriors! Let all the fighting men draw near and attack. Beat your ploughshares into swords and your pruning hooks into spears. Let the weakling say, 'I am strong!'" — Joel 3:9–10. The prophets commanding the people to fight on God's behalf — and the exact inversion of Micah 4:3.

"Go now and fall on the Amalekites and destroy them, and put their property under ban. Spare no one: put them all to death, men and women, children and babes in arms, herds and flocks, camels and asses" — 1 Samuel 15:3.

"Greater love has no man than this that he lay down his life for his friends" — Jesus, in John. Used to argue that fighting to protect others is the highest form of love.

"Love your neighbour" — cuts both ways: to protect your neighbour you might need to fight for them.

More detail

Most Christians think war is wrong and conflicts should be resolved peacefully, but a minority argue that as a last resort Christians may take up arms against an oppressor. The split exists because the Bible itself is divided, and an answer that quotes only one side is a half answer.

Two extra details earn credit. Jesus was occasionally prepared to use violence: he "overturned the tables of the money changers and the benches of those selling doves" in the Temple (Mark 11:15–16) — his righteous anger. And there is a long history of Christian leaders supporting governments in using force: once Christianity became a legal and then the official religion of the Roman Empire, Christian leaders were far more willing to condone warfare.

The standard move for Christians who accept war is this: Jesus' teaching on peace tells an individual how to respond to violence done to them; it does not tell a nation what to do when someone else is being made to suffer.

"We utterly deny all outward wars and strife and fighting with outward weapons for any end or under any pretence whatsoever. And this is our testimony to the whole world."

— Declaration of the Society of Friends to Charles II (1661). The best evidence that pacifism is a settled Christian position and not a modern opinion.

— Mahatma Gandhi (1869–1948). Used to argue that passive resistance is not weakness but a different and stronger kind of power. Martin Luther King is the usual second example.

More detail

A pacifist believes war and violence can never be justified, because human life is so valuable that nothing can justify putting it at risk. Even fighting in self-defence is deemed wrong; there will always be another way to settle a conflict.

The word comes from the Latin pax (peace) and ficus (making). Pacifists also work to prevent war in the first place, by promoting justice and preserving religious and human rights — on the argument that denying people basic freedoms is what leads to conflict.

  • The word pacifism was coined by Emil Arnaud (1864–1921), a French peace campaigner, and adopted at the tenth Universal Peace Congress in Glasgow in 1901. The practice goes back as far as the Roman Empire — Seneca the Younger wrote against warfare.
  • A strong pacifist theme runs through Jesus' teachings, especially the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7), where he told his followers to turn the other cheek and love their enemies.
  • Pacifism also developed among Humanists: Erasmus argued strongly against war in In Praise of Folly (1509), and Rousseau, Kant and Bentham all spoke out against it, focusing on the waste of human resources. Leo Tolstoy (1828–1910) wrote The Kingdom of God is Within You, a history and defence of pacifism.
  • It was visible in the twentieth century in the conscientious objectors of both World Wars and in groups against nuclear weapons such as CND (the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament).

The named Christian example is the Quakers — the Religious Society of Friends — who take the absolutist position. They accept there is evil in the world but deny it can be overcome by weapons that harm and kill; instead Christians should use the weapons of the Spirit — truth, love and peace. In both World Wars Quakers were conscientious objectors: refusing conscription, many served as medics, stretcher-bearers or fire-watchers, and some were imprisoned and fined.

More detail

A just war is one fought for the right reasons and in the correct way; Just War theory defines when it is right to go to war and how that war should be fought. The spec asks for its history, so name the three authors — "some Christians say" scores nothing.

  • Augustine (354–430) laid the roots, at a time when Christianity was an official religion of a Roman Empire fighting wars across a huge territory. He set out two principles: the right to go to war and the correct conduct during war.
  • Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) developed and expanded Augustine's thought into four reasons that make it right to go to war and two principles governing conduct.
  • Francisco de Vitoria (c.1483–1546) added the principle about what should happen after a war: the defeated nation must be properly organised so its citizens can resume their daily lives in safety as quickly as possible.
  • Jus ad bellum — when it is right to go to war: a just cause; a legitimate authority; a right intention; as a last resort; with a strong chance of success.
  • Jus in bello — how the war is conducted: methods must be proportional; innocent people should not be attacked; prisoners treated fairly; weapons of mass destruction not used.
  • Jus post bellum — after the war: the losers treated humanely, and help given to get the people and the country back on their feet.

Holy war

Spec 3.1 · Lesson 4, Revision Booklet 3 p.7–8

  • Three characteristics — a religious goal, authorised by a religious leader or God, and promising a spiritual reward.
  • The Crusades — the First Crusade (1095–1099), called by Pope Urban II to free Jerusalem, promising a plenary indulgence.
  • Old Testament episodes — the "wars of Yahweh" (Numbers 21:14), Moses and the Amalekites (Exodus 17), Jericho (Joshua 6).

Texts used to justify holy war

More detail

A holy war is one in which religion is the driving force, and it has three defining characteristics. Learn all three — a 3-mark question on this is almost free marks.

  1. It must be fought to achieve a religious goal.
  2. It must be authorised by a religious leader or by God.
  3. It must promise a spiritual reward to those who fight in it.

The Christian example is the Crusades. The First Crusade (1095–1099) was called by Pope Urban II to free Jerusalem from Muslim control so Christian pilgrims could visit the city, including sites such as the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. Urban promised those who took part the reward of heaven, stating that anyone who died on Crusade would have their sins forgiven and go straight there — a plenary indulgence. They were known as milites Christi. All three characteristics are present, which is why this is the example to use.

The Old Testament supplies three more, and a named reference is worth more than a retelling:

  • The "wars of Yahweh" are mentioned in Numbers 21:14 — battles dedicated to God's plans for his people, in which God himself was involved.
  • Exodus 17:8–16 — the Amalekites attack the Israelites escaping Egypt. Moses holds the rod of God aloft on the hilltop, and while it is raised the Israelites have the upper hand; Aaron and Hur help him hold it up to secure victory, which is then dedicated to God.
  • Joshua 6 — the destruction of Jericho, the first target after crossing the Jordan into the promised land. The Israelites marched in silence around the city with the priests carrying the Ark of the Covenant, blew shofars, shouted at Joshua's signal so the walls collapsed, then killed all the inhabitants and dedicated the treasures to God.

Can war ever be holy? The Bible is ambiguous: those three Old Testament episodes against a New Testament with no tradition of holy war at all, whose tone is agape, forgiveness and compassion — which is why most early Christians were absolute pacifists. Holy war only began to be taught in the eleventh century, with the First Crusade, and by the end of the thirteenth century the idea had become less popular, as people came to think the Crusades were fought to gain territory and increase papal power.

Today most Christians reject it: God is love, Jesus rejects violence, and the Pope has apologised for the Crusades. Many would accept a war fought to defend people's rights, but regard a war fought for God as incompatible with a loving, compassionate and forgiving God. Some suggest the only acceptable holy war is one against injustice and evil.

Weapons of mass destruction

Spec 3.1 · Lesson 5, Revision Booklet 3 p.8–9

  • WMD defined — nuclear, chemical and biological weapons, able to inflict death and damage on a huge scale.
  • Nuclear facts — Hiroshima, 6 August 1945, killed 84,000; Nagasaki 40,000 more; nine states held about 15,700 warheads by 2015.
  • Banned by treaty — the Chemical Weapons Convention (1993), and the Biological Weapons Convention signed by 180 states.

Benefits and problems — the spec wants both:

Christian attitudes. The criteria for a just war or holy war make WMD very hard to justify: Christians are forbidden from killing innocents or vulnerable populations not involved in the war, and WMD deliberately target exactly those groups — the old, the young and women. Christians also appeal to the sanctity of life and to their role as stewards and custodians of God's creation — "the earth is the Lord's". Most therefore oppose their use and many favour working towards nuclear disarmament.

But some Christians think holding WMD as a deterrent while not necessarily using them is permissible, which is why many predominantly Christian countries hold nuclear arsenals.

Non-religious attitudes. Humanists would not support their use — human life is far too valuable — though some take a pragmatic line that in a world where nuclear weapons exist it is necessary to have them to deter an attack. Atheists are divided: some oppose them absolutely, and because they believe in no afterlife they think preserving this life and this planet matters even more; others argue WMD are a necessary means of self-defence, invoking Utilitarianism — an arsenal unlikely ever to be used extends the greatest happiness to the greatest number, because the fear of mutually assured destruction ironically keeps the peace. Situation Ethics may say "sometimes" — whichever course is most loving.

Testing WMD raises its own ethics. Nuclear tests have been atmospheric, underwater, exoatmospheric and underground; many countries signed the Comprehensive Nuclear Test Ban Treaty (1996), though India and Pakistan did not, and North Korea is the only country currently testing. Chemical and biological testing has used human subjects: Porton Down exposed volunteers to nerve agents including sarin in the 1950s; terminally ill patients were injected with Uranium 235 in the 1960s; a US navy ship sprayed microbes over San Francisco in 1950; anthrax spores were released into the Northern Line tunnels in 1963–64.

More detail

The United Nations defines terrorism as any act which aims to cause death or serious harm to civilians, with the purpose of intimidating a group of people or a population, or compelling a government to abstain from or perform specific actions. The shorter definition is the illegal use of violence to further a cause. Saying "the UN defines it as" is worth doing — it shows the definition is not your own guess.

  • Forms: suicide bombings, car bombs, gun and knife attacks, and using vehicles as weapons.
  • Aims: to use acts of atrocity to gain publicity, cause fear and intimidate authorities into yielding to demands.

The named case study is the IRA and the Brighton bombing. The Irish Republican Army was established in Northern Ireland to protect the Catholic community; towards the end of the 1960s a breakaway group, the Provisional IRA, decided physical force was necessary. It ran a bombing campaign in Northern Ireland, clashing with the British Army and the Royal Ulster Constabulary, aiming to cause so much damage that British forces would withdraw. The campaign crossed the Irish Sea: about a month before the 1984 Conservative Conference, Patrick Magee planted a bomb in The Grand Hotel, Brighton, timed for the last night of the conference with the explicit aim of assassinating Margaret Thatcher. She survived; five people were killed and at least 31 injured.

Christians have been on both sides. The Army of God, an American Christian terrorist organisation formed in 1982 and committed to reducing abortions in America, has committed kidnap, attempted murder and murder, and has been associated with abortion clinic bombings and arsons; some Christians supported the aims and actions of the IRA. Some members of civil rights groups fighting social injustice have also used violence — regarding themselves as freedom fighters while their opponents called them terrorists, as with Camilo Torres. But the majority of Christians think terrorism cannot be justified morally — trying to force people to change their mindset or behaviour through a campaign of terror does not comply with the biblical teachings of love, justice, peace and compassion.

Real examples, which the examiner rewards heavily.

  • Gee Walker forgave her son's murderers; as a Christian she felt it was Jesus' command and will.
  • Rev Julie Nicholson refused to forgive the terrorists who killed her daughter in the 7/7 bombings — the guilty party had not repented, so she saw no point. A perfect counter-example for a 12-marker.

At a global level, the Roman Catholic Church — based in the independent state of the Vatican City — is a member of the United Nations in a non-voting capacity, with a permanent representative who can address the assembly. The one Christian organisation working for peace: Corrymeela.   1. To be a sign that Protestants and Catholics can share in a common witness and ministry of reconciliation.
  2. To provide opportunities for meeting, dialogue and learning that dispel ignorance, prejudice and fear, and promote mutual respect, trust and co-operation.
  3. To support victims of violence and injustice, enable the healing of personal and social wounds, and promote new initiatives for social and political change.

Much of its work is in schools and youth groups, including workshops on conflict resolution and an international peace conference. Why? Because Jesus is the Prince of Peace, and because it puts the Bible's teaching on forgiveness into action. Davey said people are reminded as they leave Ballycastle that "Corrymeela begins when you leave" — the point being that the change has to happen back in their own community.

The United Nations was created in 1945, after the Second World War, with the central aim of maintaining international peace and security. It does this by working to prevent conflict, helping parties in conflict make peace, deploying peacekeepers, and creating the conditions for peace to hold and flourish — four points, so a 4-mark question on the UN's role is straightforward.

  • The Security Council has primary responsibility. It determines whether there is a threat to peace or an act of aggression, can call on parties to settle disputes peacefully, can help draw up the terms of a settlement, and can enforce measures including economic sanctions and military action.
  • The General Assembly plays a complementary role, giving member states a forum and making recommendations to the Security Council in the form of resolutions.
  • Ukraine, 2022 — a usable example. The UN mobilised agencies for humanitarian relief, particularly for those fleeing. On 2 March 2022 the General Assembly held a special session and approved a resolution deploring Russia's actions by 141 votes to 5, prompting Secretary-General António Guterres to say: "End hostilities in Ukraine now. Silence the guns now. Open the door to dialogue and diplomacy now."
  • Peacekeeping examples: policing the "green line" in Cyprus between the Turkish north and Greek south; and peacekeeping in South Sudan, monitoring human rights abuses, delivering aid and enforcing the law where necessary.

The consequences of sin

"But the cowardly, the unbelieving, the vile, the murderers, the sexually immoral, those who practice magic arts, the idolaters and all liars — they will be consigned to the fiery lake of burning sulphur. This is the second death."

— Revelation 21:8, describing what awaits sinners who have failed to repent. Used to argue that sin has eternal, not merely legal, consequences — the point of a 6-marker on "the consequences of sin". The phrase "the second death" is worth including.

More detail

A sin is an offence against God; a crime is an illegal act punishable by the law — so not all sins are crimes, but Christians would argue all crime has its source in sin. The Greek word for sin, hamartia, means to "miss the mark": God expected humanity to obey him in order to be in a relationship with him, and by turning away and becoming self-centred humanity has not fulfilled God's goals.

The Original Sin was committed by Adam and Eve, who gave in to the serpent's temptation and disobeyed God's command not to eat the fruit from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. They were banished from Eden and separated from God — the Fall, the Fall from God's grace (undeserved love). Christians believe all humanity is affected: for some it is a spiritual inheritance each person is born with; for others the story describes the universal human condition — however good our intentions, we are easily influenced into doing wrong.

Types of sin. Roman Catholics distinguish three.

  • Original Sin — the initial nature of people. All human beings begin life separated from God because of the actions of Adam and Eve, but can still be reconciled to him through the sacrament of baptism.
  • Mortal sin — grave immoral acts which imperil the soul; violations of the Ten Commandments are the examples, such as murder. All mortal sins result in the loss of the state of grace, and while outside it a Roman Catholic cannot fully participate in church activities.
  • Venial sin — minor infractions of God's commandments, such as lying. Less severe, but if ignored they weaken a person's inner moral strength and make greater sins more likely — they are said to rust the soul.

Protestants instead distinguish sins of commission — doing what one has been commanded not to do, such as violence — from sins of omission, failing to do what one should, such as not giving aid to those in need. Jesus said only blasphemy against the Holy Spirit cannot be forgiven.

Sins can be forgiven. Roman Catholics believe the effects of sin are erased through the sacrament of penance and reconciliation: by confessing to a priest, wholly desiring a return to God's grace and accepting an earthly punishment, the individual is reconciled to God.

Types of crime, with their usual seriousness: crimes against persons (GBH, violence, murder — life imprisonment); crimes against property (theft, arson — imprisonment); hate crimes (prejudice, racism); crimes against morality; white-collar crime (fraud and financial crime); and organised crime (people trafficking, county-lines drug gangs, prostitution rings — long sentences).

Why crime happens — the spec lists these, so a 4-marker wants two of them developed:

  • Poverty — need: many people convicted of shoplifting food were stealing to feed themselves or their family, and a lack of job opportunities or unrealised aspirations can lead to crime.
  • Socialisation — upbringing and family background: poor parenting or a troubled family life makes it harder to conform to society's expectations.
  • Prejudice — discrimination against particular social or racial groups reinforces negative perceptions, forcing people into isolation and possibly crime.
  • Low self-esteem — those who do not value themselves are more likely to have a low opinion of others too.
  • Drugs — a great deal of acquisitive crime is committed by heroin or crack cocaine users to pay for their drugs.
  • Politics, and, Christians would add, sin: because sin is part of human nature, all people have the potential to commit a crime.

Christian responses to crime — the spec names two organisations, so learn both:

  • Street Pastors. Pioneered in London in 2003 by Les Isaac. It sets up city projects in partnership with the local police and council, and trains volunteers to make the streets safer, offering reassurance, safety and support through caring, listening and helping — water for the drunk and distressed, a way home. It began in the night-time economy and now works in the daytime too, in parks, schools and colleges. It is ecumenical, with many different churches involved, and a group will spend the night praying for the town.
  • Prison Fellowship. Its mission is to show Christ's love to people in prison by visiting and supporting them, using prayer and practical help such as literacy teaching and someone to confide in. It has a network of over 3,000 volunteers working in almost all the prisons in England and Wales, and supports prisoners' families too. It works with people of all faiths or none, treating every person with respect and dignity, believing no one is beyond hope and every life can be transformed.

More broadly, Christians try to address the causes of crime: support groups for parenting and addiction, protest against social injustice, charities and food banks, and targeting the times and places where crime is most likely.

Punishment — its aims, and why Christians disagree about them

Spec 3.5 · Lesson 10, Revision Booklet 3 p.18–19

  • Punishment defined — the penalty inflicted for breaking the law; its main aim is to vindicate the law itself.
  • Five aims — protection, deterrence, retribution, reform and reparation. Learn the names and one line on each.
  • No official Christian opinion — neither Catholic nor Protestant churches have one, so all argue from biblical justice and forgiveness.
  • Most Christians prioritise reform — every offender is made in God's image, so rehabilitation matters more than payback.
  • Restorative justice — victim and offender meet, so the victim regains control and the offender sees the harm done.
  • A minority want harshness — reading the Old Testament retribution strand as avenging the crime and deterring reoffending.

Justice: the retributive strand

"Anyone who injures their neighbour is to be injured in the same manner: fracture for fracture, eye for eye, tooth for tooth. The one who has inflicted the injury must suffer the same injury."

— Leviticus 24:19–20. The classic retribution text.

"The one who sins is the one who will die. The child will not share the guilt of the parent, nor will the parent share the guilt of the child."

— Ezekiel 18:20, showing God's justice is fair and individual: nobody is punished for someone else's wrong. Also Genesis 3, where Adam and Eve are cast out of Eden in punishment for disobeying God.

Because God's justice is real, Christians believe they must monitor their own behaviour — the text the spec names for this is Galatians 6:1–5: "Brothers and sisters, if someone is caught in a sin, you who live by the Spirit should restore that person gently. But watch yourselves, or you also may be tempted. Carry each other's burdens, and in this way you will fulfil the law of Christ." Note that even this justice text says restore gently — which is why it is often used for reform rather than retribution.

Forgiveness: the reforming strand

"You have heard that it was said, 'Eye for eye, and tooth for tooth.' But I tell you, do not resist an evil person. If anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to them the other cheek also."

— Matthew 5:38–39. Jesus rewrites the Old Testament retribution law, quoting Leviticus 24 in order to overturn it.

He put it into practice in John 8:1–11, confronted by a crowd holding a woman charged with adultery, for which the sentence was death: he answered that whichever of them was without sin should cast the first stone, nobody would, and the woman was released with the instruction to sin no more.

Also: "Do not repay anyone evil for evil… Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good" (Romans 12:17ff), used to support reformation over retribution; the parable of the Prodigal Son, confirming God's willingness to forgive the truly penitent; and the parable of the unforgiving servant, warning that Christians must show mercy if they wish God to forgive them. Jesus taught that to be forgiven a person must repent — the word is metanoia, meaning a complete turnaround. It is an active word: the sinner has to do something that moves them away from the sin and towards a more God-like life.

Punishment is the penalty inflicted on a person for breaking the law, and it has five standard aims. Learn the five names and a one-line gloss for each; most of this spec area is built on them.

Types of punishment: imprisonment, a suspended sentence, community service, probation, a fine, a compensation order, a restitution order, a hospital order, and in some countries death. The main aim is to vindicate or justify the law, thereby ensuring everyone obeys it — the other five aims sit underneath that.

Context the examiner likes: the European Convention on Human Rights gives the accused the right to a fair trial — carried out publicly, in an unbiased court, with an opportunity to defend oneself — and in the UK a person is considered innocent until proven guilty. A judge and jury weigh the evidence, and the sentence varies with the severity of the crime.

There is no official Christian opinion on punishment, but the Bible supplies two general principles — justice and forgiveness — and they pull in opposite directions.

So Christian attitudes split. Note first that neither the Roman Catholic Church nor the Protestant denominations have an official opinion on the general issues surrounding punishment — all Christians look to the Bible for principles. Upholding the law matters to all of them: God is the source of justice, and Christians as God's stewards have a duty to keep society just, which gives a smooth-running society and boundaries that keep people in check. But care must be taken that punishment is fair and that mitigating circumstances are considered.

For most Christians the main aim should be to reform the criminal so they can be rehabilitated. Many therefore prefer restorative justice, in which the criminal and the victim meet so each gains insight into the other's feelings and behaviour: the victim may regain a sense of control, and the offender may understand the effects of their behaviour, which helps them reform. A minority, focusing on the Old Testament, think punishment should be harsh, avenging the crime and making the offender think twice about reoffending.

More detail

Capital punishment, or the death penalty, involves taking the life of a criminal; its aims are to protect society, to deter people from committing the same crime, and, as retribution, to compensate the victim and the victim's family. A crime punishable by death is a capital offence.

The UK dates are worth getting right, because a vague "it was banned a while ago" throws away an easy mark. The last executions, by hanging, took place in 1964. Capital punishment for murder was abolished in Great Britain in 1965 and in Northern Ireland in 1973. It remained on the books for certain crimes such as treason until it was completely abolished in 1998.

Two further points from the booklet are worth having. Since abolition in the UK, many people have been pardoned and released years after the crime they allegedly committed — had the death penalty still been legal they would already be dead. And in the US there have been botched executions, where the drugs were not administered correctly and the prisoner suffered in agony before dying, alongside hundreds of convicts on Death Row for years, which is severe for the prisoner and their family and enormously expensive for the state.

Christian arguments for. Article 37 of the Thirty-Nine Articles of the Church of England (1563) states that the Laws of the Realm may punish Christian men with death for "heinous and grievous offences", and in the sixteenth century many ministers agreed. Until the Middle Ages all Christians believed capital punishment was part of God's plan of salvation; Aquinas agreed, as did Pope Innocent III (1161–1216), and in 1566 the Council of Trent reaffirmed its morality, calling the just use of the power "an act of paramount obedience to the Commandment which prohibits murder". Supporters today cite Leviticus 24:21, which recommends the loss of life for those who take a life, and Genesis 9:6; Natural Law, to create an ordered society; and St Paul, that everyone should "obey the ruling authorities". Calvin is cited both ways — that laws do not override forgiveness.

Christian arguments against. Most Anglican priests now disagree with its use: in July 1983 the General Synod of the Church of England carried a motion that it "would deplore the reintroduction of capital punishment into the United Kingdom's sentencing policy". They cite the biblical teachings on the sanctity of life — punishing someone by taking their life devalues life itself — and the teaching and example of Jesus, who stopped an execution and rejected retribution ("love your enemies", Matthew 5:44; "turn the other cheek"). They also question whether it deters at all, and prefer imprisonment, which allows an opportunity for reform.

The Roman Catholic position changed across the twentieth century. In 1980 the National Conference of Catholic Bishops in America approved a statement criticising the death penalty. In 1995, in his encyclical Evangelium Vitae (the Gospel of Life), Pope John Paul II argued that execution should be used only in cases of absolute necessity, and that progress in penal systems had made such cases a rarity. On 3 October 2020, in the encyclical Fratelli Tutti (All Brothers), Pope Francis ratified the Church's opposition, declaring there can be no stepping back: "Today we state clearly that 'the death penalty is inadmissible' and the Church is firmly committed to calling for its abolition worldwide." The Salvation Army believes in reformation — Christ can transform lives.

Non-religious attitudes. In 1989 the International and Ethical Union, the worldwide group for Humanists, called for abolition across the world on the grounds that the death penalty denies the right to life as recognised by the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Humanists also doubt that it deters — and argue it may exacerbate crime, since a murderer who knows he will be killed if caught has an incentive to kill more people to avoid capture — and note that an incorrect conviction and execution cannot be reversed, whereas a wrongly imprisoned person can be released and compensated. Some non-religious people support it where the crime is so heinous that there is little chance of reforming the offender, applying Utilitarianism or Situation Ethics case by case.

The 12-mark frame

Five paragraphs, in this order. The top band needs Christian teaching in every one of them and a conclusion that actually decides, so write the conclusion you are heading for before you start.

  1. Restate the statement and say what turns on it. One or two sentences naming the real question — whether war can ever be just, whether forgiveness has limits, whether punishment should reform or repay.
  2. The strongest case for, with teaching and scripture. Not a list: one argument developed, then appraised. If the statement is pro-peace, this is Matthew 5:9 and 5:44, Micah 4:3 and Jesus' refusal to resist arrest in Gethsemane; if it is pro-force, Deuteronomy 7, 1 Samuel 15:3 and the Temple cleansing.
  3. The strongest case against, with teaching and scripture. Same again from the other side, and say which of the two arguments is stronger and why — the appraisal is where the AO2 marks are, not the listing.
  4. Where Christians disagree with each other. This is the paragraph that reaches the top level, because it shows the disagreement is internal to the religion: Quaker absolute pacifism against Just War theory; Gee Walker against Rev Julie Nicholson; Leviticus 24 against Matthew 5. Add a non-religious view for perspective — Utilitarianism, Situation Ethics or a Humanist position — but remember it does not count as the reference to a religion.
  5. A conclusion that decides. Name the winning argument, say why it outweighs the other, and tie it back to the words of the statement.

Sentence starters that earn the evaluation marks:

  • "Most Christians would agree with this statement, because…"
  • "An absolute pacifist, such as a Quaker, would argue…"
  • "However, a Christian who accepts Just War theory would respond that…"
  • "This argument is the stronger of the two, because…"
  • "Christians themselves are divided here: those who follow the Old Testament's retributive strand… whereas those who follow Jesus' teaching on forgiveness…"
  • "A Utilitarian would weigh this differently, since…" / "A situation ethicist would ask only what is most loving, and so…"
  • "On balance, I disagree with the statement, because the teaching of Jesus… outweighs…"

03Understanding it

U1

Why do Christians disagree about war when they all read the same Bible?

Show answer

Because the Bible itself contains both strands. The Old Testament has God commanding the destruction of the Amalekites (1 Samuel 15:3) and the taking of Jericho by supernatural means; the New Testament has Jesus blessing the peacemakers and telling his followers to love their enemies.

Christians then weigh those strands differently. Pacifists treat Jesus' teaching as the final revelation that supersedes the older material. Supporters of Just War argue that Jesus' commands were addressed to individuals rather than states — St Bernard's position — and point to his righteous anger in the Temple.

The exam point: every 12-marker here is won by showing that the disagreement is internal to Christianity, not between Christians and everyone else.

U2

What is the strongest objection to Just War theory, and how would a supporter answer it?

Show answer

The strongest objection: modern war can never be just. Modern weaponry causes devastation that is neither proportionate nor discriminate, so jus in bello is unsatisfiable in practice — Dresden and the atomic bombs are the standard illustrations, and around a million civilians died in Iraq.

The answer: the theory is a restraint, not a permission. Its purpose is to make most wars unjustifiable and to limit the ones that happen. Abandoning it does not produce peace; it removes the only criteria by which a Christian can criticise a war at all.

The objection about soldiers' conduct is weaker, because bad conduct breaks the theory rather than disproving it.

U3

Why do Christians find weapons of mass destruction harder to justify than conventional weapons?

Show answer

Because of jus in bello. WMD kill indiscriminately, so they cannot avoid attacking the innocent, and their scale makes any response disproportionate. Just War theory explicitly says WMD should not be used.

They also offend the sanctity of life and destroy God's creation — "the earth is the Lord's". Pacem in Terris called for an end to the arms race, and Pope Benedict XVI said a nuclear war would have "no victors, only victims".

The counter-position worth knowing: some Christians distinguish possessing from using. A deterrent that is never fired kills nobody — which is why states with strong Christian affiliations hold nuclear arsenals.

U4

Rev Julie Nicholson refused to forgive her daughter's killers. Gee Walker forgave her son's murderers. Which is the more Christian response?

Show answer

For Gee Walker: Jesus set no limit on forgiveness — "not seven times, but seventy-seven times" (Matthew 18:21–22) — and the Lord's Prayer makes being forgiven conditional on forgiving. The Prodigal Son's father forgives before anything is earned.

For Julie Nicholson: Jesus taught that forgiveness follows repentance — metanoia. Her position was that the guilty party had not repented, so forgiveness would be empty. Romans 12:19 also leaves vengeance to God rather than demanding the victim resolve it.

Use it like this: this pair is the cleanest way to show a 12-marker that Christians genuinely disagree, and that both positions are grounded in teaching rather than feeling.

U5

If the Bible never mentions bullying, how can Christians have teachings about it?

Show answer

They argue from general principles rather than a specific verse, and saying so openly is itself worth credit.

Genesis 1:26–27 — humans are made in God's image, so every person deserves respect. Agape and the Good Samaritan — love extends even to the stranger. The Beatitudes — "blessed are the meek" overturns a culture that venerates strength, which the lesson identifies as one cause of bullying. "Repay no one evil for evil" — the victim should not retaliate. Ephesians and the Psalms — honour God with your words.

The spec also asks about inclusion, which is why "everyone deserves equal treatment" is the central Christian claim here.

U6

Why do Christians say all crime comes from sin, but not all sin is crime?

Show answer

All crime from sin: crime is a breach of God's will as well as the law, because it harms a neighbour made in God's image. Christians trace it to the self-centredness that began with Original Sin.

Not all sin is crime: the two have different sources. Sin is determined by God; crime is identified by the government. So lying, greed and hatred are sins but not crimes, and a law could in principle make something criminal that God does not forbid.

The consequences differ too: crime is punished in this life, sin after death. That is the answer to "Which is worse?" — a crime has visible consequences, a sin has eternal ones.

U7

Why do most Christians think reform is the most important aim of punishment?

Show answer

Because reform is the aim that matches Jesus' teaching. He rejected retributive justice — "an eye for an eye… but now I tell you: do not take revenge" (Matthew 5:38–39) — and demonstrated it with the woman taken in adultery (John 8). He taught that forgiveness follows repentance and change, metanoia, which is exactly what reform aims at.

Christians also hold that every offender is made in God's image and can be redeemed; the Salvation Army's position is that Christ can transform lives. Practically, reform is the only aim that reduces reoffending, so it serves the stewardship duty to keep society just.

The balance to include: Christians do not abandon justice. Punishment must still be fair, must take mitigating circumstances into account, and a minority citing Leviticus 24 still prioritise retribution.

U8

Genesis 9:6 and John 8:1–11 are both used in the capital punishment debate. How can one religion hold both?

Show answer

Genesis 9:6 — "Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed; for in the image of God has God made man." Supporters read the imago Dei as making murder so grave that only a life answers for it.

John 8:1–11 — "If any one of you is without sin, let him be the first to throw a stone at her." Opponents read Jesus as stopping an execution outright, because no sinful human is fit to pass that judgement.

The resolution most Churches have reached is developmental: the Thirty-Nine Articles once supported it and the Roman Catholic Church once regarded it as part of God's plan, but Pope John Paul II restricted it to absolute necessity and Pope Francis rejected it in 2020. The same imago Dei that once justified execution is now used to forbid it.

D3

Show answer

Most Christians believe WMD can never be justified, because nuclear, chemical and biological weapons kill indiscriminately 1; this breaks the Just War condition of jus in bello, which forbids attacking the innocent and requires proportionate methods, and explicitly rules out weapons of mass destruction 1

Christians also appeal to the sanctity of life: life is God-given, so destroying it on this scale usurps God's authority 1; WMD also destroy God's creation, which Christians are stewards of — "the earth is the Lord's" — and the Roman Catholic Church in Pacem in Terris demanded "that the arms race should cease", while Pope Benedict XVI said a nuclear war would have "no victors, only victims" and Stephen Cottrell, Archbishop of York, held in 2020 that even possession could never be a just use of arms 1

D4

"There is no such thing as a Just War."

Evaluate this statement, showing you have considered different points of view. In your answer, you should refer to at least one religion. (12)

Show answer

Arguments for the statement.

Modern weaponry causes devastation that is neither proportionate nor discriminate, so jus in bello can never actually be met — Dresden and the atomic bombs on Japan are the standard examples 1

Controlling soldiers' conduct in the heat of war is impossible, so the rules collapse in practice even where they are accepted in principle 1

Deciding who is an innocent bystander and who is involved is a subjective judgement, so the condition protecting the innocent has no clear content 1

Even a "just" war produces horrors afterwards — around a million civilians died in Iraq — so jus post bellum is routinely failed 1

Arguments against the statement.

The conditions can be met: WWII had a legitimate authority (Chamberlain with the backing of Parliament), a just cause (stopping Hitler) and was a last resort after negotiation failed 1

The theory functions as a restraint rather than a permission — it exists to make most wars unjustifiable and to limit the conduct of the rest 1

Without it, resisting an aggressor becomes impossible, and some evil can only be met with force; pacifism also arguably relies on others fighting on your behalf 1

Christian teaching.

Augustine and Aquinas developed the theory precisely so Christians could resist evil without abandoning conscience, and many Christians accept war as a last resort to defeat a greater evil 1

Against this, pacifist Christians such as the Quakers argue the theory compromises Jesus' teaching — "blessed are the peacemakers", "love your enemies" — and Micah's vision of swords hammered into ploughs shows God's ideal world is disarmed 1

Supporters reply that Jesus showed righteous anger in the Temple, and that St Bernard held his peace commands applied to individuals rather than states 1

Conclusion.

Judgement: the theory's conditions are genuinely satisfiable at the point of going to war, which is why WWII is so widely accepted as just, but modern weapons make the conditions on fighting it very hard to satisfy 1

So the statement is too strong: a just war is possible but has become rarer, and the theory remains the only standard by which Christians can criticise a particular war at all — which is a reason to keep it rather than abandon it 1

D8

"Capital punishment is never justified."

Evaluate this statement, showing you have considered different points of view. In your answer, you should refer to at least one religion. (12)

Show answer

Arguments for the statement.

It is self-contradictory: taking a life to punish the taking of a life undermines the very value it claims to defend 1

Miscarriages of justice occur and an execution cannot be reversed, so the system guarantees that innocent people will eventually be killed 1

It does not deter murder, so it fails on its own terms, and in the US it falls disproportionately on poor Black and Latino defendants 1

It brutalises society and feeds a cycle of violence, and the long wait between sentence and execution is costly as well as cruel 1

Arguments against the statement.

It is the ultimate retribution for the worst crimes and embodies a natural justice — a life for a life 1

It protects law-abiding citizens absolutely, since an executed offender cannot reoffend 1

It may give the victim's family comfort or closure that no other sentence can, and some crimes are so heinous that the offender appears beyond reform 1

Christian teaching.

Supporters cite Genesis 9:6 — "whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed; for in the image of God has God made man" — and Leviticus 24; the Thirty-Nine Articles of the Church of England allowed it for the most heinous offences, and St Paul told Christians to obey the ruling authorities 1

Opponents cite the sanctity of life and John 8, where Jesus stops an execution with "if any one of you is without sin, let him be the first to throw a stone at her", and Matthew 5, where he rejects "an eye for an eye" 1

The Churches have moved: Pope John Paul II restricted it to cases of absolute necessity, Pope Francis opposed it outright in 2020 as fostering vengeance, and the Salvation Army argues Christ can transform any life — while Humanists reached the same conclusion from the right to life, calling for worldwide abolition in 1989 1

Conclusion.

Judgement: the retributive case is the strongest argument for, but it rests on a reading of imago Dei that Jesus himself set aside in John 8 when he refused to join a lawful stoning 1

Since the deterrent claim is unsupported and the risk of irreversible error is real — many people in the UK have been pardoned years after conviction — I agree with the statement, which is also the direction the Churches themselves have moved in, from Article 37 of the Thirty-Nine Articles to Fratelli Tutti 1

D11

Explain the differences between sins and crimes. (6)

Show answer

The two differ in source: a crime is an illegal act identified by the government, whereas a sin is determined by God 1; this matters to Christians because the law can change while God's will does not, so an act can be legal and still sinful — which is why Christians sometimes campaign against laws they regard as unjust 1

They differ in effect: a crime breaks the law of the land, while a sin is a violation of God's will, rooted in disobedience and being self-centred rather than God-centred, as Adam and Eve were in the Original Sin 1; this gives sin a relational dimension a crime does not have, because it damages the sinner's relationship with God 1

They differ in consequences: a crime is punished in this life, by imprisonment, a fine or community service, whereas the punishment for sin comes after death 1; Revelation 21 warns that liars, murderers and idolaters will find their place "in the fiery lake of burning sulphur", which is why Christians regard repentance — metanoia — as more urgent than any court appearance, and why not all sins are crimes while Christians would argue all crime has its source in sin 1

D12

"Passive resistance is more effective than fighting."

Evaluate this statement, showing you have considered different points of view. In your answer, you should refer to at least one religion. (12)

Show answer

Arguments for the statement.

It has a record of success: Gandhi's campaigns and Martin Luther King's bus boycotts, marches and speeches achieved change that violence had not 1

It wins public sympathy, which converts opponents instead of hardening them, so the settlement lasts — as in South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission 1

It breaks the cycle of violence rather than continuing it, and it costs no lives, which matters if human life is treated as having absolute value 1

Gandhi's claim that non-violence is "mightier than the mightiest weapon of destruction devised by the ingenuity of man" is at least arguable, given how often military victories have had to be re-fought 1

Arguments against the statement.

It is a weak response to a determined aggressor who is not susceptible to public opinion — appeasement did not stop Hitler 1

It can be hypocritical: pacifists still benefit from the protection of those who do fight on their behalf 1

It rules out a pre-emptive strike that might have saved more lives than it cost, so on a straightforward count of lives it can be the costlier option 1

Christian teaching.

Many Christians are pacifists because of the biblical teachings of peace, love and forgiveness; the Quakers use the "weapons of the Spirit" — truth, love and peace — and became conscientious objectors in both World Wars, serving as stretcher-bearers or going to prison 1

Jesus' own teaching supports this: "blessed are the peacemakers", "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you", and Micah's vision of swords hammered into ploughs 1

Against this, conditional pacifists and supporters of Just War argue that extreme cases leave no option, citing "greater love has no man than this that he lay down his life for his friends" and Jesus' righteous anger in the Temple 1

Conclusion.

Judgement: effectiveness depends on the opponent. Against a government that needs public legitimacy, passive resistance is demonstrably more effective; against a regime indifferent to opinion and willing to kill, the evidence is much weaker 1

So I partly agree: passive resistance should always be tried first and is usually more effective, but the claim that it is always more effective is not supported — which is why most Christians are conditional rather than absolute pacifists 1
