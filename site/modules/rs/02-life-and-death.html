  • Dualism: body and soul — the body deteriorates and dies, but the soul is the permanent part of the person.

Spec 2.1 · Non-religious beliefs and views about death and human destiny · Immortality of the soul · Whether or not there is an afterlife; why some believe and others do not

Death as the end: physicalism

Physicalism is the view that each person is only a living, physical body, so death marks the end of that individual's existence.

  • We are capable of thought and feeling because of the functions of our brains, not because of a soul.
  • A loss of brain function or efficiency affects our ability to relate to people and to recall our past — when brains deteriorate, reasoning and self-control wane.
  • There is no scientific evidence of a soul or spirit.
  • Consciousness is physical brain activity, and death plainly destroys the brain and the material body.
  • Therefore there is no afterlife.

Physicalists add a sharper point about why people believe in an afterlife: the belief is caused by the desire to go on living, by fear of death, and by an inability to imagine a world without us. Marx went further — the promise of a next life can be used by the ruling classes to control communities, which is why he called religion an opiate.

Body and soul: dualism and Plato

Spec 2.1 · Immortality of the soul

Dualism is the view that a human being is composed of body and soul: the body deteriorates and dies, but the soul is the permanent part of the person.

Plato distinguished two realms. The body changes and so belongs to the realm of becoming; the soul is a simple entity that cannot be broken into parts and is not subject to change, so it belongs to the realm of being. For Plato the immortal soul continues to exist after the death of the body.

This matters in RS because the argument is not only religious. Some atheists and Humanists accept an afterlife because they think human beings are psycho-somatic entities — body and soul together — and they appeal to Plato rather than to scripture. They may also point to paranormal evidence:

  • Near-death experiences — reports of consciousness continuing while the body is clinically dying.
  • Spiritualism — psychics, mediums, communication with the dead, ghosts.
  • Reincarnation and remembered lives.
  • Some evidence that brain activity continues after the heart stops.

Belief in an afterlife may also simply be beneficial: it gives life meaning and it comforts those whose loved ones have died.

Why Christians believe in life after death

Spec 2.1 · Christian beliefs about resurrection and the nature of life after death

  • Four reasons, in order — God’s omnipotence and omnibenevolence, Jesus’s teaching, Jesus’s resurrection, and dualism.
  • John 3:16 is the hinge — eternal life is God’s offer, received through faith in Jesus.
  • Resurrection, not just an immortal soul — Paul’s "spiritual bodies" (1 Corinthians 15); Matthew 27:52 has bodies raised.
More detail
  • God can and wants to — he created us once, so he can create us again, and he loves us enough to want to.
  • Christians split on what survives — an immortal soul influenced by Greek thought, or God resurrecting body and soul together.

Christian belief in an afterlife rests on the nature of God, the teaching and resurrection of Jesus, and a dualistic understanding of the person.

  1. The omnipotence and omnibenevolence of God — he created us once and he can create us again, and he loves us enough to want to.
  2. Jesus taught it, and his atoning work on the cross made it possible for humanity to be with God in the next life.
  3. Jesus's resurrection shows that death is not a permanent state.
  4. Dualism — we are body and soul, and the soul can continue to exist with God.

Christians divide on what survives. Some teach an immortal soul superior to the body, influenced by Greek thought. Others reject that as unbiblical and as detracting from humanity's dependence on God: to experience life after death we depend on God's love and power, and just before judgement God resurrects those who have died, body and soul together — which is what happened to Jesus.

Eschatology is the study of Christian teachings about the End Time, or Eschaton, and it is based on the apocalyptic teachings of the book of Revelation.

Resurrection and judgement

Spec 2.1 · Judgement; the law of cause and effect in relation to life after death

  • Three grounds of judgement — belief in God, acceptance of Jesus’s sacrifice, and obedience to his commandments.
  • Faith: "whoever believes in the Son has eternal life" — salvation is grace, not something earned.
  • Works: the Sheep and the Goats — judged on feeding the hungry and visiting prisoners (Matthew 25:31–46).
More detail
  • Resurrected and judged together — at the Eschaton the dead rise and stand with the living before Jesus.
  • James settles it — "faith without works is dead", so works are the evidence of faith rather than a rival to it.
  • A 6-marker wants consequences — charity, forgiveness, obedience, confession and missionary work.

At the beginning of the Eschaton the dead will be resurrected and judged alongside the living by Jesus.

Judgement is based on belief in God, acceptance of Jesus's sacrifice, and obedience to God's commandments. God will judge all either at the end of life or at the end of the world; all people are sinful, and salvation is a gift through Christ.

Cause and effect in a Christian frame: faith in Jesus plus good works leads to reward in the afterlife. Some Protestants emphasise faith alone — we cannot buy our way into heaven, and the Church cannot intercede for us. Others point to James to insist that works matter too. Where other religions are concerned, some Christians are exclusivists and hold that salvation is only for Christians; others disagree.

Heaven and hell

Spec 2.1 · Christian beliefs about heaven and hell

  • Heaven is the reward — eternal communion with God and no sin or suffering; Jesus likens it to a wedding feast.
  • Hell is eternal punishment — Revelation’s "lake of fire"; Dante’s Inferno imagines nine circles.
  • Four readings of hell — literal torture, annihilation, a symbol of separation from God, or empty.
More detail
  • Three readings of heaven — a real place, this world transformed, or a symbol of relationship with God.
  • Universalism — hell as correction with eventual salvation for all; Luke 16:19–31 tells against it.
  • Exclusivists — hold heaven is only for Christians, which leaves the problem of those never told.

Heaven is described as paradise and hell as a place of torment — but Christians disagree about whether either is a place at all.

Heaven. It is the reward for faith and/or good works; the kingdom of God fully experienced; eternal life in everlasting communion with God; a shared community with no sin, evil, suffering or death. Jesus likens it to a banquet or wedding feast, and Revelation describes "a rainbow, resembling an emerald, encircling the throne of God". The interpretations:

  • Heaven is a real place, a higher metaphysical world.
  • Heaven is this world transformed after the day of judgement.
  • Heaven is a symbol of eternal life in positive relationship with God, not a location.
  • Some see it in terms of bodily resurrection, others as a higher world of souls.
  • Exclusivists hold that it is only for Christians.

Hell. It is eternal punishment for the wicked — the immoral and non-believers — with no hope of redemption; the domain of the devil. Revelation describes wrongdoers being "thrown into the lake of fire", and Dante's Inferno imagines nine circles, with Limbo first, entered through the gate of Hell itself, over which is written "abandon hope all you who enter here". The interpretations:

  • Hell is a literal place of eternal torture.
  • Hell is annihilation — simply ceasing to exist.
  • Hell is a symbol of separation from God: existing without divine love and peace.
  • Hell is incompatible with the love of God, so if it exists it must be empty.

Some Christians find eternal punishment impossible to square with a just God, and so treat hell as a place of correction, expecting eventual universal salvation — universalism. Against that, the book of Revelation and some of Jesus's own teaching — the parable of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31) — describe the afterlife as eternal and one's fate as fixed.

Purgatory

Spec 2.1 · Religious beliefs about the nature of life after death

  • A Roman Catholic belief — after judgement the righteous are purified of their sins before entering heaven.
  • Prayers for the dead — the process can be shortened, so relatives are encouraged to pray.
  • The Protestant objection — not in the Bible, salvation is by faith alone, and it implies Christ’s sacrifice was insufficient.
More detail
  • The "final purification of the elect" — developed in the medieval period, because most are not pure enough for heaven.
  • The standard 4-marker — "two different Christian beliefs about purgatory": one from each column, each developed.

Purgatory is a Roman Catholic belief that after judgement the righteous spend time being purified of their sins before entering heaven.

It was developed during the medieval period to explain how someone who had sinned could still be saved: most people are not pure enough to enter heaven immediately and need purification. The Roman Catholic Church calls it the "final purification of the elect". Because the process can be shortened, relatives are encouraged to pray for the dead.

Linear and cyclical views of human existence

Spec 2.1 · Linear and cyclical views; immortality of the soul, resurrection, rebirth and judgement

  • Linear (Christian) — one life, one death, one resurrection and one judgement, then heaven or hell.
  • Hinduism: atman, karma, moksha — the soul is released and reincarnated, aiming to unite with Brahman.
  • Buddhism: no soul at all — anicca, anatta and dukkha; the skandhas separate, so only consequences survive.
More detail
  • Cyclical — deeds decide rebirth into a higher or lower form, or escape from the cycle altogether.
  • Compare on four themes — identity, judgement, opportunity and the problem of evil.
  • Paper 1 rule — your named religion must be Christianity; other traditions only supply the alternative view.

Christianity holds a linear view of human existence; the cyclical view belongs to Eastern religions, and the contrast is what the exam wants.

The linear view (Christian): you are born, you live, you die; at the End Time the dead are resurrected and stand with the living to be judged on their belief in and acceptance of God and on their life choices; they then experience either eternity in heaven or eternal damnation in hell. Death is the end of human life, and there is one life and one judgement.

The cyclical view: a person's deeds determine whether they are reborn into a higher or lower form of life, or escape the cycle of birth and rebirth altogether.

TraditionWhat it teaches
HinduismWe are psycho-somatic beings; each living being has an atman (soul), the permanent aspect of the individual. At death the atman is released and reincarnated; the type of creature depends on karma — one's actions. The aim is moksha, freedom from the cycle of samsara, so the atman unites with Brahman, the World Soul.
BuddhismThe idea of a psycho-somatic unity is an illusion — there is no soul. Every individual suffers three universal symptoms: anicca (change), anatta (no soul) and dukkha (suffering or dissatisfaction). Extinguishing greed and selfishness by following the Noble Eightfold Path brings enlightenment and nirvana; otherwise at death the skandhas separate and merge with others to form new beings, so only our consequences survive.

Christian teachings on the meaning and purpose of life

Spec 2.2 · Eternal life, the kingdom of God, salvation, Jesus as lord and saviour; salvation by faith versus works

  • Meaning comes from creation and salvation — we were made by God and are offered eternal life through Christ.
  • Aquinas: the supreme good is God — not happiness, but a positive relationship with the God who made us.
  • Love, kingdom and self-denial — love God and neighbour, build the kingdom, take up your cross daily (Luke 9:23).
More detail
  • Augustine: we cannot save ourselves — so we must put our trust in Christ.
  • Christian hedonism — the purpose of humanity is to enjoy God lavishly, by knowing, worshipping and serving him.
  • Salvation: faith, works or both — John 3:16 against the Sheep and the Goats, with James insisting on both.

For Christians the meaning and purpose of life come from the doctrines of creation and salvation: we were made by God, and we are offered eternal life through Christ.

  • Created by God (Genesis) — we are part of God's plan, and meaning comes from that.
  • To praise and serve God. Aquinas argued that the supreme good for a human being is not happiness but a positive relationship with God: we are alive because God created us, and seeking God out and experiencing him gives life meaning.
  • Salvation and forgiveness — accepting Christ's sacrifice on the cross and living by God's commandments. Augustine held that we cannot save ourselves and must put our trust in Christ.
  • Love and Christian ethical practice — the greatest commandment to love God and love your neighbour.
  • Building the kingdom of God — aiming to transform the earth so that it reflects God's kingdom.
  • Self-denial — "Whoever wants to be my disciple must deny themselves and take up their cross daily and follow me." (Luke 9:23)
  • Christian hedonism — the primary purpose of humanity is to enjoy God lavishly, by knowing, worshipping and serving him.

Salvation is achieved through the grace of God, faith, worship and love. Protestants who emphasise faith alone argue that we cannot buy our way into heaven and the Church cannot intercede; others insist, with James, that good works are required evidence.

Non-religious meaning and purpose — and whether the question even helps

Spec 2.2 · Non-religious ideas about the meaning and purpose of life; whether life can have meaning only for religious people

  • Six named positions — Darwinian survival, Aristotle’s flourishing, hedonism and Epicurus, Bentham, Sartre, Nietzsche.
  • Sartre: radical freedom — no God means no script, so we must choose and invent meaning for ourselves.
  • Nietzsche: nihilism — God is dead, values are baseless, and all meaning and purpose are false.
More detail
  • Epicurus is the mild version — minimise pain and live a happy, tranquil life.
  • Humanist views do not count as the religion — a 12-marker still needs reference to at least one religion.
  • Marx: religion as an opiate — a false higher meaning that distracts people from social progress.

Non-religious accounts of meaning do not appeal to God, and the spec expects you to name the thinkers.

  • Darwinian — the purpose of life is to survive and reproduce.
  • Aristotle — the purpose is to flourish: living and doing well allows the full expression of the human intellect.
  • Hedonism — pleasure is the most important intrinsic good (hedone is Greek for pleasure). Epicurus argued a milder version: minimise pain and live a happy, tranquil life.
  • Bentham and the utilitarians — value lies in seeking the greatest good for the greatest number.
  • Sartre — if God is dead there is no script or plan; there is no external, fixed meaning, so we must choose and invent meaning for ourselves. Radical freedom.
  • Nietzsche — God is dead, all values are baseless and reason is impotent; all meaning and purpose are false. Nihilism.

Spec 2.3 · Christian teachings about the sanctity of life and the particular value of human life; non-religious beliefs about the value of life — not yet taught in class — built from the specification

Sanctity of life is the belief that life is a sacred gift from God: it is intrinsically valuable, so it must be protected, and it is not for human beings to decide when it ends.

Two halves to the belief, and the exam wants both:

  1. Life is intrinsically valuable — its worth does not depend on how useful, healthy or happy the person is. It comes from God.
  2. Life is not ours to end — God gives it and God takes it away, so decisions about death belong to him.

The Roman Catholic position is the strongest form of the belief. Natural Law gives preserve life as a primary precept, and an ordered society is one in which human life is protected. The Catechism teaches that human life is sacred because from its beginning it involves the creative action of God, and that it must be respected and protected absolutely from the moment of conception.

More liberal Christians hold a weaker form. Life is still sacred and the presumption is always against ending it — but in a tragic case, ending a life may be the most loving option, and love (agape) is the one absolute. This is situation ethics, and it is where almost all the internal Christian disagreement in 2.3 comes from.

Non-religious views on the value of life do not appeal to God, and they are examined just as hard:

  • Utilitarians value a life for the happiness it contains, so quality of life rather than mere biological life is what matters.
  • Peter Singer argues that human life is not intrinsically more valuable than animal life; what matters morally is self-awareness and the capacity to suffer, which a foetus or a brain-dead patient may lack.
  • Most non-religious people still hold human life to be of very high value: the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) states in Article 3 that everyone has the right to life.
  • The warning example of judging some lives less valuable than others is Nazi Germany's racial ideology — which both religious and non-religious thinkers use as a reason to keep the value of human life absolute.

Spec 2.8 · Religious and non-religious beliefs and attitudes to childlessness; decisions not to have children and the reasons for such decisions — not yet taught in class — built from the specification

Christianity has traditionally treated children as a blessing and procreation as one of the purposes of marriage, so childlessness raises a real question — and Christians disagree sharply over whether a couple may deliberately choose it.

Keep the two kinds apart, because the arguments are different:

  1. Involuntary childlessness — the couple want children and cannot have them. No Christian treats this as a sin; the question is what may be done about it.
  2. Voluntary childlessness — the couple choose not to have children. This is the contested one.

Reasons people decide not to have children: they do not want them; they are infertile or have difficulty conceiving; there is a risk of passing on a genetic disease; careers matter more to them; the financial cost, and the freedom of life without them; and for some, concern about population or the environment.

  • Eugenics is the shared worry — religious and non-religious alike fear deciding which lives are worth starting.

Contraception

Spec 2.8 · Contraception; Christian beliefs and teachings about contraception

  • The clearest denominational split in the section — Catholics forbid artificial methods, Protestants leave it to conscience.
  • Humanae Vitae (1968) — every marital act must remain open to the transmission of life.
  • Lambeth 1930 — the Church of England accepted contraception in marriage where there is a morally sound reason.
More detail
  • Artificial against natural — condom, pill, coil, implant and sterilisation, against natural family planning and abstinence.

Spec 2.8 · Contraception; Christian beliefs and teachings about contraception including reasons for and against it, and non-religious attitudes — not yet taught in class — built from the specification

This is the clearest denominational split in the whole section: the Roman Catholic Church forbids artificial contraception and permits natural methods, while Anglicans and most Protestants leave it to the couple's conscience.

  • Artificial contraception — a device, drug or operation that prevents conception: the condom, the pill, the IUD or coil, implants, sterilisation.
  • Natural contraception — avoiding conception without any device, by restricting sex to the woman's infertile days: the rhythm or calendar method, now usually called natural family planning. Total abstinence counts too.
Christian arguments against artificial contraceptionChristian arguments for it
Natural Law: sex has a procreative and a unitive purpose, and artificial contraception deliberately destroys the first while keeping the second. Catholic teaching in Humanae Vitae (1968) holds that every marital act must remain open to the transmission of life, and treats artificial contraception as intrinsically wrong.The Church of England accepted contraception within marriage at the 1930 Lambeth Conference where there is a morally sound reason for limiting a family, and later treated family planning as a matter for the couple's own conscience.
God decides when a person conceives, and contraception takes that decision from him — the same "playing God" argument as in 2.3.Responsible parenthood: a couple should only have the children they can feed, house and raise properly, and the Bible nowhere forbids contraception.
Children are a blessing (Psalm 127:3) and Genesis 1:28 commands being fruitful, so planning them away works against God's purpose for marriage.The lesser of two evils: contraception prevents unwanted pregnancies — and therefore abortions — and prevents the spread of sexually transmitted infections, including HIV.
Some methods may act after fertilisation rather than before it, which on the Catholic view of conception makes them abortive rather than contraceptive.Situation ethics: if using contraception is the most loving thing to do for the couple, their existing children and the wider family, then agape permits it.
It is said to encourage sex outside marriage and promiscuity by removing the consequences.Conscience and freedom: most Protestant churches hold that this is a private decision and not a matter of doctrine at all.
But note: the Catholic objection is to artificial methods. Natural family planning is permitted, because the couple do not interfere with the act itself.Critics of the Catholic position reply that the intention is identical in both cases, so the distinction is about the means and not the purpose.

Non-religious attitudes:

  • A straightforward matter of choice for the couple; Mill's harm principle finds nobody else harmed.
  • Far better than bringing unwanted children into the world, and a basic tool of public health against HIV and other infections.
  • A feminist issue: contraception gave women control over their own bodies, and with it education, careers and a real say in family size.
  • The only caution is that it should be used responsibly — reliably, and with regard to a partner's health.

✗ "Catholics are not allowed contraception."
✓ "Catholic teaching forbids artificial contraception, because Natural Law makes sex both unitive and procreative (Humanae Vitae, 1968) — but it permits natural family planning, where the couple restrict sex to infertile days."

✗ "The Bible says contraception is wrong." No verse addresses it. The argument runs through Natural Law and the procreative purpose of marriage, and saying so is worth more than inventing a quotation.

Celibacy

Spec 2.8 · Celibacy, including reasons for and against it

  • Celibacy is a calling, not a rule — the settled choice to abstain from sex and usually from marriage.
  • Who takes the vow — Latin-Church Catholic priests, monks and nuns; Anglican and Methodist clergy may marry.
  • For: 1 Corinthians 7:1 and 7:32–33 — Paul’s own preference, and undivided attention to the Lord’s affairs.
More detail
  • Celibacy is not chastity — chastity is the right ordering of sexual behaviour for whatever state you are in.

Spec 2.8 · Celibacy, including reasons for and against it and differing attitudes to it — not yet taught in class — built from the specification

Celibacy is choosing to abstain from sexual activity and usually from marriage, and in Christianity it is treated as a particular calling rather than a general rule.

Who it applies to: Roman Catholic priests of the Latin Church, and monks and nuns, take a vow of celibacy. Anglican, Methodist and other Protestant clergy may marry; the Orthodox churches ordain married men as parish priests but choose their bishops from among the celibate. Catholic and conservative evangelical teaching also calls gay Christians to celibacy, as set out in 2.4.

Reasons for celibacyReasons against it
Undivided service: without a spouse and children, a priest, monk or nun is free for prayer, ministry and the people they serve (1 Corinthians 7:32–33).Not everyone can do it. Paul himself says that those who cannot control themselves should marry (1 Corinthians 7:9), so requiring it of all clergy sets up people to fail.
Imitating Christ: Jesus was celibate, and Matthew 19:12 treats renouncing marriage for the kingdom as a genuine calling.Genesis 1:28 commands being fruitful, and Genesis 2:18 says it is not good for a person to be alone — so marriage, not celibacy, is the created norm.
Paul's preference: "it is good for a man not to touch a woman" (1 Corinthians 7:1), and he wished others could be as he was.Most Protestants reject compulsory clerical celibacy as a human rule rather than a biblical one; the Reformers argued a married minister understands family life better.
Special standing: Revelation describes those who "did not defile themselves with women" as following the Lamb, which has been read as giving the celibate a particular place in heaven.Peter and the other apostles were married — Jesus heals Peter's mother-in-law (Mark 1:30) — so celibacy was never a condition of leading the church.
Discipline: self-denial is a Christian virtue, and giving up something good for God is an act of devotion.It is an unnatural denial of a God-given part of being human, and a rule imposed rather than freely chosen can do real damage.

Non-religious attitudes: celibacy is simply a matter of choice, and nobody else's business. It can let a person concentrate on study, work or other commitments; a relationship can be committed and close without sex; and some people are asexual, so for them it is not a sacrifice at all. The usual non-religious criticism is not of chosen celibacy but of required celibacy, imposed by an institution on people who did not choose it for themselves.

"An unmarried man is concerned about the Lord's affairs — how he can please the Lord. But a married man is concerned about the affairs of this world." — 1 Corinthians 7:32–33
Used to argue: the celibate person can give God undivided attention, which is the practical case for clerical celibacy.

"Others have renounced marriage because of the kingdom of heaven. The one who can accept this should accept it." — Matthew 19:12
Used to argue: Jesus commends celibacy and says it is not for everyone — both halves matter.

"But if they cannot control themselves, they should marry… it is better to marry than to burn with passion." — 1 Corinthians 7:9
Used to argue: celibacy is a gift given to some, not a demand made of all. Even Paul concedes the point.

✓ That is chastity, not celibacy. Celibacy is the settled choice to abstain from sex and usually from marriage; chastity is the right ordering of sexual behaviour for whatever state you are in — which for an unmarried Christian means abstinence, and for a married one means faithfulness.

Spec 2.8 · Adoption and fostering, including religious and non-religious attitudes to them — not yet taught in class — built from the specification

Adoption and fostering are the alternative that almost every Christian argument in this section ends up recommending — against abortion, against childlessness, and against the fertility treatments the churches find difficult.

  • Adoption — parental responsibility is transferred permanently and legally to the adoptive parents, and the child becomes a full member of the new family.
  • Fostering — temporary care in a family home while legal responsibility stays with the birth parents or the local authority; the child may return home or go on to be adopted.

Two further arguments come up constantly and are worth having ready. First, Jesus himself was raised by a man who was not his biological father, which Christians cite to show that a family is made by love and commitment rather than by genetics. Second, adoption is the standing alternative: many Christians argue it is preferable to abortion, and preferable to fertility treatments that create and discard embryos — a childless couple can still raise a child, and a child who needs a family gets one.

"Every Christian with the means should foster or adopt" — for— against
James 1:27 makes looking after orphans the mark of pure religion, and Matthew 25:40 ties judgement to care for the vulnerable.The Bible commends caring for the vulnerable without specifying how; giving, fostering support, or helping a struggling family can meet the same duty.
Thousands of children are waiting for a permanent family, and the need is real and immediate.Not every household is suited to it. A placement that breaks down can harm the child more than waiting would.
Christians are themselves adopted children of God (Ephesians 1:5), so the practice reflects the gospel.Christians also have duties to their own children, their parents and their work (1 Timothy 5:8), and these can rightly come first.
Christian teaching that opposes abortion is much stronger when the same Christians offer the alternative they recommend.A duty is too strong a word for something that must be freely and carefully chosen; most churches call it a vocation, not an obligation.

Where Christians have disagreed: adoption by same-sex couples. In England and Wales unmarried and same-sex couples have been able to adopt since 2005, and the equality regulations of 2007 removed the ability of agencies to refuse them. Some Catholic adoption agencies closed or separated from the Church rather than comply. The arguments are the ones from 2.4 and 2.5: on one side the welfare of a child who needs a family now, and gay people as equally made in God's image; on the other the church's doctrine that marriage is between a man and a woman, and the claim that a child is best placed with a mother and a father.

Non-religious attitudes are strongly supportive, and the governing principle in law is that the welfare of the child is paramount — so what matters is whether the household can love and care for the child, not its shape. The usual non-religious additions: adoption is an answer to childlessness that does not need medical technology; it reduces the harm of children growing up in institutional care; and the process should be made quicker and less obstructive.

Why Christians support adoption

"Religion that God our Father accepts as pure and faultless is this: to look after orphans and widows in their distress." — James 1:27
Used to argue: caring for children who have no one is not optional charity but the test of real religion.

"A father to the fatherless… God sets the lonely in families." — Psalm 68:5–6
Used to argue: placing a child in a family is God's own work, so Christians doing it are joining in with him.

"He predestined us for adoption to sonship through Jesus Christ." — Ephesians 1:5
Used to argue: adoption is the image Paul uses for salvation itself — Christians are adopted children of God, so adopting a child mirrors what God has done for them.

"Whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me." — Matthew 25:40
Used to argue: the Sheep and the Goats makes care of the vulnerable the basis of judgement, and a child in care is exactly who is meant.

✗ "Adoption is when you look after someone else's child for a while."
✓ That is fostering. Adoption transfers parental responsibility permanently and legally. Mixing them up costs a definition mark in part (a).

In a 12-marker on childlessness, contraception or abortion, adoption is the point that turns an argument into an evaluation — "there is an alternative, and here it is" — so keep it in reserve for every one of them.

The must-knows

The two positions on death: physicalism (we are only a physical body, so death ends us) and dualism (body and soul, and the soul persists).

Four reasons Christians believe in an afterlife: God's omnipotence and omnibenevolence; Jesus taught it; Jesus's resurrection; dualism.

Three destinations: heaven, hell and — for Roman Catholics only — purgatory, which Protestants reject as unbiblical.

Linear versus cyclical: one life, one judgement (Christian) against rebirth determined by deeds (Hinduism, Buddhism).

Faith or works: John 3:16 for faith, the Sheep and the Goats for works, James to tie them together.

Named thinkers: Plato, Aquinas, Augustine for the religious side; Aristotle, Epicurus, Bentham, Sartre, Nietzsche, Marx for the non-religious side.

The 12-mark frame

The same five paragraphs work for both halves of this section, but the vocabulary changes. Use the afterlife column for 2.1–2.2 questions and the ethics column for 2.3–2.8.

Paragraph 1 — restate the statement and say what turns on it. Afterlife: "The claim that death is the end matters because Christian hope, judgement and the whole point of living well depend on something surviving." Ethics: "The claim that [abortion / euthanasia / choosing childlessness] is always wrong turns on whether the sanctity of life is absolute, or whether agape can override it."

Paragraph 2 — the strongest case FOR, with teaching and scripture, then appraise it. Afterlife: "A Christian would argue that eternal life is God's offer — 'whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life' (John 3:16) — and that Jesus's resurrection shows death is not permanent. This is strong within the faith, but it rests on the authority of a text the critic does not accept." Ethics: "A Christian who follows Natural Law would argue that preserving life is a primary precept, and that life is sacred from conception because every human bears the image of God (Genesis 1:27). This is the most consistent position, because it needs no judgement about whose life is worth living."

Paragraph 3 — the strongest case AGAINST, with teaching and scripture, then appraise it. Afterlife: "However, a physicalist would respond that consciousness is brain activity and death destroys the brain, so there is nothing left to survive; near-death experiences have naturalistic explanations. This is the stronger evidential position, though it cannot disprove an afterlife either." Ethics: "However, a situation ethicist would respond that agape is the only absolute, so in a tragic case the most loving act may be the one that ends a life — and Christians also have a duty to relieve suffering, which here pulls against the duty to preserve life."

Paragraph 4 — where Christians disagree with each other. Name the denominations; "some Christians… other Christians…" is worth less. "Roman Catholics hold that…, while the Church of England accepts that in strictly limited circumstances…" · "Catholics believe in purgatory as the final purification of the elect, which Protestants reject as unbiblical." · "Quakers have celebrated same-sex marriages since 2009, whereas the Roman Catholic Church teaches that marriage is by its nature between a man and a woman."

Paragraph 5 — a conclusion that decides. Weigh the arguments, state your own view, and give the reason. "On balance, the argument from autonomy is strong, but the slippery-slope objection has not been answered, so I agree only with the limited version of the statement." · "Because the paranormal evidence is unverifiable, I find the physicalist case stronger on the evidence — though it does not touch the Christian argument from the nature of God." Never end with "it depends what you believe".

Two things lose easy marks here. Humanist and atheist views give useful perspective but do not count as the reference to one religion. And in the ethics questions, adoption and palliative care are the alternatives that turn an argument into an evaluation — keep one of them in reserve for every one of them.

03Understanding it

The "why" questions behind the content. Answer these in your own words and the 6- and 12-markers become a matter of selecting material rather than hunting for it.

U1

Why do Christians believe in life after death?

Show answer

From the nature of God: he is omnipotent, so having created us once he can create us again; and omnibenevolent, so he wants to.

From Jesus: he taught that there is life after death, and his atoning work on the cross made it possible for humanity to be with God. His own physical resurrection shows death is not permanent.

From the nature of the person: Christians are dualists — we are body and soul, and the soul can continue to exist with God (Ecclesiastes 12:7).

From scripture's authority: the Bible plainly states that eternal life is on offer — "whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life" (John 3:16).

U2

What is the strongest objection to belief in an afterlife?

Show answer

That consciousness is physical brain activity. If thought, memory and personality depend on the brain — and we can watch them fail as the brain fails — then death, which destroys the brain, destroys the person. No separate thing is left to continue.

Supporting objections: there is no good-quality empirical evidence; psychics, mediums, ghosts and near-death experiences all have rational psychological explanations; and there is a conceptual problem — what exactly would continue, and would it still be you?

The debunking move: belief in an afterlife is itself explained as a desire to go on living, fear of death, and an inability to imagine a world without us — and, for Marx, as a tool of social control.

U3

Why do some non-religious people believe in an afterlife, when they do not believe in God?

Show answer

Because the argument for a soul need not be religious. Some atheists and Humanists hold that human beings are psycho-somatic entities — body and soul — and appeal to Plato: the body belongs to the changing realm of becoming, while the soul is simple, unchanging and belongs to the realm of being, so it survives the body.

They may also point to paranormal evidence: near-death experiences, spiritualism and communication with the dead, remembered lives, and evidence that brain activity continues after the heart stops.

Finally, the belief can be defended on its effects: it gives life meaning and it comforts the bereaved.

U4

Why do Christians disagree about hell?

Show answer

Because eternal punishment is hard to reconcile with a just and loving God. If hell is endless torture for a finite life, the punishment seems disproportionate, which undermines God's justice; and if God is omnibenevolent, some argue hell must be incompatible with his love, so if it exists it must be empty.

The alternatives: hell as annihilation — simply ceasing to exist; hell as a symbol of separation from God, existing without divine love and peace; or hell as a place of correction, leading eventually to universal salvation.

Against all of that stands scripture: Revelation describes wrongdoers "thrown into the lake of fire", and the parable of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31) presents the afterlife as eternal with one's fate fixed.

U5

Why do Protestants reject purgatory?

Show answer

Two reasons, and both are worth a mark. First, it is not in the Bible — it was developed in the medieval period, and Protestants who take scripture as the sole authority find no basis for it.

Second, it conflicts with salvation by faith alone. If a person can be purified over time, and if the prayers of the living can shorten that process, then salvation starts to look like something achieved by human works — and that implies Christ's sacrifice was not sufficient on its own.

The Catholic case: most people are not pure enough to enter God's presence immediately, so a "final purification of the elect" explains how a just God can still save imperfect people.

U6

How does a linear view of existence differ from a cyclical one, and what does each do better?

Show answer

Linear (Christian): one life, then resurrection and a single judgement, then heaven or hell. Cyclical: deeds determine rebirth into a higher or lower form until one escapes the cycle.

Linear does better on identity and urgency. The same person is raised and judged, so identity is preserved; and because there is only one life, the choices made in it matter absolutely.

Cyclical does better on the problem of evil and on opportunity. Suffering is explained as the consequence of deeds in a previous life, no judge is needed because karma works as an impersonal law, and there are further chances to improve.

Each has a cost: the linear view must explain why a good God permits suffering and eternal punishment; the Buddhist version of the cyclical view has no soul, so it is unclear that anything of you continues at all.

U7

How does belief in judgement change the way a Christian actually lives?

Show answer

It makes present behaviour consequential. In the parable of the Sheep and the Goats, the righteous are those who fed the hungry, clothed the poor, helped the sick and visited prisoners — "what you do for the least of these, you do for me" — so charity, service and care for the needy become urgent rather than optional.

It also encourages obedience to God's commandments, forgiveness of others, confession of sin, and evangelism, since judgement is based on belief in God and acceptance of Jesus's sacrifice.

The tension to note: Christians who emphasise faith alone argue that works cannot buy salvation; those who follow James reply that "faith without works is dead", so works are the evidence of real faith.

U8

Can life have meaning without God? Give the strongest argument on each side.

Show answer

Strongest "no": without God there is no absolute, objective meaning. Nietzsche followed the point through — if God is dead, all values are baseless and reason is impotent, which is nihilism. Religious meaning is also underwritten by an afterlife, so only it can give ultimate purpose to this life.

Strongest "yes": meaning does not have to be objective to be real. Sartre argues that with no script we must choose and invent our own purpose; Aristotle grounds it in flourishing, and Bentham in the greatest good for the greatest number. Happiness is a purpose almost everyone accepts, and it does not depend on God existing.

A judgement: the "no" side wins on absolute meaning and loses on lived meaning. Most people find purpose in relationships, work and happiness regardless of belief — so a fair conclusion is that religion supplies a kind of meaning that atheism cannot, without being the only source of meaning there is.

U9

Why do two Christians reading the same Bible reach opposite conclusions on euthanasia?

Show answer

Because they start from different texts and give them different weight. One begins with "you shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13) and "the LORD gave and the LORD has taken away" (Job 1:21), and concludes that life is God's to end. The other begins with "love your neighbour as yourself" (Mark 12:31) and with Jesus relieving suffering wherever he found it, and concludes that leaving someone in agony is the less Christian act.

Because they disagree about how the Bible works. A conservative Christian reads it as giving absolute rules that hold whatever the consequences. A liberal Christian reads it as giving one absolute principle — agape — and treats the rules as generally reliable guidance about how to apply it. That is situation ethics, and it is the single biggest cause of internal Christian disagreement in this whole section.

Because the duties genuinely conflict. A Christian has a duty to protect life and a duty to relieve suffering, and in a terminal case the two point in opposite directions. Neither side is ignoring scripture; they are ranking two scriptural duties differently.

And because authority differs. Catholics read scripture through the magisterium and Natural Law, which gives a settled official answer; Protestants who hold to scripture alone, with no teaching authority above the individual conscience, end up with a range of answers instead.

U10

Why do sanctity of life and quality of life pull against each other?

Show answer

Because they locate value in different places. Sanctity of life says the worth of a life is intrinsic — it comes from God, through imago Dei (Genesis 1:27), and so it does not change when the person's health, usefulness or happiness changes. Quality of life says worth depends on the content of the life: what it is actually like to be that person now.

So they give opposite answers in the hard cases. On a sanctity view, a patient in unrelieved pain still has a life of full and equal value, so ending it is a wrong done to them. On a quality view, a life of nothing but suffering may be no benefit to the person living it, so insisting it continue is the cruelty.

Each has a real cost. Sanctity of life risks preserving suffering it could relieve, and it has to explain why an absolute rule should win when it causes harm. Quality of life risks the slippery slope: once lives are graded, there is no obvious line to stop at, and someone has to do the grading.

Can a Christian hold both? Many do, and saying how is worth the top marks. The presumption is always for life; but Christians are not obliged to preserve a body at all costs, and Catholic teaching itself permits withdrawing burdensome or futile treatment while forbidding any act intended to cause death. Good palliative care is the practical attempt to honour both at once — raise the quality of the life without ending it.

U11

Why has Christian teaching on homosexuality and same-sex marriage split the churches, rather than changing across all of them together?

Show answer

Because the churches answer two separate questions and disagree on both. First: are the biblical prohibitions (Leviticus 18:22; Romans 1:26–27) binding today, or are they culturally conditioned in the way the Bible's instructions on slavery are now taken to be? Second: what is marriage — if Jesus grounds it in male and female (Mark 10:6–9), then extending it is not a reform but a change of definition.

Because each church has a different decision-making structure. The Roman Catholic Church has a teaching authority that can hold a settled position, and Natural Law gives it a reasoned one: sex is ordered to procreation. The Quakers decide by the discernment of the meeting, which is why they moved first, in 2009. The Church of England has to carry General Synod, the bishops and a worldwide communion with it, which is why it has kept its doctrine of marriage while commending prayers of blessing from 2023.

Because the law deliberately left it to them. The Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013 included the "quadruple lock", which bars the Church of England from conducting same-sex marriages and protects other religious bodies from being compelled. So a change in civil law did not force a change in doctrine, and the churches diverged: the URC in 2016, the Scottish Episcopal Church in 2017, the Methodist Church in Britain in 2021.

The underlying point, and it is worth a mark: Christians differ because they give different weight to scripture, tradition, reason and present-day experience. Affirming Christians lead with agape and Galatians 3:28; traditional Christians lead with scripture and the created order. Note that the disagreement is live and present-tense — it is not a case of the old view giving way to a new one.

U12

Why is an annulment not simply a Catholic form of divorce?

Show answer

Because the two do opposite things. A divorce ends a marriage that existed. An annulment declares that no valid marriage ever existed — for example because one party was coerced, was deceived about something fundamental, or lacked the capacity to consent at the time of the vows.

Why the Church needs the distinction. Catholic teaching holds marriage to be a sacrament, and that in a valid, consummated sacramental marriage it is God who joins the couple: "what God has joined together, let no one separate" (Mark 10:9). If God did the joining, no human court can undo it, so a civil divorce changes the couple's legal position without ending the marriage in the eyes of the Church. Annulment does not claim to undo God's act; it examines whether the act ever took place.

The practical consequence. Without an annulment a Catholic who remarries civilly is, on the Church's teaching, still married to the first spouse — which is why remarriage has traditionally barred people from communion, though Amoris Laetitia (2016) encouraged pastoral discernment case by case.

The criticism, which belongs in a 12-marker: that annulment is a legal device producing the same outcome as divorce while allowing the Church to say it has not permitted one. The reply is that the grounds are genuinely different — an annulment turns on what was true at the wedding, a divorce on what has happened since.

U13

Why do many Christians accept AIH but reject AID and surrogacy?

Show answer

Because the objection is about who is involved, not about using medicine. In AIH both egg and sperm come from the married couple, so the child is still the fruit of their own union and nothing has entered the marriage from outside. In AID and egg donation a third party's genetic material comes into the marriage, and Catholic teaching has called this a kind of mechanical adultery. In surrogacy a third party's body is involved as well.

Three further objections stack up on the AID and surrogacy side: the child risks being treated as a commodity that has been bought, rather than received as a gift; it is said to weaken the exclusive bond of marriage; and it makes it straightforward for single people and same-sex couples to have children, which some churches regard as cutting the child's upbringing loose from marriage as they understand it.

But notice this is not the Catholic position. Catholic teaching rejects AIH too, because any technique that conceives a child apart from the marital act separates the unitive from the procreative — that is the Natural Law argument of Donum Vitae (1987). The AIH-but-not-AID line is the Protestant middle ground: medicine may help a couple to conceive, but only their own gametes may be used.

The replies, for the other side of the answer: that situation ethics makes giving a longed-for child the most loving act available, whatever the source of the gametes; that God works through science, so a donor is simply part of the means; and that donor conception is regulated in the UK, with counselling and limits, precisely to manage the risks about identity and consent.

U14

Why would a church treat celibacy as a higher calling when Genesis commands people to be fruitful?

Show answer

Because the New Testament adds something Genesis does not have. Genesis 1:28 blesses marriage and children in creation; Jesus and Paul then speak of a second, narrower calling. Jesus says some "have renounced marriage because of the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 19:12), and Paul says plainly that "it is good for a man not to touch a woman" (1 Corinthians 7:1). Jesus himself was celibate, so the church has an example as well as a text.

Because of the practical argument Paul makes. "An unmarried man is concerned about the Lord's affairs… a married man is concerned about the affairs of this world" (1 Corinthians 7:32–33). Without a spouse or children, a priest, monk or nun is free for prayer and ministry and belongs to the whole community rather than to one household — which is the Catholic case for clerical celibacy.

Note how the church limits the claim. Celibacy is treated as a gift given to some, not a rule for all: Matthew 19:12 adds "the one who can accept this should accept it", and Paul concedes that those who cannot control themselves "should marry". So it is a higher calling in the sense of a fuller renunciation, not a judgement that marriage is second-rate — marriage is still a sacrament in Catholic teaching.

The objections, which make the other half of a 12-marker: that Genesis 2:18 says it is not good to be alone; that Peter and the other apostles were married, so celibacy was never a condition of church leadership; that Protestants regard compulsory clerical celibacy as a human rule with no biblical warrant, and argue a married minister understands family life better; and that a requirement imposed rather than freely chosen can do real harm. Non-religious critics make the same distinction — they object to required celibacy, not to anyone choosing it.

Happiness is a purpose most people accept, and it plainly does not depend on God existing 1

Religion may even be damaging here: a false sense of purpose can stop people appreciating this life, and Marx called religion an opiate that distracts from real social progress 1

Christian teaching to anchor it: Aquinas held that the supreme good for a human being is not happiness but a positive relationship with God, and Augustine that we cannot save ourselves and must trust in Christ — which is precisely the kind of meaning the non-religious views cannot supply 1

Appraisal: the religious case is strong on ultimate meaning and weak as a claim about all meaning, since it has to say that billions of non-believers lead meaningless lives, which does not match what they report 1

Conclusion: the word "only" is what sinks the statement 1

Religion offers a kind of meaning — absolute, God-given and extending beyond death — that non-religious accounts cannot match, and Christians would say that is the meaning that matters; but purpose found in flourishing, love, work and the happiness of others is real meaning too, so I disagree with the statement as it stands 1

D19

Explain why Christians have different attitudes to abortion. (6)

Show answer

All Christians start from the sanctity of life: life is a gift from God, humans are made in his image (Genesis 1:27), and "you shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13) 1

Conservative Christians treat that principle as absolute. Roman Catholic teaching holds that life begins at conception, that Natural Law requires us to preserve life, and that the Catechism demands protection of life from the moment of conception — so abortion is never permissible 1

They support this from scripture that treats the unborn child as already known to God: "before I formed you in the womb I knew you" (Jeremiah 1:5) and "you knit me together in my mother's womb" (Psalm 139:13) 1

Liberal Christians give more weight to the second Christian duty, the relief of suffering, and to love as the one absolute. On situation ethics, abortion may be the most loving option — for instance after rape, or where the mother's life is at risk 1

They may also hold that the early foetus is not yet a full person, or that the decision belongs to the individual conscience before God rather than to the church 1

The Church of England sits between the two: it holds abortion to be a great moral evil, but accepts that in strictly limited circumstances it may be the lesser of two evils — which shows the disagreement is ultimately about whether sanctity of life can ever be outweighed by quality of life 1

D20

Explain Christian teachings about marriage and its purposes. (6)

Show answer

Marriage is part of God's plan in creation: "a man leaves his father and mother and is united to his wife, and they become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24), so it creates a single new unit rather than a private arrangement 1

It is God's act and therefore permanent — "what God has joined together, let no one separate" (Mark 10:9) — which is why the vows run "for better, for worse… till death us do part" 1

In the Catholic and Orthodox churches marriage is a sacrament, an outward sign of God's grace, which raises it above a legal contract; Jesus honoured marriage by attending the wedding at Cana (John 2:1–11) 1

Its purposes, as the Church of England marriage service sets them out, include the couple's mutual comfort, help and delight — marriage is a lifelong partnership, not only an institution for children 1

It is the proper place for a sexual relationship: sex unites the couple and is open to procreation, which in Catholic Natural Law teaching are two purposes that belong together 1

And it is the foundation of family life — the place for bringing up children in the faith, which is why Catholic teaching calls the family the domestic church and why marriage matters to the wider community and not only to the couple 1

D21

Explain why Christians think family life is important. (6)

Show answer

The family is part of God's design: marriage in Genesis 2:24 exists partly for the bringing up of children, so family life is how the creation ordinance is carried out 1

It is where faith is handed on — children are brought to baptism, taught to pray and raised "in the training and instruction of the Lord" (Ephesians 6:4) — which is why Catholic teaching calls the family the domestic church 1

The Decalogue itself protects it: "honour your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12) makes respect for parents a commandment, and the duty continues into their old age 1

The duty runs both ways: children owe obedience (Ephesians 6:1), and parents must not exasperate their children but bring them up patiently (Ephesians 6:4; Colossians 3:20–21) 1

Providing for relatives is treated as a test of faith — "anyone who does not provide for their relatives… has denied the faith" (1 Timothy 5:8) — so caring for the elderly and the vulnerable in the family is a religious obligation 1

And in Natural Law the family is the building block of an ordered society, which is why churches invest heavily in sustaining it through rites of passage, Sunday schools, marriage preparation and counselling 1

Arguments for: life is a sacred gift from God, so its value does not depend on its condition; humans are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27), which gives every life intrinsic worth 1

The decision belongs to God, not to us — "the LORD gave and the LORD has taken away" (Job 1:21) — and "you shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13) admits no exception for suffering 1

Catholic Natural Law requires us to preserve life, and the Catechism teaches that life must be protected from conception to the very end; abandoning that for quality of life produces what John Paul II called a culture of death 1

Practically, once lives are graded by quality there is no clear place to stop, and the people judged to have the lowest quality of life are the ones least able to defend themselves 1

Arguments against: Christians also have a duty to relieve suffering, which Jesus modelled in healing the sick and which judgement itself is measured by (Matthew 25:40) — and sanctity of life can leave someone trapped in agony 1

On situation ethics, love is the one absolute, so the most loving option may be to end a pregnancy or a life of unbearable suffering; liberal Christians read 1 Corinthians 13 this way 1

Utilitarians argue the value of a life lies in the experience it contains, so a life of unrelieved pain is not a benefit to the person living it 1

Even opponents already make quality-of-life judgements: Catholic teaching permits withdrawing treatment that is burdensome and futile, which concedes that mere biological life is not the only thing that matters 1

Christian teaching to anchor it: the Church is genuinely divided — Roman Catholic teaching treats sanctity of life as absolute from conception to natural death, while the Church of England and liberal Protestants accept that in strictly limited circumstances ending a life may be the lesser evil 1

Appraisal: the sanctity argument is strongest as a protection — it refuses to let anyone be written off — but weakest when it demands that suffering be prolonged for the sake of a principle the sufferer does not share 1

Conclusion: the two are not really rivals in most cases, since good palliative care serves both; the statement only bites in the tragic cases where they genuinely conflict 1

In those cases I would keep sanctity of life as the default, because a society that starts weighing lives is dangerous to the weakest — but I would allow the compassionate exceptions the Church of England already recognises, so I agree with the statement as a presumption rather than as an absolute 1

Arguments for: gay people are made in God's image like everyone else (Genesis 1:27), so affirming Christians argue they cannot be treated as defective 1

If love is the greatest Christian virtue (1 Corinthians 13:13) and the test is "love your neighbour as yourself" (Mark 12:31), a faithful and committed same-sex relationship meets it 1

The traditional texts are contested: Jesus says nothing about homosexuality, the Leviticus holiness code also forbids things Christians no longer observe, and Romans 1 is read in context as about idolatry and exploitation rather than committed partnerships 1

Churches have already changed their reading of scripture on slavery and on the status of women, and Galatians 3:28 is used to argue that in Christ the old categories of exclusion no longer stand; non-religiously, orientation is not a choice and Mill's harm principle finds no victim 1

Arguments against: conservative Christians hold that scripture prohibits same-sex sexual acts — Leviticus 18:22 calls it detestable, and Paul describes men and women abandoning "natural relations" (Romans 1:26–27) 1

Jesus grounds marriage in the creation of male and female and in the two becoming one flesh (Mark 10:6–9, citing Genesis 1:27 and 2:24), so sexual complementarity is taken to be part of God's design 1

On Natural Law, sex is ordered to procreation within marriage, which is why Catholic teaching calls homosexual acts intrinsically disordered while insisting that gay people themselves be accepted with respect, compassion and sensitivity (Catechism 2357–2358) 1

There is also a freedom-of-conscience argument against the word "everyone": requiring universal acceptance of any sexual ethic overrides the right of religious believers to hold a different view 1

Christian teaching to anchor it: the Church is openly split — the Roman Catholic Church and most conservative evangelicals hold the traditional teaching, while Quakers have celebrated same-sex marriages since 2009 and the Methodist Church in Britain since 2021; the Church of England keeps its doctrine of marriage but agreed prayers of blessing in 2023, and figures such as Desmond Tutu campaigned for full acceptance 1

Appraisal: the conservative case is strong on the plain sense of specific texts and weak on why those texts are binding when neighbouring ones are not; the affirming case is strong on love and on the image of God and weaker on Jesus's own appeal to male and female in Mark 10 1

Conclusion: the sticking point is "everyone". Accepting gay people — refusing to abuse, exclude or discriminate against them — is something all the churches now say they require, and the Catholic Catechism says it explicitly 1

But accepting the relationships as equivalent to marriage is a doctrinal judgement Christians are still making in good faith on both sides, so I agree that everyone should accept gay people and treat them equally in law, while holding that "everyone" cannot reasonably be made to share one theological conclusion 1

Arguments for: Jesus is explicit — "what God has joined together, let no one separate" (Mark 10:9) — so marriage is God's act and no human authority can undo it 1

The vows are unconditional: "for better, for worse, for richer, for poorer, in sickness and in health… till death us do part", made in the presence of God, so divorce breaks a promise to God as well as to a spouse 1

Catholic teaching follows this through: a valid, consummated sacramental marriage cannot be dissolved, so the Church offers annulment — a declaration that no valid marriage existed — rather than divorce 1

Divorce also has real costs beyond the couple: financial loss, disputes over children, higher welfare costs, instability for children, and damage to the family as the building block of society 1

Arguments against: the New Testament itself is not united. Matthew 19:9 allows divorce for sexual immorality, and Paul says that if an unbelieving partner leaves, the believer "is not bound" (1 Corinthians 7:15) 1

Love is the greatest commandment, and forcing someone to remain in an abusive or loveless marriage is neither loving nor protective of the children — on situation ethics, divorce can be the most loving option 1

Most churches accept this in practice: the Church of England has since 2002 allowed priests to marry a divorced person in church at their discretion, Methodists permit remarriage, and the Orthodox churches allow it with a more penitential service 1

Non-religiously, divorce is regrettable but sometimes necessary, the law allows it, and the children's interests are better served by ending a destructive marriage than by preserving its form 1

Christian teaching to anchor it: the churches' divergence follows the three strands in scripture — Mark 10 for no divorce, Matthew 19 for adultery as a ground, 1 Corinthians 7 for the most flexible position — and the Catholic alternative is annulment rather than permission 1

Appraisal: the "never" position honours the vow and protects the weaker partner from being discarded, but it struggles with abuse and desertion, and it makes the Church look as though it values an institution more than the people inside it; the permissive position is compassionate but risks treating lifelong vows as provisional 1

Conclusion: "never" is too strong even by the Bible's own standards, since Jesus himself allows an exception in Matthew 19:9 and Paul allows another 1

So I disagree with the statement: religious people should treat marriage as lifelong and enter it expecting never to divorce, and a church is right to make that the teaching — but where a marriage has become harmful, the Anglican and Orthodox approach of compassion with seriousness seems more faithful to Christian love than an absolute prohibition 1
