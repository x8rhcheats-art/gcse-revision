01How it actually works

Most Christian celebrations mark key events in the lives of Jesus and his earliest followers. They let Christians focus on the fundamental beliefs of their religion and worship God as a community. Denominations differ in how many they keep: Roman Catholic and Orthodox Christians have a very full calendar, with saints' days as well as feast days, while most Protestants concentrate on the three major feasts — Christmas, Easter and Pentecost. Pilgrimage, by contrast, is not an obligation in Christianity, and some Christians object to it outright — which is what makes it such reliable 10-mark material.

Advent — preparing for Christmas

Spec 2.1 · "How they are observed · The worship, rituals and traditions associated with them"

  • Advent — from adventum, "coming"; the fourth Sunday before Christmas to Christmas Eve.
  • A season of preparation — expectation, prayer, fasting and penitence, looking back to the incarnation and forward to Jesus' return as judge.
  • The church is kept sombre — few decorations, so nothing distracts from contemplation.
  • The Advent crown — four candles lit one per Sunday, so the light grows as the wait shortens: Jesus the light of the world.
  • Christingle — orange the world, candle Jesus, red ribbon his suffering, fruit the fruits of creation.
More detail

Advent takes its name from adventum, meaning "coming" or "arrival", and runs from the fourth Sunday before Christmas to Christmas Eve. It is a time when Christians reflect on why God became incarnate, and also look forward to Jesus' return as judge and king — so it is a season of expectation, preparation and prayer, traditionally of fasting and penitence.

Because it is a season of preparation, the church building is kept deliberately sombre, with few decorations to distract from contemplation. Light is the controlling symbol, and it appears in two rituals:

  • The Advent crown or wreath — a circle of holly and ivy around four candles. One is lit on the first Sunday, two on the second, and so on, so that as the wait shortens the light grows. It points to Jesus as the light of the world.
  • Christingle services, for children. Each child is given an orange with a red ribbon round it, a candle set in the top and four cocktail sticks of fruit or sweets around it. The priest explains the symbolism: the orange is the world, the lit candle is Jesus, the light of the world sent to save it from sin, the red ribbon is his suffering, and the fruit is the fruits of the natural world provided by God.

Christmas — how it is celebrated

Spec 2.1 · "The celebration… of the festivals of Christmas and Easter"

  • 25 December was chosen, not recorded — set by Pope Julius I to Christianise the Roman festival of Saturnalia.
  • Orthodox Christians keep 7 January — twelve days later, because they use the Julian calendar.
  • Midnight Mass — a Eucharist from 11:30pm; the lights are raised after midnight as the light of the world enters the cosmos.
  • The crib — the infant laid in the manger on Christmas Eve, the wise men replacing the shepherds at Epiphany.
  • Nativity plays and carol singing — the story learnt and passed on, and the message taken house to house for charity.
  • Presents and cards — the gifts of the wise men, and Jesus himself as God's gift to the world.
More detail

The Gospels do not give the date of Jesus' birth; 25 December was chosen in the fourth century by Pope Julius I to Christianise the Pagan celebrations already held then — it was the date of the Roman festival of Saturnalia. By 529 AD it was a civil holiday. Orthodox Christians use the Julian calendar and so celebrate on 7 January, twelve days later. (Oliver Cromwell banned the celebration in the 1640s for encouraging "liberty to carnal and sensual delights" — even Christmas puddings.)

Why Christmas matters to Christians

Spec 2.1 · "Their importance and significance to the followers of the religion"

  • Christmas celebrates the incarnation — the moment God became human, which is the point every answer must reach.
  • Joy, hope and thanksgiving — God's love, the light sent to save the world, and gratitude for the gift.
  • The coming of the Messiah — and so the beginning of salvation.
  • Born in a stable — God identifies with the poor and the marginalised, which is why Christmas is a time for charity.
  • "Prince of peace" — the Christmas truce of 1914 is the stock example.
  • Sadness inside the joy — Christians know Jesus was born in order to die; saying so lifts a 6-marker.

Christmas celebrates the incarnation — the moment God became human — and the importance of that is two-fold: as a man Jesus showed how people should behave, and as God he revealed God's own nature, above all his omnibenevolence.

  • It is a time of joy: a reminder of God's love and his desire to rebuild the relationship humanity had broken by sin.
  • It is a time of hope: Jesus, the light of the world, was sent to save it from sin, and also illuminates how humanity should behave.
  • It is a time of thanksgiving: Christians express gratitude for the gift of the incarnation.
  • It marks the coming of the Messiah and the beginning of salvation.
  • Jesus was born in a stable, which shows God identifies with the poor and marginalised.
  • It is a time for charity, like the gifts of the wise men, and a time when families come together.
  • It celebrates peace — Jesus as "prince of peace". The Christmas truce of 1914 is the stock example.

The other Christmas 10-marker is "The festival of Christmas has lost its religious meaning". Both columns need Christian content, because the interesting version of the argument happens between Christians.

Lent and Shrove Tuesday — preparing for Easter

Spec 2.1 · "The history of the festivals · How they are observed"

  • Lent — forty days of fasting and penitence preparing for Easter, the most important festival of the year.
  • What is given up — Catholics typically a luxury such as chocolate; Orthodox Christians meat and dairy.
  • Shrove Tuesday — forbidden foods eaten up, and "shriven" means forgiven: Christians confessed before Lent began.
  • Ash Wednesday — last year's palm crosses burnt and the ash marked as a cross, a sign of humility.
  • Jesus' forty days in the wilderness — recalled in the service, which is why many join Bible study groups in Lent.
More detail

Easter is the most important festival in the Christian calendar, so Christians spend forty days preparing for it during Lent — a time of fasting and penitence. Roman Catholics typically give up a luxury such as chocolate; Orthodox Christians refrain from meat and dairy.

Shrove Tuesday — Pancake Day — is the day before Lent begins. It comes from the era of strict fasting, when all forbidden foods had to be eaten up; "shrove" comes from "shriven", meaning forgiven, because Christians attended church that day to confess their sins before Lent.

Ash Wednesday is the first day of Lent. Many Christians attend a special Eucharist at which last year's palm crosses are burnt and the ashes used to mark the sign of the cross on worshippers' foreheads. The ash is a sign of humility and of a focus on spiritual rather than worldly concerns, and a reminder to reflect on Jesus' sacrifice. During the service Christians are reminded of Jesus' forty days of fasting in the wilderness before his ministry began, and the priest urges them to use Lent to work out how they can best serve God — which is why many join Bible study groups during it.

Holy Week

Spec 2.1 · "The worship, rituals and traditions associated with them"

  • Palm Sunday — the triumphal entry on a donkey; a procession, and blessed palm crosses given out.
  • Maundy Thursday — the Last Supper, the washing of feet and the institution of the Eucharist; Maundy Money.
  • Good Friday — the crucifixion; altars stripped bare, the Passion read, three-hour services and processions of witness.
  • Easter Saturday — Jesus in the tomb, marked by an all-night vigil.
  • Easter Day — the resurrection; churches redecorated in gold and flowers, with Communion and joyful hymns.
  • Learn them in order — "state three key days in Holy Week" is a real question, with the ritual attached.
More detail

The last week of Lent is Holy Week, and each day re-enacts a stage of Jesus' last week. "State three key days in Holy Week" is a real question, so learn them in order with their ritual attached.

Easter Sunday — how it is celebrated

Spec 2.1 · "The celebration… of the festivals of Christmas and Easter"

  • Easter Sunday — the culmination of Holy Week: the resurrection and victory over sin and death.
  • The Easter Vigil — begins outside in darkness; the paschal candle is carried in and Christ is proclaimed risen three times.
  • The light spreads — every candle lit from the paschal candle, symbolising the news of the resurrection reaching the world.
  • The paschal candle — a cross, five grains of incense for the five wounds, and alpha and omega.
  • The Easter garden — the stone over the tomb on Saturday, rolled back on Sunday to show it empty.
  • Easter eggs — life emerges from something that looks inanimate, as joy came out of the disciples' despair.
More detail

Easter Sunday is the culmination of Holy Week: it celebrates the resurrection of Jesus and his victory over the powers of sin and death.

  • The Easter Vigil. On Easter Saturday Christians gather at about 11:30pm. The service begins outside, in darkness. The priest lights a small fire, lights a blessed paschal candle from it, carries it into the church and announces three times that Christ is risen. The congregation's candles are lit from it so the church slowly fills with light — symbolising the spread of the news of the resurrection throughout the world. The liturgies of Word and Sacrament follow.
  • The paschal candle carries a cross for the crucifixion, five pieces of incense for the five wounds of Christ, and the Greek letters alpha and omega to show Christ is the beginning and the end.
  • The Easter garden — a model of the tomb. On Easter Saturday a flat round stone covers the entrance, recalling Jesus' death and burial; on Easter Sunday it is rolled back to show the grave is empty.
  • Easter eggs. An egg looks inanimate, but life emerges from it. So too the disciples' despair at Jesus' death turned to joy at his resurrection.

Why Easter matters to Christians

Spec 2.1 · "Their importance and significance to the followers of the religion"

  • The most important festival of the year — without the resurrection Christianity would not exist.
  • Triumph over sin and death — and of good over evil, light over darkness.
  • Proof of Jesus' identity — the Son of God incarnate and the Messiah, so everything he taught is vindicated.
  • Atonement — it recognises Jesus' sacrifice and God's love in sending his Son to redeem the world.
  • A promise for believers — Christ is "the first fruits", so death does not end a Christian's existence.
  • The preparation teaches too — Lent the value of fasting, Maundy Thursday service, sacrifice and the Eucharist.

The verse that settles "why Easter?"

"Now if Christ is proclaimed as raised from the dead, how can some of you say there is no resurrection of the dead? If there is no resurrection of the dead, then Christ has not been raised; and if Christ has not been raised, then our proclamation has been in vain and your faith has been in vain."

1 Corinthians 15:12–15

St Paul makes the resurrection the load-bearing claim of the whole religion. Used to argue Easter outranks every other festival, including Christmas — and in a 10-marker, to appraise the alternative view rather than just state it.

  • It celebrates the justice and mercy of God.

What a pilgrimage is, and why Christians go

Spec 2.2 · "Places of pilgrimage including reasons for their significance to the followers of the religion"

  • A pilgrimage — a sacred journey for spiritual reasons, God encountered through the places, people and situations met.
  • The journey mirrors an inner one — life itself as a pilgrimage towards heaven, so it can be purely inward.
  • Christians are not obliged to go — which is what makes pilgrimage reliable 10-mark material.
  • Closeness and discipline — the places where Jesus lived, and the sacrifice of the journey itself shown to God.
  • Spiritual growth and healing — time to pray without distraction, and some sites are associated with cures.
  • Community — many sites are ecumenical centres, and the Bible is brought to life.
More detail

A pilgrimage is a sacred journey undertaken for spiritual reasons, in which God is encountered through the places, people and situations the pilgrim meets. The physical journey mirrors an inner one — the idea of life itself as a pilgrimage towards heaven — so "pilgrimage" can also mean a purely inward journey.

Christians are not obliged to go. Those who do, go for these reasons:

  • It helps them feel closer to God. Visiting the places where Jesus lived, taught and died reminds them how God intervened to sustain his creation.
  • It is a form of spiritual discipline: completing the journey and spending the day in prayer shows God how important he is to them, through sacrifice and hardship.
  • It enables spiritual growth — time to reflect, pray and worship without mundane distractions, in places pervaded by awe and holiness.
  • Some sites are associated with healing, so pilgrims go to ask for a cure.
  • It provides a sense of community; many sites are ecumenical centres welcoming Christians of every denomination.
  • It brings the Bible to life, and makes pilgrims more aware of their limited time on earth, which helps them set priorities.

The history of Christian pilgrimage, and the Protestant objection

Spec 2.2 · "The traditions and observances of pilgrims at them"

  • Jesus went on pilgrimage — to Jerusalem as a child, for Passover (Luke 2:41–43).
  • The Holy Land — travel began after Constantine's mother went there, and grew once Christianity was legal.
  • Martyrs' tombs and relics — displayed in reliquaries, where pilgrims asked the saints to intercede with God.
  • The Middle Ages — journeys of months or years for blessing, thanksgiving, healing or penance.
  • The Protestant objection — venerating relics was feared to break the second commandment against idols.
  • Revived for Catholics — in the nineteenth century, because of St Bernadette of Lourdes.

Luther against pilgrimage

"All pilgrimages should be stopped. There is no good in them: no commandment enjoins them, no obedience attaches to them. Rather do these pilgrimages give countless occasions to commit sin and to despise God's commandments."

Martin Luther, The Christian Nobility, 1520

The single best quotation for the "against" half of any pilgrimage 10-marker: it makes the objection a Christian objection, not a sceptic's.

More detail

Pilgrimage has a history Christians argue about, and knowing it is what makes a 10-marker on pilgrimage more than opinion.

  1. Jesus himself went on pilgrimage to Jerusalem as a child, for Passover (Luke 2:41–43).
  2. Christians began travelling to the Holy Land after the Emperor Constantine's mother went there on converting — and it became popular once Christianity was legal in the Roman Empire.
  3. Before that, persecuted Christians became martyrs and were later canonised; their tombs became pilgrimage sites, where Christians prayed to the saints to intercede with God for them. Their relics — preserved bones or skin — were displayed in reliquaries in shrines.
  4. In the Middle Ages, European pilgrims made the journey on foot, sometimes taking years, for spiritual blessings, to give thanks, for healing, or as a penance.
  5. The Protestant Reformers opposed pilgrimage, especially to relic sites, fearing that venerating relics broke the second commandment against idols.
  6. For Roman Catholics it revived in the nineteenth century because of St Bernadette of Lourdes.

Bethlehem

Spec 2.2 · "Bethlehem, Jerusalem and any one other place of significance"

  • Bethlehem — five and a half miles from Jerusalem, the birthplace of Jesus and the city of King David.
  • The Church of the Nativity — built around 338 AD by Constantine and Helena, run jointly by Catholics and two Orthodox churches.
  • The door of humility — so low that every pilgrim bows to the Christ child on entering.
  • The Grotto of the Nativity — a fourteen-pointed star marks the birthplace; the Chapel of the Manger is beside it.
  • Shepherds' Fields and the Milk Grotto — a church shaped like a shepherd's tent, and a cave linked to fertility.
  • A war zone — pilgrims also gain an understanding of the plight of Palestinians, under occupation as Israel was then.
More detail

Bethlehem lies about five and a half miles west of Jerusalem and is believed by Christians to be the place where Jesus was born; it is also the city of King David. It has been a pilgrimage site for nearly two thousand years — St Justin Martyr, writing in the second century, said that anyone wanting proof of Christ's birth should go to Bethlehem and see the cave and the manger for himself.

  • The Church of the Nativity, in Manger Square, is the most important site. It was built in the fourth century, commissioned by Constantine the Great and his mother Helena around 338 AD, and is administered jointly by the Roman Catholic Church and the Greek and Armenian Orthodox Churches — itself a sign of pilgrimage's ecumenical character.
  • Pilgrims enter through a very low door, the door of humility. It was originally built to stop thieves plundering the church, but the effect is that every pilgrim automatically bows to the Christ child.
  • Inside is the Grotto of the Nativity, containing a fourteen-pointed star believed to mark the spot where Jesus was born. Pilgrims pray here and listen again to the nativity story.
  • Next to it is the Chapel of the Manger, where Mary is believed to have laid Jesus after his birth.
  • Shepherds' Fields, nearby, is where the angel told the shepherds to go to Bethlehem. The church there is designed to look like a shepherd's tent.
  • The Milk Grotto — a cave with a Franciscan chapel above it, where tradition holds the Holy Family sheltered during the Slaughter of the Innocents, before the flight into Egypt. A drop of Mary's milk is said to have fallen and turned the ground white. Both Christians and Muslims believe scrapings from the stones increase a mother's milk and aid fertility: mothers mix it into drinking water, would-be mothers put the rock under the mattress.
  • On Christmas Eve there are processions of pilgrims from Manger Square to the church, and many Christians time their pilgrimage for Christmas to reflect on the incarnation.

What pilgrims take away is partly the same as Jerusalem — the Bible brought to life, closeness to God, fellowship — but Bethlehem adds something specific: it is in a war zone, so pilgrims gain an appreciation of Middle Eastern politics and an understanding of the plight of Palestinians. Some groups visit hospitals, orphanages or Palestinian Christian churches and form charitable links. That also brings the nativity story closer: Israel at the time of Jesus was under occupation too.

Jerusalem

Spec 2.2 · "Bethlehem, Jerusalem and any one other place of significance"

  • Jerusalem — where Jesus was tried, executed, buried and raised; holy to Jews and Muslims too.
  • The Cenacle — thought to be the Upper Room where the Eucharist was instituted.
  • The Mount of Olives — the Church of All Nations, where he prayed before his arrest in Gethsemane.
  • The Church of the Holy Sepulchre — the site of the crucifixion and burial, though some prefer the Garden Tomb.
  • The Via Dolorosa — the route of the cross, walked at the Stations of the Cross, especially on Good Friday.
  • What pilgrims gain — the Bible brought to life, closeness to God, empathy with his suffering, fellowship and a challenge.

Jerusalem is where Jesus spent his last days: he entered the city on a donkey the Sunday before Passover, celebrated the festival with his disciples, prayed on the Mount of Olives, was arrested in Gethsemane, tried, executed and buried outside the city walls — and rose again. It is a holy city for Jews, Muslims and Christians, and is also associated with Old Testament figures such as David. Pilgrims go to spend time in prayer and contemplation, trying to understand the suffering Jesus endured and the price of his sacrifice.

Lourdes is the alternative: a town at the foot of the Pyrenees where in 1858 a fourteen-year-old girl, Bernadette Soubirous, had repeated visions of the Virgin Mary in the grotto of Massabielle — dressed in white with a blue sash and a yellow rose on each foot. Mary told her to drink at the spring beneath her feet and to build a chapel on the spot. A village woman gave the water to her sick baby, who recovered, establishing the link with healing. Pilgrims — mostly Roman Catholic — pray at the grotto and in the Sanctuary of Our Lady of Lourdes, drink or bathe in the water, join the nightly Torchlight Marian Procession before Mass in the grotto, and confess their sins at the Reconciliation Chapel.

More detail

Taizé is the answer to "explain the importance of pilgrimage to one place other than Bethlehem or Jerusalem" — and it works because it is modern, ecumenical and about young people, so it contrasts cleanly with the two Holy Land sites.

In 1940, Roger Louis Schutz-Marsauche — Brother Roger — set up a safe house for Jews escaping the Nazis at Taizé in south-east France. After the war he returned and founded an ecumenical monastic order dedicated to a simple life and to reconciliation, in view of the distress of the Second World War. About a hundred Roman Catholic and Protestant monks belong, dividing their time between Taizé and poor areas of Asia, Africa and South America; they earn their own keep and give the rest of their time to the needy.

Tens of thousands of young people visit each year. They camp in the fields around the church and monastery, and during their stay they:

  • Join the life of the monks — morning prayer, group discussions, midday prayer, song practice, workshops and evening prayer, with collective worship three times a day.
  • Study the Bible, meet in discussion groups on subjects such as science and religion, ecology and Genesis, or caring for the disabled — and help with the daily chores.
  • Some choose to spend their day in complete silence.
  • Worship in the famous Taizé style: simple phrases, usually from the Psalms, set to music and repeated as a prayer chant — designed to aid prayer and meditation, and simple enough that everyone can join in whatever their language.

What they gain: fellowship and friendships from around the world; a chance to explore and deepen their faith; peace; time to listen to God; and an understanding of the relevance of Christianity to modern life. All are welcome regardless of religious affiliation, and visitors are encouraged to live the Gospels in a spirit of joy, simplicity and reconciliation.

02Cheat sheet

The must-knows

Christmas = the incarnation, God becoming human. 25 December was fixed by Pope Julius I over the Roman Saturnalia; Orthodox Christians keep 7 January.

Easter = the resurrection, and the most important festival. Without it, Christianity would not exist (1 Corinthians 15).

Holy Week, in order: Palm Sunday · Maundy Thursday · Good Friday · Easter Saturday · Easter Day.

Symbols of light: Advent crown, Christingle candle, Midnight Mass lights, paschal candle — all point to Jesus as light of the world.

Three pilgrimage sites: Bethlehem (nativity), Jerusalem (passion and resurrection), Taizé (reconciliation and youth) — or Lourdes (Mary and healing).

The objection to pilgrimage is Christian: God is omnipresent · relics risk idolatry · the money could go to the poor · Luther, 1520.

The 10-mark frame

  • Restate the statement and name the comparison it forces — Easter against Christmas, pilgrimage against prayer at home, Bethlehem against Jerusalem. Say in the first line what would settle it.
  • The strongest case for, with Christian teaching and scripture attached: 1 Corinthians 15:12–15 for Easter, the incarnation for Christmas, Luke 2:41–43 for pilgrimage, the Church of the Nativity and the Grotto for Bethlehem.
  • The strongest case against, also with Christian teaching rather than bare scepticism: without the incarnation there is nothing to resurrect; God is omnipresent; Luther in 1520; Matthew 25:31–46, where the money would do more good given to the poor.
  • Where Christians disagree with each other — the full Catholic and Orthodox calendar against the three Protestant feasts; 25 December against the Orthodox 7 January; the Reformers against relics and shrines; the Church of the Holy Sepulchre against the Garden Tomb.
  • A conclusion that decides, naming the reason that decided it and giving your own opinion. "Both sides have a point" is not a conclusion.

The sentence starters that earn the evaluation marks: "Most Christians would argue…" · "However, Protestant Christians have objected since the Reformation that…" · "An Orthodox Christian would put it differently, because…" · "This argument carries less weight, because…" · "On balance, I agree that… because…"

03Understanding it

U1

Why do Christians celebrate Christmas on a date the Gospels never give, and does the Pagan origin of the date undermine the festival?

Show answer

25 December was fixed in the fourth century by Pope Julius I precisely because a Pagan festival, Saturnalia, already fell then — the aim was to Christianise an existing celebration. By 529 AD it was a civil holiday. Orthodox Christians, using the Julian calendar, keep 7 January instead.

Christians generally argue it does not undermine anything, because what is celebrated is the incarnation, not an anniversary. The date is a convenience; the claim that God became human is the content.

The sharper version of the objection is that the Pagan inheritance explains the feasting and the excess — which is why Cromwell banned the festival in the 1640s for encouraging "liberty to carnal and sensual delights". That is useful in a 10-marker on Christmas losing its religious meaning: the commercial side is not a modern invention.

U2

Christmas is a time of joy. Why do Christians also describe an element of sadness in it?

Show answer

Because they know why Jesus was born. The incarnation was God's move to rebuild the relationship humanity had broken by sin — and the way it was rebuilt was the crucifixion. So the birth already points to the death.

The nativity story says so itself: the magi's myrrh, a burial spice, sits among the gifts alongside gold for authority and frankincense for spirituality.

U3

Why is light the controlling symbol of both Christmas and Easter, and what is the difference in what it means?

Show answer

Both festivals use growing light out of darkness, because both celebrate Jesus as the light of the world. The ritual shape is almost identical: a service beginning in darkness, a candle, and light spreading through the congregation.

At Christmas the light means arrival. The Advent crown's candles increase week by week as the wait shortens; the Christingle candle in the orange is Jesus sent into the world; Midnight Mass starts dimly lit and brightens after midnight because the light of the world has entered the cosmos.

At Easter the light means victory and news. The paschal candle is lit outside in the dark and carried in as the priest announces three times that Christ is risen; the congregation's candles are lit from it so the church fills with light — the spread of the resurrection news throughout the world, and the triumph of light over darkness.

U4

Why is Good Friday called "good" when it marks an execution?

Show answer

Because of what the death achieved: it recalls the sacrifice Jesus made for people's sins. Christians believe the crucifixion brought atonement, so the day is good in its effect, not in its events.

The worship does not pretend otherwise. It is the most sombre day of the year: churches are stripped bare of decorations and statues to recall Jesus' abandonment and remove distractions, the Passion is read or sung, and some Christians attend a three-hour service of sermons from noon to 3pm, or walk a procession of witness carrying a cross.

U5

What is the strongest objection to Christian pilgrimage, and why does it carry more weight coming from a Christian than from an atheist?

Show answer

The strongest objection is God's omnipresence: if God is everywhere, no place is closer to him than any other, so travelling to one is at best unnecessary and at worst a misunderstanding of what God is. Protestants add that veneration of relics and Marian shrines risks breaking the second commandment against idols, and that the money would be better spent on the needy — Jesus said that providing for them is providing for him (Matthew 25:31–46).

It carries more weight from inside Christianity because it cannot be dismissed as unbelief. Luther is the clinching citation: "no commandment enjoins them, no obedience attaches to them" (1520). An atheist saying pilgrimage is pointless is only saying religion is pointless; Luther is saying pilgrimage is bad theology.

U6

Both Bethlehem and Jerusalem are Holy Land sites tied to Jesus' life. What does a pilgrim get from Bethlehem that Jerusalem does not give?

Show answer

Much is shared: the Bible brought to life, closeness to God, fellowship. Bethlehem adds two things.

The incarnation rather than the passion. Jerusalem is about suffering, sacrifice and resurrection; Bethlehem is about God entering the world as a poor child. The door of humility makes the point physically — every pilgrim has to bow to get in.

The politics. Bethlehem is in a war zone, so pilgrims gain an appreciation of Middle Eastern politics and an understanding of the plight of Palestinians; some groups form charitable links with hospitals, orphanages or Palestinian Christian churches. That loops back into the nativity, because Israel in Jesus' day was under occupation too.

U7

Why has Taizé become a pilgrimage site for young people when it has no Biblical events, no relics and no miracles?

Show answer

Because its significance is what happens there, not what happened there. Brother Roger founded it in 1940 as a safe house for Jews escaping the Nazis and refounded it after the war as an ecumenical community dedicated to a simple life and reconciliation. Roman Catholic and Protestant monks live under one rule, which is itself the message.

Practically, it suits young pilgrims: they camp, join the monks' day of prayer, chores and discussion, and worship in the Taizé chant — short phrases from the Psalms, repeated, simple enough for anyone to join whatever their language. Some spend the day in silence. All are welcome regardless of affiliation.

Why that matters for the exam: Taizé neatly dodges the Protestant objections. There are no relics to venerate and no shrine to a saint, so the idolatry charge does not land — which makes it the safest choice for "one place other than Bethlehem or Jerusalem".

U8

"Without festivals, Christianity would die out." How would you argue this both ways?

Show answer

For. Festivals are how the beliefs get transmitted and rehearsed: nativity plays and Sunday school teach children the story; Holy Week walks the congregation through the passion year after year; the readings, carols and liturgy carry the doctrine. They also build the community — Christmas and Easter are when attendance is highest, and Christmas is a day of obligation for Catholics. Without the calendar, belief would be private, unrehearsed and unrepeated.

Against. What is essential is the belief, not the celebration. The early Church had no fixed Christmas at all — 25 December was a fourth-century decision. Quakers keep no festival calendar and are unquestionably Christian, and Cromwell banned Christmas without Christianity collapsing. A Christian is sustained by Scripture, prayer and the Eucharist, which are weekly or daily rather than annual.

Conclusion that will score: festivals are not the foundation — Christianity could survive without them, as some denominations show — but they are the main mechanism by which it is passed on, so losing them would weaken it severely without killing it.

04Exam drill

How to answer each part — Paper 2

  • 4 marks — knowledge only. Two pieces of information, each with "a little development". If an example is asked for, give the example first and then its significance. Point + development = 2 marks each.
  • 6 marks — explain the significance of a person, event, belief or practice. Level marked, not point marked: two or three points explained in depth, drawing out the significance. An answer that only describes cannot reach the top level.
  • 10 marks — the evaluation. Draw on a number of viewpoints within Christianity, appraise the quality of each argument as you make it, consider both perspectives, give your own opinion with reasoned arguments, and finish with a balanced, justified conclusion.

Paper 2 questions come in threes: a 4-mark outline, a 6-mark explanation and a 10-mark evaluation. The 6s and 10s are level marked, not point marked, so the mark points below are a target for coverage rather than a tick list.

D1

Outline two ways Christians celebrate Christmas. (4)

Show answer

1. Many Christians attend Midnight Mass 1; the service begins about 11:30pm on Christmas Eve with readings, prayers and carols about the birth of Jesus, and the church is lit more brightly after midnight to symbolise the light of the world entering the cosmos 1

2. Many churches display a crib 1; it is a model of the stable with Mary, Joseph, the shepherds and the animals, and the figure of the infant Jesus is placed in the manger on Christmas Eve 1

Other acceptable pairs: nativity plays, carol singing from house to house, exchanging presents and cards recalling the gifts of the magi.

D2

Outline two reasons why a Christian might not go on pilgrimage. (4)

Show answer

1. God is omnipresent 1; so there is no need to visit a special place to encounter him, and a Christian could strengthen their faith just as well through Bible study and prayer at home 1

2. The money could be used to help those in need 1; Jesus said in the Parable of the Sheep and the Goats that providing for the needy is providing for him, so charity may be a better way of encountering God 1

Also acceptable: sites dedicated to Mary and the saints, and the veneration of relics, risk idolatry — breaking the second commandment, which is why the Protestant Reformers opposed pilgrimage.

D3

Outline two practices performed on a Christian pilgrimage to Bethlehem. (4)

Show answer

1. Pilgrims enter the Church of the Nativity through the door of humility 1; the door is so low that every pilgrim automatically bows to the Christ child 1

2. They pray at the Grotto of the Nativity 1; it contains a fourteen-pointed star marking the spot where Jesus is believed to have been born, and pilgrims listen again to the nativity story there 1

Also acceptable: joining the Christmas Eve procession from Manger Square; visiting Shepherds' Fields; taking scrapings from the Milk Grotto.

D4

Outline two features of a Christian pilgrimage to Jerusalem. (4)

Show answer

1. Pilgrims walk the Via Dolorosa 1; it is believed to be the route Jesus carried his cross to Golgotha, and pilgrims say prayers at the Stations of the Cross along it, often on Good Friday 1

2. They visit the Church of the Holy Sepulchre 1; many Christians believe it stands on the site of the crucifixion and burial, and they pray at the slab where Jesus' body was laid out 1

Also acceptable: the Cenacle (the Upper Room); the Garden of Gethsemane and the Church of All Nations on the Mount of Olives; the Church of the Ascension.

D5

Explain how and why Advent is observed by Christians. (6)

Show answer

Level marked — two or three developed points.

What it is. Advent, from adventum meaning "coming", begins on the fourth Sunday before Christmas and ends on Christmas Eve 1; it is a season of preparation, expectation and prayer, traditionally of fasting and penitence, so churches are kept sombre with few decorations to avoid distracting from contemplation 1

Why. Christians use it to reflect on the reasons why God became incarnate 1; it is also a time to think of Jesus' promised return as judge and king, so the preparation looks forward as well as back 1

How — the symbolism of light. An Advent crown of holly and ivy holds four candles, one lit on the first Sunday, two on the second and so on, so the light grows as the wait shortens — pointing to Jesus as the light of the world 1; at Christingle services children are given an orange representing the world, with a lit candle for Jesus, a red ribbon for his suffering and fruit on cocktail sticks for the fruits of creation, so that the purpose of Christmas is explained through the object itself 1

D6

Explain why Easter is significant for Christians. (6)

Show answer

Level marked — two or three developed points.

First point — it is the foundation of the religion. Easter commemorates Jesus' sacrifice on the cross and celebrates his resurrection 1; St Paul makes it load-bearing: if Christ has not been raised, "our proclamation has been in vain and your faith has been in vain" (1 Corinthians 15), so without the resurrection Christianity would not exist 1

Second point — it proves who Jesus was and defeats death. The resurrection is proof of Jesus' identity as the Son of God incarnate, and shows God's triumph over the powers of sin and death — the victory of good over evil and light over darkness 1; Christians believe his sacrifice provides them with atonement with God, demonstrating God's justice and mercy 1

Third point — it promises believers their own resurrection. Paul calls Christ "the first fruits", so Christians believe they too will be raised at the End Time and may experience the Kingdom of God 1; the preparation carries its own lessons — Lent the value of fasting and reflection, Maundy Thursday the significance of service and the institution of the Eucharist 1

D7

Explain the purpose of pilgrimage for Christians. (6)

Show answer

Level marked — two or three developed points.

First point — closeness to God and the Bible. A pilgrimage is a sacred journey in which God is encountered through the places, people and situations met 1; going to the places where Jesus lived, taught and died reminds Christians how God intervened in history and brings the Bible to life, and Jesus himself went on pilgrimage to Jerusalem as a child (Luke 2:41–43) 1

Second point — spiritual discipline and growth. By completing the journey and spending the day in prayer and reflection, Christians show God how important he is to them, and the sacrifice and hardship involved are themselves a discipline 1; freed from mundane concerns, and in places pervaded by awe and holiness, they have the chance to develop spiritually — the physical journey mirroring the inner journey of life towards heaven 1

Third point — healing and community. Some sites are associated with healing, such as Lourdes, so pilgrims go to ask for a cure 1; many are ecumenical centres welcoming Christians of every denomination, so pilgrimage also gives a sense of community and fellowship 1

D8

Explain the importance for Christians of pilgrimage to one place other than Bethlehem or Jerusalem. (6)

Show answer

Level marked. Name the place in the first line — here, Taizé.

What it is and why it exists. Taizé is an ecumenical monastic community in south-east France, founded in 1940 by Roger Louis Schutz-Marsauche — Brother Roger — who first set up a safe house for Jews escaping the Nazis 1; after the war he refounded it as a community dedicated to simplicity and reconciliation, and about a hundred Roman Catholic and Protestant monks live under one rule, dividing their time between Taizé and poor areas of Asia, Africa and South America 1

What pilgrims do. Tens of thousands of young people camp there each year and join the monks' day — morning, midday and evening prayer, Bible study, discussion groups, song practice and daily chores 1; worship uses the distinctive Taizé chant, short phrases from the Psalms repeated as prayer, simple enough for anyone to join whatever their language, and some visitors spend the whole day in silence 1

Why it matters. It offers fellowship with Christians from around the world, peace, time to listen to God and the chance to deepen a faith by exploring it rather than being told it 1; because it is ecumenical and open to all regardless of affiliation, it embodies the reconciliation it was founded for — and because it holds no relics or shrines, it avoids the idolatry that makes many Protestants wary of pilgrimage 1

D9

"Easter is the most important Christian festival."

Discuss this statement, considering the arguments for and against. In your answer you should include: reference to teachings; other (divergent) points of view, either within the religion or from other religions; your opinion using reasoned arguments; and a balanced conclusion. (10)

Show answer

Level marked in the real paper, so treat this as a plan for coverage rather than a tick list.

Arguments for

Easter celebrates the resurrection, which St Paul makes the load-bearing claim of the religion: if Christ has not been raised, "our proclamation has been in vain and your faith has been in vain" (1 Corinthians 15) 1

It is the proof of Jesus' identity as the Son of God incarnate, and the demonstration of God's triumph over the powers of sin and death 1

Christian practice agrees: Easter gets the longest preparation of any festival — forty days of Lent plus the whole of Holy Week — which is itself evidence of how the Church ranks it 1

Arguments against

Without Christmas there is no Easter: the incarnation is the moment God entered the world, and nothing else could follow without it 1

Christmas reaches further. It is a day of obligation, it draws the largest congregations, and through nativity plays, carols and cards it carries the Christian message to people who never attend church 1

Other Christians would reject the ranking altogether: Protestants keep three major feasts — Christmas, Easter and Pentecost — and Pentecost founded the Church, while Quakers keep no festival calendar at all and would say no festival is essential 1

Christian teaching to appraise with

The "without Christmas there is no Easter" argument is weaker than it looks: it establishes that the birth is necessary, not that it is more significant — and the nativity itself points forward to the death through the magi's myrrh 1

Christmas's wide reach is partly cultural rather than religious, which is exactly the complaint behind "Christmas has lost its religious meaning" — Easter has been commercialised far less 1

Conclusion

Both festivals celebrate parts of one claim — that God became human in order to save the world — so ranking them is slightly artificial, and Christians differ on it 1

But Paul's argument decides it for me: Christianity could survive a world that had forgotten the stable, and could not survive an occupied tomb, so I agree that Easter is the most important festival 1

D10

"The festival of Christmas has lost its religious meaning."

Discuss this statement, considering the arguments for and against. (10)

Show answer

Level marked — a plan, not a tick list.

Arguments for

The festival is now dominated by spending, feasting and presents, and for many people the incarnation plays no part in it at all 1

Even the practices that began as religious symbols have been emptied out: presents originally recalled the gifts of the magi and that Jesus was God's gift to the world, and that meaning is now rarely stated 1

The complaint is not new, which arguably shows the problem is built in: the date itself was taken over from the Roman Saturnalia, and Cromwell banned the festival in the 1640s for encouraging "liberty to carnal and sensual delights" 1

Arguments against

Inside the Church the meaning is intact and explicit: Midnight Mass gives readings, prayers and carols entirely about the birth of Jesus, and raises the lighting after midnight to show the light of the world has entered the cosmos 1

The rituals still teach doctrine directly — the crib with the infant placed in the manger on Christmas Eve, the wise men arriving at Epiphany, and the Christingle orange with its candle, ribbon and fruit each explained to the children holding it 1

Christmas also still produces religious behaviour: carol singing takes the message house to house and raises money for charity, giving echoes the magi's gifts, and Catholics treat the day as a day of obligation 1

Christian teaching to appraise with

Some of the "secular" content is religious in origin and still carries the theology: Jesus' birth in a stable is why Christmas is a time of charity and of identifying with the poor and marginalised, and the Christmas truce of 1914 came from "prince of peace" 1

But a Christian might concede the harder half of the charge: a festival observed by millions who do not believe the incarnation has lost its meaning for them, even if not for the Church 1

Conclusion

The statement is true of the wider culture and false of Christian worship, so it needs splitting rather than answering yes or no 1

My view is that Christmas has not lost its religious meaning but has acquired a louder secular one alongside it — and since the Church's own observances still state the doctrine plainly, I disagree with the statement as worded 1

D11

"A pilgrimage is just an excuse for a holiday."

Discuss this statement, considering the arguments for and against. (10)

Show answer

Level marked — a plan, not a tick list.

Arguments for

Pilgrimage is not an obligation in Christianity, so nobody has to go — which makes it look like a choice about travel rather than a religious duty 1

God is omnipresent, so the journey achieves nothing a Christian could not achieve at home through prayer and Bible study; and the money could instead relieve need, which Jesus said is service to him (Matthew 25:31–46) 1

Luther pressed the point from inside Christianity: "no commandment enjoins them, no obedience attaches to them… these pilgrimages give countless occasions to commit sin" (1520) 1

Arguments against

What pilgrims actually do is not holiday behaviour: at Taizé they camp, do chores, attend prayer three times a day, and some keep complete silence; on the Via Dolorosa they walk Jesus' route to the cross praying at the Stations 1

Hardship is the point, not a drawback — medieval pilgrims walked for years as a penance, and sacrifice is how pilgrims show God his importance to them 1

Jesus himself went on pilgrimage to Jerusalem for Passover as a child (Luke 2:41–43), so the practice has his example behind it 1

Christian teaching to appraise with

The motives pilgrims report are specifically religious: healing at Lourdes, confession at the Reconciliation Chapel, empathy with Jesus' suffering at the Holy Sepulchre — none of which a holiday supplies 1

And some pilgrimages cost the pilgrim more than they give: Bethlehem is in a war zone, and pilgrims come back with an understanding of the plight of Palestinians, sometimes forming charitable links with hospitals and orphanages 1

Conclusion

The objection has real force against the idea that pilgrimage is necessary — God's omnipresence means it is not — but very little against the claim that it is religious 1

Given what pilgrims actually do when they arrive, I disagree with the statement: a pilgrimage may be enjoyable, but discomfort, prayer and penance are not what people travel for on holiday 1

D12

"Bethlehem is the most important place of pilgrimage for Christians."

Discuss this statement, considering the arguments for and against. (10)

Level marked — a plan, not a tick list.

Arguments for

Bethlehem is where Christians believe the incarnation happened — God becoming human — which is the event every other Christian claim depends on 1

It has been a pilgrimage site for nearly two thousand years: St Justin Martyr, in the second century, told anyone wanting proof of Christ's birth to go and see the cave and the manger, and the Church of the Nativity was built around 338 AD 1

The site itself teaches: the door of humility forces every pilgrim to bow to the Christ child, and the fourteen-pointed star in the Grotto marks the birthplace, so the pilgrimage is an act of worship rather than sightseeing 1

Arguments against

Jerusalem has the stronger claim, because it holds the crucifixion and resurrection — and Paul says that without the resurrection Christian faith is in vain (1 Corinthians 15) 1

Jerusalem also holds far more: the Cenacle where the Eucharist was instituted, Gethsemane, the Via Dolorosa, the Holy Sepulchre and the Church of the Ascension — a whole week of the Gospel rather than a single night 1

For many Christians the most significant site is neither: Roman Catholics flock to Lourdes for healing, and tens of thousands of young people go to Taizé for reconciliation and ecumenical worship 1

Christian teaching to appraise with

Bethlehem's "necessity" argument is weaker than it sounds: the birth is necessary for the death, but Christians do not take that to make it more significant — the nativity itself points forward through the magi's myrrh 1

And many Protestants would reject the whole premise, since God is omnipresent and no place is holier than another — which makes "most important place" a question only some Christians think meaningful 1

Conclusion

Bethlehem is undeniably one of the two most significant sites, and for the incarnation it has no rival 1

But since Christianity rests on the resurrection rather than the birth, and Jerusalem holds the passion, the empty tomb and the ascension, I disagree: Jerusalem is the more important place of pilgrimage 1
