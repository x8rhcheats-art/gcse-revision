01How it actually works

What they actually ask

Paper 2 Section 3, Religion B: Christianity, every June paper from 2019 to 2023 — always shaped (a) 4, (b) 6, (c) 10. (The 2024 and 2025 Paper 2 papers held in this app are the Buddhism option booklets, so Christianity cannot be checked for those two years.) Two things dominate: the church building and the rites of passage. One part on each, in every single paper.

  • The church building — its features, its design, and whether any of it matters — in all five years. "The design of a church is not important for Christian worship" (2019c); "Outline two external features of a Christian church" (2020a) and "Christian churches should be more than just places of worship" (2020c); "Explain why churches are important for Christians" (2021b); "Explain the significance of the design of church buildings for Christians" (2022b); "All Christian churches should have the same features" (2023c). The 10-marker is always the same argument underneath: does the building help or distract — which is exactly the Anglican-versus-Baptist contrast.
  • Rites of passage — baptism, marriage, funerals — in all five years. Marriage: "Outline two parts of the Christian marriage ceremony" (2019a). Baptism: "Explain why baptism is important for most Christians" (2019b); "Only those who have been baptised are truly Christians" (2022c); "Outline two features of a Christian baptism" (2023a). Funerals: "Explain why funeral ceremonies are important for Christians" (2020b); "Outline two features of a Christian funeral" (2021a). All three at once: "Celebrating rites of passage is an essential part of Christianity" (2021c).
  • The Eucharist — "Explain why the celebration of the Eucharist is important for most Christians" (2023b). Asked only once in five years, which is surprising given it is the one thing the spec names explicitly — so treat it as overdue rather than as low priority.
  • Private prayer and devotional activities — "Outline two examples of Christian devotional activities" (2022a). Also only once, and only as a 4-marker.

What has not been asked: the form and style of public worship compared across two denominations has never been the question itself, even though it is the spec's own wording. It is the material the design and Eucharist answers are built from, so it is still worth knowing in full.

More detail

The spec asks almost everything in this section about "any two Christian denominations" — two places of worship, two forms of public worship. So the whole section is built around a comparison, and the safest pair to learn is Anglican and Baptist, because the differences in the building, the service and the baptism all come from the same root cause: whether worship centres on the Eucharist or on the Word. Learn that one distinction properly and most of the section falls out of it.

Christian worship, whichever denomination, is made of the same elements: praising God in music and speech; readings from Scripture and prayers of various sorts; a sermon; and holy ceremonies, often called sacraments, above all the Eucharist. It can happen in a church or alone, and Christians gather on Sunday because that is the day of Jesus' resurrection. The warrant for gathering at all is Jesus' own: "For where two or three gather in my name, there am I with them." (Matthew 18:20)

Two places of worship compared — Anglican and Baptist

Spec 3.1 · "the local places of worship of any two Christian denominations"

  • The root difference — an Anglican church is a sacred space focused on the altar, a Baptist one a room for preaching.
  • Outside — Anglican often cruciform with a tower or spire; Baptist often a plain square building.
  • Inside an Anglican church — nave and chancel, a decorated high altar, stained glass, a font near the door.
  • Inside a Baptist church — one undivided room, the pulpit centre stage, a plain table and a baptism pool.
More detail

An Anglican church is built to be a sacred space with the altar as its focus; a Baptist church is built to be a room where the Bible can be preached. Every difference in the list below follows from that.

FeatureAnglicanBaptist
Shape from outsideOften cruciform — cross-shaped. Grand, creating a sense of sacred spaceOften just a square building, with nothing special about the exterior
Tower or spireA tower or spire pointing up to God, and holding the bellsAt most a single bell
Internal divisionSeparated into nave (where the congregation sits) and chancel (around the altar)One big undivided room
For baptismA font, traditionally near the door, for infant baptismA baptism pool near the front, for the total immersion of adults
For preachingA pulpit, to the leftThe pulpit is centre stage
For readingsA lectern to the right, often eagle-shaped — symbolising the spreading of the wordPlain, and often combined with the pulpit
Focal pointThe high altar, where the Eucharist is celebrated, decorated with cloth and candlesA simple wooden table, undecorated, and not at the centre
WindowsStained glass — memorials, or telling Bible stories to people who could not read themPlain glass

One further Anglican idea worth a sentence in a 6-marker: some Christians see the layout as a map of a Christian's journey through life. They are brought to the font as an infant for baptism, may be married before the altar, and their coffin will rest before that same altar at their funeral — so the building itself tells the story of a life lived in the Church.

Why the two designs are so different

Spec 3.1 · "Different forms of church design and their significance… the reasons for their historical and contemporary importance"

  • A different focus of worship — the Eucharist puts the altar centre stage, the word puts the pulpit there.
  • Different theological emphases — beliefs about baptism give a font in one church and a pool in the other.
  • History — many Anglican churches pre-date the Reformation; Baptists are non-conformists who set themselves apart.
  • Money — rich parishes and guilds paid for splendid Anglican churches; Baptists grew without wealthy backing.
More detail

"Explain why Christian churches have different designs" is a standard 6-marker, and there are four reasons — two theological, two historical.

  1. A different focus of worship. For Anglicans worship centres on the Eucharist, so the altar is centre stage. For Baptists it centres on the preaching of the word, so the pulpit or lectern is the most important feature. The furniture follows the theology.
  2. Different theological emphases on particular doctrines. Beliefs about baptism are the clearest case: a font for infant baptism in the Anglican church, a pool for adult immersion in the Baptist one.
  3. History. Many Anglican churches pre-date the Reformation and so follow the Catholic pattern, which then became traditional. Baptists are non-conformists who deliberately wanted to set themselves apart from the Catholic tradition.
  4. Money. Anglican parish churches were a source of community pride: rich individuals, groups, guilds and parishes paid for splendid buildings — the wool churches of Suffolk, the Somerset towers. Baptists were more marginal and grew rapidly in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries without the financial backing of the rich.

Two patterns of public worship

Spec 3.2 · "The form and style of public worship of any two Christian denominations"

  • Learn the two patterns as a pair — a service of the Eucharist, and a service of the Word.
  • Eucharist — hymns and a sermon, then Communion at the altar; priest-led, robed and liturgical.
  • Word — a Bible reading and a sermon teaching that text; Baptist, informal, congregation heavily involved.
More detail

Learn the two patterns as a pair: a service of the Eucharist, and a service of the Word.

The mark is for the reason, not the adjective. Formality is a consequence of a belief about how God is reached.

Why the Eucharist matters

Spec 3.2 · "with particular reference to the celebration of the Eucharist/Communion/Mass/Lord's Supper"

  • Four names, one thing — Eucharist, Holy Communion, Mass, the Lord's Supper.
  • A sacrament — stressed most by Catholics and Anglicans, who hold more regular services than Protestants.
  • A re-creation of the Last Supper — fulfilling Jesus' command to "do this in remembrance of me".
  • A sign of unity and the new covenant — "though we are many we are one body".
More detail

The Eucharist matters to almost all Christians, whatever the denomination — though Catholics and Anglicans stress it most and hold more regular services than Protestants do. Two groups reject it: the Quakers and the Salvation Army. The names are interchangeable and the exam may use any of them: Eucharist, Holy Communion, Mass, the Lord's Supper.

  • It is a sacrament.
  • It is a re-creation of the Last Supper, and fulfils Jesus' own command to "do this in remembrance of me".
  • It unites Christians of all backgrounds and creates fellowship — "though we are many we are one body for we all partake of the one bread".
  • It is a sign of the new covenant.
  • It is a reminder, or a re-creation, of Jesus' saving sacrifice and a sign of his continuing love. Protestants differ here: they hold that Jesus' death on the cross was a "once for all" sacrifice, so the Eucharist does not itself represent a sacrifice.

What Christians believe happens at the Eucharist

Spec 3.2 · divergent Christian belief about the Eucharist

  • Transubstantiation — Roman Catholics and Catholic Anglicans: the bread and wine really become Jesus' body and blood.
  • Consubstantiation — many Anglicans and Lutherans: Jesus is spiritually present in the bread and wine.
  • Memorial — most Protestants: nothing special happens; it re-creates the Last Supper.
  • Three positions, three words — the sharpest vocabulary in the section, and what separates a top answer.
More detail

Three positions, three words. This is the sharpest piece of vocabulary in the section and the examiner's favourite way of separating a top-level answer from a middling one.

BeliefWho holds itWhat it claims

The Eucharist 10-marker is "All Christians should celebrate the Eucharist", and the word doing the work is all — because the objection comes from inside Christianity.

Private prayer

Spec 3.2 · "Private prayer and/or devotional activities and their significance for the individual Christians"

  • Prayer is communication with God — and the four types spell ACTS.
  • Adoration, confession, thanksgiving, supplication — praise, repentance, gratitude, and asking for help.
  • Why it matters — it builds a relationship with God and brings hope, strength and an understanding of his will.
More detail

Prayer is communication with God, and the four types have initials worth memorising because "outline two reasons why a Christian might pray" is a stock 4-marker.

Type of prayerWhat the Christian is doing
AdorationPraising God
ConfessionSaying sorry, repenting of sins — a key part of all religious services
ThanksgivingThanking God for blessings or for particular events
SupplicationAsking God for something — help or strength, for oneself or for others

Why it matters: prayer builds a relationship with God; it is communication with him; it is a source of hope and strength; it is how a Christian comes to understand God's will. It has been called "spiritual breathing" — something done constantly rather than occasionally.

And the problems with prayer, which is where the 10-mark marks live. Maurice Wiles argued that a God who answers prayers while ignoring the Holocaust is not worth believing in. There are psychological explanations of what prayer does for the person praying. A Marxist critique says do something about the problem yourself rather than asking God. And there is the simple fact of unanswered prayer.

Devotional activities

Spec 3.2 · "Private worship and devotional activities and their significance in the lives of individuals"

  • The Rosary — a Catholic bead pattern of Hail Marys and Lord's Prayers, focused on the Virgin Mary.
  • Icons — Orthodox Christians meditate on an image of Jesus or a saint: "contemplation in colour".
  • Bible reading — a verse read over and over, for strength and to apply it to one's own life.
  • Spiritual exercises — the Jesus Prayer repeated while walking or prostrate: discipline and humility.
More detail

Devotional activities are practices that bring a person closer to God — four to know, and each one belongs to an identifiable tradition, which is worth saying.

Baptism

Spec 3.2 · "Rites of passage… with particular reference to baptism, marriage and funerals"

  • A rite of passage — a ceremony marking a point of change in a person's life.
  • Infant baptism — Catholics and Anglicans; the child is brought to the font and godparents make the vows.
  • Believers' baptism — Baptists baptise adults by total immersion, after a testimony of their own faith.
  • Why it matters — a sacrament, an initiation, the washing away of original sin, and following Jesus' example.
More detail

A rite of passage is a ceremony or ritual that marks a point of change in a person's life, and baptism is the one where denominations differ most sharply. Catholics and Anglicans baptise babies. Baptists baptise adults — "believers' baptism" — on two grounds: an adult can make their own choice, and Jesus himself was baptised as an adult.

StageAnglican — infant baptismBaptist — believers' baptism
Who is there and what they do firstParents and godparents attend and the child is brought to the fontThe candidate may make a testimony, explaining their faith and why they have come forward
MarkingThe priest makes the sign of the cross on the child's forehead, claiming them for ChristThe adult wears white, symbolising purity and the dress of an initiate
PromisesThe godparents make vows on the child's behalf — rejecting the devil and all rebellion against God, renouncing the deceit and corruption of evil, repenting of sin, and turning to Christ as SaviourThe testimony serves as the candidate's own promise — which is the point of baptising adults
The waterBlessed water is poured over the baby's head: "I baptise you in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit"Total immersion, usually in a pool, sometimes a river, baptised in the name of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit
AfterwardsThe priest anoints the child with a special oil, chrism; a candle is often given, symbolising Jesus as light of the world and the move from darkness into lightIt is common for the adult to speak in tongues afterwards — a gift of the Holy Spirit

Why baptism is important: it is a sacrament; it is an initiation ritual into the Church; it is the washing away of original sin; it follows Jesus' own example, baptised by John the Baptist in the river Jordan. In infant baptism it also celebrates the birth of a child, and in popular — though not religious — culture functions as a naming ceremony. In believers' baptism the adult is thought to be born again, the total immersion representing death, and so to be assured of salvation.

And the baptism 10-marker, "A person can only be a Christian if they have been baptised". The two denominations disagree with each other on both sides of it, which is exactly what the evaluation marks are for.

The words of the Anglican baptism service

"In baptism, God calls us out of darkness into his marvellous light. To follow Christ means dying to sin and rising to new life with him."

Used to argue that baptism is not a naming ceremony but a death and resurrection in miniature — which is why the candle and the imagery of light matter, and why Baptists read total immersion as representing death.

Marriage

Spec 3.2 · "Rites of passage… marriage"

  • Marriage — the legal and religious joining of a couple, who make vows before God, family and friends.
  • "What God has joined together let no one separate" — said by the priest as he joins their hands.
  • God sanctioned it — Eve became Adam's wife, and Jesus attended the wedding at Cana.
  • It creates families — the building blocks of society, and the proper place to raise children.
More detail

Marriage is the legal and religious joining of a man and a wife, in which the couple make vows to each other in the presence of God, their family and friends. The priest joins their hands and proclaims "What God has joined together let no one separate."

Why Christians hold it to be important:

  • God sanctioned it — Eve became Adam's wife, and Jesus attended a wedding at Cana in Galilee.
  • God joins the couple, and helps sustain and support their relationship afterwards.
  • It is held to be the only proper place to have and bring up children.
  • It creates families, which Christians see as the building blocks of society.
  • It is a celebration of the couple's love.

The booklet covers the marriage service itself only by cross-reference to notes that were not in the material, so the detail of the vows and the ring exchange is not reproduced here. If a question asks you to explain how the rites at a wedding service reflect the Christian view of marriage, build the answer out of the four points above plus the words the priest says — and ask for the Relationships notes to fill the gap.

Funerals

Spec 3.2 · "Rites of passage… funerals"

  • The Gathering — the coffin brought in to John 11:25, "I am the resurrection and the life".
  • The Eulogy and prayers — a speech celebrating the person's life; Psalm 23 may be read or sung.
  • Commendation and Committal — the deceased offered to God, the body committed "in the sure and certain hope".
  • What it reveals — salvation for believers, God's mercy and strength, and hope of the resurrection.
More detail

A Christian funeral has a fixed shape, and the four stages conveniently all begin with C — gathering, eulogy, prayers, commend, committal.

  1. The Gathering. The coffin is brought into church while the priest reads from the Bible — "'I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me will live, even though he dies,' says the Lord" (John 11:25).
  2. The Eulogy — a speech or sermon celebrating the person's life.
  3. Prayers for the deceased and for those who mourn. Psalm 23, "The Lord is my shepherd", may be read or sung.
  4. The Commendation — the deceased is offered to God.
  5. The Committal — the body is committed to the grave: "We now commit his/her body to the ground; earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust: in the sure and certain hope of the resurrection to eternal life."

Why funerals matter: to remember the deceased; to give thanks to God for their life; to commend — to offer them to God; to commit them to their grave; and to comfort those who mourn.

What a funeral reveals about Christian beliefs about death — which is a 6-marker in its own right: that there is salvation for those who believe; that God is merciful, both to the deceased and to the mourners; that God is a source of strength; and that there is hope of the resurrection. The committal says so explicitly — the body goes into the ground "in the sure and certain hope of the resurrection to eternal life".

✗ "A funeral is important because it lets the family say goodbye."
✓ "The committal commits the body to the ground 'in the sure and certain hope of the resurrection to eternal life' — so the service is a statement that death is not the end, not only a farewell."

"Funeral services have no purpose except to say goodbye" is a real 10-marker. The answer is the wording of the liturgy.

Why rites of passage matter at all

Spec 3.2 · "Rites of passage and their meaning and importance for Christians"

  • They celebrate a key part of a life — and mark a transition, often a difficult one.
  • They reinforce religious views — stating the belief at the moment it matters most.
  • They link people to their religion and support them — presenting life as a journey through it.
  • The building says the same thing — font, altar and the space before the altar, from birth to death.
More detail

Six reasons, and they work for baptism, marriage and funerals alike — so this is the paragraph that converts any rite-of-passage question into an answer.

  • They celebrate a key part of someone's life.
  • They mark a transition — often an important or difficult one.
  • They reinforce religious views, stating the belief at the moment it matters most.
  • They link people to their religion, presenting life as a journey through it.
  • They support people through the community and the church.
  • They are a celebration shared with family and community.

That last point connects back to the building: in an Anglican church the font, the altar and the space before the altar carry a person from birth to marriage to death in the same room.

The last 10-marker in the section takes all three rites together: "Celebrating rites of passage is an essential part of Christianity". The six reasons above give the "for" column; this table pairs each with its answer.

02Cheat sheet

The must-knows

The one distinction that explains the section: Anglican worship centres on the Eucharist, so the altar is the focal point; Baptist worship centres on the Word, so the pulpit is.

Three Eucharistic beliefs: transubstantiation (really becomes) · consubstantiation (spiritually present) · memorial (nothing special happens).

Four kinds of prayer: adoration · confession · thanksgiving · supplication.

Four devotional activities: rosary (Catholic) · icons (Orthodox) · Bible reading (all) · the Jesus Prayer.

Baptism splits: Anglicans baptise infants at a font, with godparents' vows; Baptists baptise adults by total immersion, after a testimony.

Funeral shape: Gathering · Eulogy · Prayers · Commend · Commit — "in the sure and certain hope of the resurrection to eternal life".

Who rejects the Eucharist: Quakers and the Salvation Army.

The 10-mark frame

  • Restate the statement and name the two denominations you are going to compare. Anglican and Baptist is the safest pair, because every difference follows from one cause — whether worship centres on the Eucharist or on the Word.
  • The strongest case for, with Christian teaching and scripture attached: Matthew 18:20 for worshipping anywhere, "do this in remembrance of me" for the Eucharist, Jesus' own baptism in the Jordan and the washing away of original sin for baptism, John 11:25 and the committal for funerals.
  • The strongest case against, also with Christian teaching rather than bare doubt: the church building as sacred space and as a map of a life from font to altar to funeral; the Quakers and the Salvation Army, who reject sacraments entirely and are plainly Christian; the problems with prayer — Maurice Wiles, the Marxist critique, and unanswered prayer.
  • Where Christians disagree with each other — transubstantiation against consubstantiation against the memorial view; font against pool; set liturgy against free worship; infant baptism against an adult's own testimony. This is the paragraph the spec calls divergence, and it is what lifts an answer into the top band.
  • A conclusion that decides, giving the reason that decided it and your own opinion — and watch the wording of the statement, because "all Christians should" and "only if" are usually what makes it too strong to agree with.

The sentence starters that earn the evaluation marks: "An Anglican would argue…" · "However, a Baptist would respond…" · "Quakers go further and reject the practice altogether, which shows…" · "This argument proves less than it appears, because…" · "On balance, I agree that… because…"

03Understanding it

U1

Why can you work out most of a denomination's theology just by looking at the inside of its church?

Show answer

Because the furniture follows the belief about how God is reached. If the sacraments are the way to God, the altar where the Eucharist is celebrated has to be the focal point, raised and decorated, in a chancel set apart from the congregation. If the Bible contains all a Christian needs for salvation, then the pulpit belongs centre stage and the Communion table can be a plain piece of wood off to one side.

The same logic runs through the smaller details. An eagle-shaped lectern symbolises the spreading of the word. A font by the door means infants are baptised; a pool at the front means adults are immersed. Stained glass tells Bible stories to people who could not read them; plain glass reflects a tradition that distrusts images as distraction.

U2

Two of the four reasons Christian churches differ in design are not theological at all. Does that weaken the religious significance of the buildings?

Show answer

The two non-theological reasons are history — many Anglican churches pre-date the Reformation and so follow the Catholic pattern, which became traditional, while Baptists are non-conformists who deliberately set themselves apart — and money: Anglican parishes attracted rich donors, guilds and civic pride, as the Suffolk wool churches show, while Baptists grew in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries without wealthy backing.

It does not really weaken the significance, for two reasons. First, the historical reason is itself theological in origin: non-conformists wanted a different building because they wanted a different relationship with Rome. Second, the meaning a building carries now does not depend on why it was first built — the door of humility in Bethlehem was built to stop thieves, and still makes every pilgrim bow.

The honest concession, useful in "Christian communities should not waste their money on expensive church buildings": some grandeur was about status rather than God, and Baptist plainness can be presented as a virtue made of necessity.

U3

Why do Christians believe they should worship together, when private prayer is available any time?

Show answer

Jesus promised his presence specifically to the gathering: "For where two or three gather in my name, there am I with them." (Matthew 18:20) So corporate worship is not just private prayer multiplied — something is held to be available in it that is not available alone.

The Eucharist makes the same point in practice: it is the sacrament of unity, "though we are many we are one body for we all partake of the one bread", and it cannot be a sign of fellowship if performed in isolation. Christians also gather on Sunday because it is the day of the resurrection, so the timing itself is a shared statement of belief.

The counter, for a 10-marker: God is omnipresent and hears private prayer, Quakers worship without priest or sacrament, and worship in the home avoids the distractions of a crowd. So the strong claim — that church attendance is necessary — is harder to defend than the claim that it is valuable.

U4

What is actually at stake between transubstantiation, consubstantiation and the memorial view?

Show answer

Whether the Eucharist does something or remembers something.

Transubstantiation — Roman Catholics and Catholic Anglicans — holds that the bread and wine really become Christ's body and blood. If so, the Eucharist is a genuine encounter with Christ and the priest who consecrates it is indispensable. Consubstantiation softens this to a spiritual presence: Christ is there, but the elements have not changed. The memorial view holds that nothing special happens at all — it is a re-creation of the Last Supper.

The knock-on effects are large. Protestants also hold that Jesus' death was a "once for all" sacrifice, so the Eucharist cannot be a sacrifice repeated — which is why they celebrate it less often than Catholics and Anglicans, and why Quakers and the Salvation Army can drop it altogether without thinking they have lost access to God.

U5

Why do Baptists baptise adults rather than babies, and what is the strongest Anglican reply?

Show answer

The Baptist case: baptism is a commitment, and only an adult can make their own choice. The candidate gives a testimony explaining their faith and why they have come forward — something an infant cannot do, which is why Anglicans need godparents to make the vows on the child's behalf. Baptists add that Jesus himself was baptised as an adult, by John in the Jordan, so the practice follows his example. And total immersion represents death, which matches the theology of being born again better than a sprinkling does.

The Anglican reply: baptism washes away original sin and is an initiation into the Church, so delaying it withholds something a child needs rather than respecting their freedom. The godparents' vows are not a substitute for a decision but a promise to raise the child so they can make one later — which is what confirmation is for. And the service claims the child for Christ with the sign of the cross, which is God's act rather than the infant's.

U6

What is the strongest objection to prayer, and how might a Christian answer it?

Show answer

Maurice Wiles puts the sharpest form: a God who answers prayers about small personal matters while ignoring the Holocaust is not worth believing in. That is stronger than ordinary "unanswered prayer" complaints because it attacks God's character, not his existence — a selectively intervening God is morally worse than one who never intervenes.

Alongside it: psychological explanations account for prayer's effects without God, and the Marxist critique says praying about a problem is a substitute for acting on it.

The Christian reply is to look at what prayer is for. Only one of the four types — supplication — asks God to do anything; adoration, confession and thanksgiving make no request at all. On that account the purpose of prayer is to build a relationship with God and to understand his will — it is "spiritual breathing" — rather than to obtain results, so unanswered requests do not refute it. The Marxist objection also misreads most Christian practice, since prayer and charitable action generally go together.

U7

What does the wording of a Christian funeral reveal about Christian beliefs about death?

Show answer

Almost everything, because the liturgy states the doctrine outright. The coffin is brought in to "I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me will live, even though he dies" (John 11:25) — so the service opens by denying that death is final. The committal closes it the same way: the body goes to the ground "earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust" "in the sure and certain hope of the resurrection to eternal life".

So four beliefs are on display: there is salvation for those who believe; God is merciful to the deceased and to those mourning; God is a source of strength; and there is hope of the resurrection. The structure matches — the commendation offers the deceased to God, which only makes sense if there is somewhere to be offered to.

It is also honest about grief. The eulogy celebrates a life and the prayers are for the mourners as well as the dead, so the service comforts as well as teaching.

U8

Why do Christians bother with rites of passage at all, if what matters is belief?

Show answer

Because the rites do work that private belief does not. They mark a transition at the moments that are hardest or most significant — birth, marriage, death — and they reinforce the religious view precisely when it is most needed, stating it in public words that the person involved may be in no state to formulate. They link an individual to their religion, presenting a life as a journey through it, and they bring the support of a community rather than leaving someone alone with a belief.

The Anglican church building makes the argument physically: the same person is brought to the font as an infant, married before the altar, and has their coffin rest before that same altar — one room holding a whole life.

The objection, for the 10-marker: Quakers and the Salvation Army manage without sacraments, so the rites cannot be essential; and a cynic can point out that baptisms are often arranged by parents for family reasons and funerals attended by people who believe none of it. That makes "essential" the word to argue about — valuable and essential are not the same claim.

04Exam drill

How to answer each part — Paper 2

  • 4 marks — knowledge only. Two pieces of information, each with "a little development". If an example is asked for, give the example first and then its significance. Point + development = 2 marks each.
  • 6 marks — explain the significance of a person, event, belief or practice. Level marked, not point marked: two or three points explained in depth, drawing out the significance. An answer that only describes cannot reach the top level.
  • 10 marks — the evaluation. Draw on a number of viewpoints within Christianity, appraise the quality of each argument as you make it, consider both perspectives, give your own opinion with reasoned arguments, and finish with a balanced, justified conclusion.

Paper 2 questions come in threes: a 4-mark outline, a 6-mark explanation and a 10-mark evaluation. The 6s and 10s are level marked, not point marked, so the mark points below are a target for coverage rather than a tick list.

D1

Outline two internal features of a place of Christian worship. (4)

Show answer

1. An Anglican church has a high altar 1; it is the focal point of the building, decorated with cloth and candles, and it is where the Eucharist is celebrated 1

2. It also has a font 1; it holds the blessed water for infant baptism and traditionally stands near the door, at the start of a Christian's journey through the building 1

Other acceptable features: the pulpit for preaching; the eagle-shaped lectern for Bible readings; stained glass telling Bible stories; the division of nave and chancel; a Baptist baptism pool for total immersion.

D2

Outline two features of a Eucharist service. (4)

Show answer

1. The congregation take the bread and wine 1; this re-creates the Last Supper and fulfils Jesus' command to "do this in remembrance of me" 1

2. The service is priest-led and follows a set liturgy 1; the priest wears robes and follows a traditional ceremony focused on the altar, with minimal involvement from the congregation 1

Also acceptable: hymns and a sermon before the Communion itself; external stimuli such as statues, candles and incense.

D3

Outline two features of infant baptism. (4)

Show answer

1. The godparents make vows on the child's behalf 1; they reject the devil and all rebellion against God, renounce the deceit and corruption of evil, repent of sin and turn to Christ as Saviour — because the infant cannot promise for itself 1

2. Blessed water is poured over the baby's head at the font 1; the priest says "I baptise you in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" 1

Also acceptable: the sign of the cross on the forehead, claiming the child for Christ; anointing with chrism; the giving of a candle, symbolising Jesus as light of the world.

D4

Outline two features of a Christian funeral. (4)

Show answer

1. The Gathering: the coffin is brought into church while the priest reads from the Bible 1; the reading is typically "I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me will live, even though he dies" (John 11:25), so the service opens by denying that death is the end 1

2. The Committal: the body is committed to the grave 1; the priest says "earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust: in the sure and certain hope of the resurrection to eternal life" 1

Also acceptable: the eulogy celebrating the person's life; prayers for the deceased and the mourners, with Psalm 23 read or sung; the commendation, offering the deceased to God.

D5

Explain why Christian churches have different designs. (6)

Show answer

Level marked — two or three developed points.

First point — a different focus of worship. For Anglicans worship centres on the Eucharist, so the high altar is the focal point of the building, set in a chancel and decorated with cloth and candles 1; for Baptists it centres on the preaching of the word, so the pulpit is centre stage and the Communion table is a plain piece of wood that is not even central 1

Second point — different theological emphases. Beliefs about baptism are built into the furniture 1; an Anglican church has a font for infant baptism, while a Baptist church has a pool at the front for the total immersion of adults, because Baptists hold that only an adult can choose for themselves 1

Third point — history and money. Many Anglican churches pre-date the Reformation and so follow the Catholic pattern, which became traditional, while Baptists are non-conformists who deliberately set themselves apart from that tradition 1; Anglican parish churches were also a source of community pride funded by rich individuals and guilds, whereas Baptists grew in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries without wealthy backing, which is part of why their buildings are plain 1

D6

Explain why Christians celebrate the Eucharist. (6)

Show answer

Level marked — two or three developed points.

First point — obedience to Jesus. The Eucharist is a re-creation of the Last Supper, at which Jesus took bread and wine and called them his body and blood 1; he commanded his disciples to "do this in remembrance of me", so celebrating it is direct obedience to Christ, and it is a sign of the new covenant he made 1

Second point — it is a sacrament and a means of grace. For Roman Catholics and Catholic Anglicans the bread and wine really become Christ's body and blood — transubstantiation — so it is a genuine encounter with him 1; others hold that Christ is spiritually present (consubstantiation), while most Protestants treat it as a memorial and stress that Jesus' death was a "once for all" sacrifice, which is why they celebrate it less often 1

Third point — unity. It unites Christians of all backgrounds and creates a sense of fellowship — "though we are many we are one body for we all partake of the one bread" 1; and it is a reminder of Jesus' saving sacrifice and an indicator of his continuing love, which is why it is the focus of Catholic and Anglican worship, although Quakers and the Salvation Army reject it altogether 1

D7

Explain why baptism is important for most Christians. (6)

Show answer

Level marked — two or three developed points.

First point — it is a sacrament and an initiation. Baptism admits a person into the Church, and is believed to wash away original sin 1; the Anglican service states the meaning plainly — "In baptism, God calls us out of darkness into his marvellous light. To follow Christ means dying to sin and rising to new life with him" — which is why a candle is given, symbolising Jesus as light of the world 1

Second point — it follows Jesus' example. Jesus was baptised by John the Baptist in the river Jordan to mark the beginning of his ministry 1; Baptists take this further and argue that because Jesus was baptised as an adult, baptism should follow a personal decision, so the candidate gives a testimony and is totally immersed, the immersion representing death and being born again 1

Third point — it marks a transition and links a person to the faith. As a rite of passage it celebrates the birth of a child and brings the support of the church community, and in popular though not religious culture it functions as a naming ceremony 1; because the infant cannot promise anything, the godparents make the vows on the child's behalf, undertaking to raise them in the faith until they can choose for themselves at confirmation 1

D8

Explain what a funeral service reveals about Christian attitudes to death. (6)

Show answer

Level marked — two or three developed points.

First point — death is not the end. The coffin is brought in to the words "I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me will live, even though he dies" (John 11:25) 1; the committal makes the same claim at the close — the body is committed to the ground "in the sure and certain hope of the resurrection to eternal life" — so the whole shape of the service is a statement of the hope of resurrection 1

Second point — salvation and the mercy of God. The commendation offers the deceased to God, which only makes sense if there is somewhere for them to go, and it shows a belief in salvation for those who believe 1; prayers are said for the deceased and for the mourners, revealing a belief that God is merciful to both and is a source of strength 1

Third point — the life is still valued. The eulogy celebrates the person's life and the service gives thanks to God for it, so Christian hope about the next life does not make this one unimportant 1; Psalm 23, "The Lord is my shepherd", may be read or sung, which frames death as God accompanying a person rather than abandoning them 1

D9

"God can be worshipped anywhere."

Discuss this statement, considering the arguments for and against. In your answer you should include: reference to teachings; other (divergent) points of view, either within the religion or from other religions; your opinion using reasoned arguments; and a balanced conclusion. (10)

Show answer

Level marked in the real paper, so treat this as a plan for coverage rather than a tick list.

Arguments for

God is omnipresent, so no building can contain him or bring a worshipper closer than anywhere else 1

Jesus attached his presence to the people, not the place: "For where two or three gather in my name, there am I with them" (Matthew 18:20) — which says nothing about a church 1

Much Christian worship already happens elsewhere: private prayer, Bible reading, the rosary, icons and the Jesus Prayer are all devotional activities carried out at home, and Quakers need no consecrated building at all 1

Arguments against

The Eucharist, the focus of Catholic and Anglican worship, is a sacrament celebrated at an altar by an ordained priest — so the central act of worship is not simply portable 1

Church buildings teach what is being worshipped: a cruciform plan, a spire pointing to God, stained glass telling Bible stories, a font and an altar that map a Christian's life from baptism to funeral 1

They also create the community that worship requires, and are used for far more than services — rites of passage, Sunday school, study groups, charity stalls and community events 1

Christian teaching to appraise with

The omnipresence argument proves less than it appears: it shows a building is not necessary for God to hear, not that it is useless for helping humans attend — which is the point of external stimuli such as candles, incense and statues 1

And denominations genuinely divide on it, which means neither side can claim to be simply right: where the Bible is held to contain all a Christian needs, the building matters much less than where the sacraments are the way to God 1

Conclusion

Taken literally the statement is true, and most Christians would accept it — God is everywhere and hears prayer anywhere 1

But "can be" is not "is best": since the Eucharist and the gathered community both depend on a shared place, I agree with the statement while holding that church buildings still do something private worship cannot 1

D10

"All Christians should celebrate the Eucharist."

Discuss this statement, considering the arguments for and against. (10)

Show answer

Level marked — a plan, not a tick list.

Arguments for

Jesus commanded it: at the Last Supper he gave the bread and wine and told his disciples to "do this in remembrance of me", so celebrating it is obedience rather than preference 1

It is a sacrament — for Catholics and Catholic Anglicans the bread and wine really become Christ's body and blood (transubstantiation), so to abstain is to refuse an encounter with Christ himself 1

It is the sacrament of unity: "though we are many we are one body for we all partake of the one bread", and it is a sign of the new covenant, so a Christian who does not share in it stands outside something the Church does together 1

Arguments against

Quakers and the Salvation Army reject it entirely and are unquestionably Christian, which shows it cannot be a condition of being one 1

On the memorial view, held by most Protestants, nothing special happens to the bread and wine — it is a re-creation of the Last Supper, so the obligation is much weaker than Catholics suppose 1

Protestants also hold Jesus' death to be a "once for all" sacrifice, so the Eucharist cannot be a sacrifice repeated; that is why Protestant worship centres on the Word, with the Bible read and preached, and holds Communion less frequently 1

Christian teaching to appraise with

The "Jesus commanded it" argument is the strongest on the for side, and the memorial view does not actually deny it — most Protestants do celebrate Communion, just less often and with a different understanding of what it is 1

So the real disagreement is narrower than the statement suggests: it is about what the Eucharist is, and only Quakers and the Salvation Army reject the practice outright, on the grounds that worship need not be sacramental at all 1

Conclusion

Nearly all Christians do celebrate it, and the command of Jesus gives them a strong reason to 1

But "all Christians should" cannot be sustained against Quaker practice, which demonstrates a Christian life without it; so I disagree with the statement as worded, while agreeing the Eucharist is central for most Christians 1

D11

"A person can only be a Christian if they have been baptised."

Discuss this statement, considering the arguments for and against. (10)

Show answer

Level marked — a plan, not a tick list.

Arguments for

Baptism is the initiation ritual of the Church — the ceremony by which a person is admitted — so logically there is no membership before it 1

It is a sacrament, believed to wash away original sin; on that view an unbaptised person has not had done for them the thing a Christian needs doing 1

It follows Jesus' own example, baptised by John in the Jordan, and in believers' baptism the adult is held to be born again, the total immersion representing death — which Baptists take as the assurance of salvation 1

Arguments against

Quakers and the Salvation Army practise no sacraments, including baptism, and are Christian denominations — so baptism cannot be the test 1

Denominations cannot even agree what counts: Anglicans baptise infants at a font with godparents' vows, Baptists insist on an adult's own decision and total immersion, and each regards the other's practice as inadequate 1

If baptism is what makes a Christian, infant baptism is hard to defend, since the baby believes nothing and the promises are made by someone else — which is precisely the Baptist objection 1

Christian teaching to appraise with

Both traditions actually locate the decisive thing in faith rather than water: Anglicans see the vows as a promise to raise the child towards their own commitment at confirmation, and Baptists require a testimony before the baptism rather than after it 1

In popular culture baptism often functions as a naming ceremony arranged by parents for family reasons, which further weakens the idea that the ritual by itself makes a believer 1

Conclusion

Baptism is extremely important for most Christians — a sacrament, an initiation and a following of Christ's example — and for Roman Catholics it comes close to being necessary 1

But since Quakers are Christians without it, and since both main traditions make faith rather than the ceremony the decisive factor, I disagree: baptism marks becoming a Christian rather than causing it 1

D12

"Celebrating rites of passage is an essential part of Christianity."

Discuss this statement, considering the arguments for and against. (10)

Show answer

Level marked — a plan, not a tick list.

Arguments for

The rites state the belief at the moment it matters most: the funeral commits a body to the ground "in the sure and certain hope of the resurrection to eternal life", and baptism calls a person "out of darkness into his marvellous light" 1

Two of the three are sacraments with direct warrant: baptism follows Jesus' own baptism in the Jordan, and marriage was sanctioned by God — Eve became Adam's wife, and Jesus attended the wedding at Cana 1

They link a person to the faith and to the community, which supports them through transitions that are often difficult — in an Anglican church the same person meets the font as an infant, the altar at their marriage and that altar again at their funeral 1

Arguments against

Quakers and the Salvation Army reject sacraments altogether, so a Christian life is demonstrably possible without these ceremonies 1

Christians disagree about the rites themselves — infant versus believers' baptism — which is hard to square with calling them essential, since each tradition thinks the other is doing it wrongly 1

In practice they are often social rather than religious: baptism functions in popular culture as a naming ceremony arranged by parents, and funerals are attended by people who believe none of the liturgy they hear 1

Christian teaching to appraise with

What is genuinely essential is the belief the rites express — salvation, resurrection, God's mercy — and that belief is also carried by Scripture, prayer and the Eucharist, which happen weekly or daily rather than a few times in a life 1

But the objection from social misuse cuts less deeply than it looks: that some people treat a baptism as a party does not show the rite has no religious content, any more than a commercialised Christmas shows the incarnation is not celebrated 1

Conclusion

Rites of passage are enormously valuable: they mark transitions, reinforce belief, and bring the support of the community at birth, marriage and death 1

But "essential" is too strong, because Quaker practice shows Christianity functioning without them; so I disagree with the statement, while holding that for most Christians the rites are the clearest public expression of what they believe 1
