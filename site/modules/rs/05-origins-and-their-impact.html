Ethics first: the three theories the course starts with

Spec 1.1 · The basis of human decision-making, before Christian sources of authority

  • Ethics — the study of how people behave, the reasons they give and the rationale behind their decisions.
  • Moral absolutism — objective principles true for all people in all situations, e.g. deontological ethics.
  • Moral relativism — morality related to the particular situation, e.g. consequentialism.
  • Kant — do your duty whatever the consequences; test it by the categorical imperative. Absolutist.
  • Bentham — the Greatest Happiness Principle: the greatest happiness of the greatest number. Relativist.
  • Fletcher — situation ethics: do the most loving thing here; the easiest to link to agape.
More detail

Ethics is the study of how people behave — what they do, the reasons they give, and the rationale behind their decisions — and it divides into two families before it divides into theories.

  • Moral absolutism — there are objective moral principles that hold true for all people in all situations, e.g. deontological ethics (duty-based).
  • Moral relativism — morality should be related to the particular situation people find themselves in, e.g. consequentialism (results-based).
TheoryWho, and the test they apply
Kantian ethicsImmanuel Kant (1724–1804): the most important thing is to do your duty, irrespective of the consequences. You find your duty by the categorical imperative — an act is right if you could expect everyone else to act the same way in the same situation, establishing a universal law. Absolutist.
UtilitarianismJeremy Bentham (1748–1832): decisions should be based on the Greatest Happiness Principle — a good action is the one that brings the greatest happiness to the greatest number of people. Relativist.
Situation ethicsJoseph Fletcher (1905–1991): decisions should be made on the basis of what is the most loving thing to do in that particular situation. Relativist, and the easiest to connect to Christian agape.

These are worth three lines in an answer about why Christians disagree with each other: a fundamentalist reading of the Bible is absolutist, while a liberal reading that weighs context and consequences looks much more like situation ethics.

The Bible — what it is and why it has authority

Spec 1.1 · "The Bible and its authority for Christians"

  • The Bible is Scripture — an agreed, authoritative collection Christians believe reveals God's word.
  • 66 books, two halves — the Old Testament (Jewish scriptures) and the New Testament (Christian scriptures).
  • The New Testament — four Gospels, Acts, the letters and Revelation; the canon was agreed in AD 367.
  • Catholics add the Apocrypha — the cleanest denominational difference about the Bible itself; Protestants do not.
  • Authority comes from its origin — 2 Timothy 3:16, "all scripture is inspired by God".
  • It contains all that is needed for salvation — and is a way of encountering God, not just information about him.

The verse that carries the argument

"All scripture is inspired by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness, that the man of God may be complete, equipped for every good work."

2 Timothy 3:16–17

Used to argue the Bible's authority comes from God, not from human authorship. Fundamentalists lean on the first clause ("inspired by God", or "God-breathed"); others lean on the last ("for teaching") — that it is useful, which does not require it to be literally true in every detail.

More detail

The Bible is Scripture: an agreed, authoritative collection of texts which Christians believe is the revelation of God's word. Its authority rests on where Christians believe it came from — not on how old it is or how well it is written.

  • 66 books, in two halves: the Old Testament (the Jewish scriptures) and the New Testament (the Christian scriptures).
  • The New Testament contains four Gospels — the life and teaching of Jesus; Acts — a history of the early church by St Luke; letters — mostly Paul's, answering theological and ethical problems in the early church; and an apocalypse, the book of Revelation, about the coming of God's kingdom.
  • It is believed to contain everything needed to lead a good life and attain salvation.
  • It is a way of encountering God, not only a source of information about him.
  • The Holy Spirit is believed to guide its interpretation.

"Bible" comes from the Greek biblia, meaning books: it is a library written between about 1200 BC and AD 200, and the list of books accepted as the genuine word of God — the canon — was finally agreed in AD 367.

Why each half matters. The Old Testament is important to Christians because it gives the context in which to understand Jesus and his significance: the New Testament writers used the Jewish scriptures to show that Jesus is the messiah the prophets foretold. The New Testament is the account of God sending his son to willingly sacrifice his life so that humanity could return to God — so it is a set of books focused on the love of God, with Jesus's teaching and example showing how to respond to that love, summed up in the two great commandments to love God and love your neighbour (Mark 12:30–31).

Two more facts for a first line: Christianity is the most widely practised religion in the world, with more than two billion followers; roughly 42 million Britons regard themselves as Christian and about 6 million actively practise.

Also note that Revelation itself splits Christians the way the rest of the Bible does: some interpret it literally as a forecast of the End Time, while others read it as symbolic — written to reassure Christians during Roman persecution and encourage resilience.

Authority in the Church of England

Spec 1.1 · "The nature and form of authority in any one Christian denomination"

  • Scripture, Tradition and Reason — three sources held in creative tension; that triad is the whole answer.
  • Scripture is primary — Article 6: it "containth all things necessary to salvation".
  • Tradition — Fathers, councils, saints and bishops, so long as they agree with the Bible (Article 20).
  • Reason — applies both to today's problems, and is what lets Anglicans set aside "women should be silent".
  • Why three — a guide both fixed and dynamic, preventing complacency and forcing constant review.
  • The structure, in order — monarch, Canterbury, York, diocesan bishops, priests, deacons and laity.
More detail

The Church of England answers moral questions from three sources held in creative tension: Scripture, Tradition and Reason. That triad is the whole answer to "explain your chosen denomination's sources of authority".

  1. Scripture is the primary source. "Holy Scripture containth all things necessary to salvation; so that whatsoever is not read therein, nor may be proved thereby, is not to be required of any man, that it should be believed as an article of the faith" — the 39 Articles of Religion, Article 6. Through prayer and the guidance of the Holy Spirit its teachings can be applied in daily life. But Anglicans themselves disagree how to read it: the full range of fundamentalist, conservative and liberal understandings is found inside the denomination — Leviticus calls homosexuality an abomination; liberals think that is out of date, conservatives think it stands.
  2. Tradition. The teachings of the Church Fathers, the early ecumenical councils, the saints and senior Anglican bishops offer insight and wisdom on how problems have been solved before — as long as those teachings agree with the contents of the Bible. "The Church hath the power to decree rites or ceremonies and authority in controversies of Faith. And yet it is not lawful for the Church to ordain anything that is contrary to God's Word written" — the 39 Articles of Religion, Article 20. Charles Gore called Tradition a "living witness to a once-spoken voice".
  3. Reason. Thought and reflection let Christians evaluate and measure Scripture and Tradition and apply them to today's problems. "Reason increases and enlarges human understanding of divine revelation through its own workings, so long as reason is used in humble dependence upon the God who gave it" — Michael Ramsey (1904–1988), former Archbishop of Canterbury. Reason is what lets Anglicans reject "women should be silent in church" as the product of a patriarchal age.

Why three and not one. Using the three in tandem is meant to give a guide that is both fixed and dynamic, preventing complacency and arrogance: having to refer to the Bible, to respected Christians of the past, and to your own rationality forces a Christian constantly to review their relationship with and knowledge of God, and how best to act in each situation. That is the sentence to use if a question asks why the triad is a strength.

The key beliefs are set out in the 39 Articles of Religion, and the distinctive ideas are a ready-made list: that the Bible contains the core of all Christian faith and thought; loyalty to the way of worship and life first set out in the Book of Common Prayer; celebration of the two sacraments ordained by Jesus, Baptism and the Eucharist; a system of Church order from ancient times focused on the ordained ministry of bishop, priest and deacon; a firm commitment to the ministry of the whole people of God, lay and ordained; and Scripture, Tradition and Reason held together in creative tension.

Two things the Church of England rejects: the authority of the Pope — the monarch is Supreme Governor of the Church of England, appointing archbishops and bishops — and the primacy of Natural Law over Scripture. Its origins explain why: it is part of the Anglican Church and separated from the Roman Catholic Church in the sixteenth century for political and doctrinal reasons, the main one being the rejection of papal authority, aiming at a middle way between Roman Catholicism and Puritanism. It embraces Protestant themes such as the importance of Scripture while keeping the Roman Catholic respect for the ancient Church and the Church Fathers (unless they contradict the Bible), and it accepts the decisions of the first four ecumenical councils on the Trinity and the Incarnation. Like the Roman Catholic and Orthodox Churches it ordains bishops, priests and deacons. That middle position produces real internal variety: some Church of England communities follow Roman Catholicism closely (High Church) while others are much closer to evangelical Protestantism (Low Church) — naming that distinction is an easy mark on "differences among Christians".

Structure, in order, because "outline two forms of leadership" is a real 4-marker: the monarch as Supreme Governor; the Archbishop of Canterbury, Primate of All England; the Archbishop of York; diocesan bishops, who oversee the priests in their dioceses; priests or vicars, who run parishes; deacons and the laity, who assist.

The individual conscience as a source of authority

Christians disagree about what the conscience actually is, and that is the whole question — four named positions, two for and two against trusting it.

Account of the conscienceWhoThe claim

The example of Jesus is the better moral guide

Bonhoeffer's scepticism has a context worth one sentence: he had watched a large number of Protestant churches in Germany accept the Nazi regime and its atrocities, which is a powerful piece of evidence that private conscience is not a reliable guide. His alternative is Christ: "Who is pure in heart? Only those who have surrendered their hearts completely to Jesus that he may reign in them alone… their hearts are not ruled by their conscience, but by the will of Jesus."

Where it bites in practice: Roman Catholic teaching holds that contraception is wrong, but a Catholic couple who do not yet want children may follow their conscience against it; the Church holds homosexual acts to be sinful, and gay Catholics may judge that teaching outdated on grounds of conscience. Catholic teaching is that a conscience must always be obeyed — and also that a good conscience will be in line with the Bible and Natural Law, and can be distorted by sin.

The ordained ministry, the laity and leadership in the community

Spec 1.1 · "Differences among Christians in their attitudes to the roles of the ordained ministry, the laity and religious leadership in local communities"

  • Ordination — the ceremony appointing ministers; the Holy Spirit passes by the "laying on of hands".
  • A sacrament for Catholics — Protestant groups do not regard the ceremony itself as important.
  • Episcopal and Free Churches — Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican have bishops; Methodists, Baptists and Quakers have ministers.
  • Apostolic succession — Matthew 16:18 makes Peter the rock, so every bishop of Rome inherits his authority.
  • Priesthood of all believers — Luther: clergy have no unique powers, so most Protestants elect rather than ordain.
  • The laity — little say in Catholic governance; active in worship and government in Protestant churches.

More detail

Ordination is the ceremony by which a church appoints its ministers, and the Holy Spirit is believed to pass to the ordinand through the "laying on of hands". How much that ceremony matters is the divide: for Roman Catholics it is a sacrament; Protestant groups do not regard the ceremony as important.

Start from the history, because it explains the split. When Christianity began there was only one Church: local groups were led by elders, later called priests, with overseers called bishops; in large cities there were senior bishops, and over time the bishop of Rome became the most senior of all. Schisms and disagreements over faith and leadership produced the denominations.

They fall into two families. Traditional or Episcopal denominations — the Roman Catholic Church, the Orthodox Churches and the Anglican Church (which includes the Church of England) — all have bishops, believed to keep Christians on the right path and the correct teachings, and to provide a link with the first Christians. Free Churches or Non-Conformist denominations — the Methodist, Baptist, United Reformed and Pentecostal churches and the Society of Friends (Quakers) — tend to have ministers who look after pastoral needs, and prefer to refer to the Bible for guidance.

The significance of the baptism — four marks' worth

1. It is Jesus' commissioning — the start of his public ministry, authorised by God.

2. It is proof that he is the Son of God: the voice from heaven gives him the Father's blessing.

3. He is anointed Messiah by the Spirit, shown by the dove, and the torn heavens announce that the Kingdom is imminent.

4. Father, Son and Spirit act together in the outreach to humanity — and Isaiah 42:1 already points to the cross.

More detail

The four Gospels, written 35–60 years after Jesus' death, are the main sources — so they must be used with care, because they were written by believers to persuade. Jesus' existence is also attested outside Christianity: the Roman historian Tacitus records that Christians were named after "Christus", who "suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of… Pontius Pilatus"; the Jewish historian Josephus records that "about this time there lived Jesus, a wise man".

The birth stories, in Matthew and Luke only, say Jesus was born to a virgin, Mary, in 4 BC in Bethlehem in Judea, and raised in the Galilean village of Nazareth. Mary conceived through the Holy Spirit — the incarnation, which literally means becoming in flesh: the Son of God, the second person of the Trinity, became man, so Christians hold that Jesus has two natures, divine and human, in perfect union — the Hypostatic Union. He was born among the animals in a stable and visited by angels, shepherds and magi. Almost nothing of his childhood is recorded; the next key event is his baptism, at about thirty years old.

The theology behind the birth, which is what a 6-marker on its significance wants: Jesus is the ultimate revelation of God, fully human and fully divine, so his disciples could encounter God more completely than ever before, and Christians today can do the same by reading the Gospels. It reveals God's omnipotence, since he is able to reveal himself as a human being, and his omnibenevolence, since the reason he did so was love. It also reveals the predicament of humanity: sin placed a barrier between God and humanity, which lost the divine–human fellowship it had at creation, and only God can pay off the debt of human sin — so the incarnation shows that salvation is possible, because by accepting Jesus's sacrifice atonement with God is available.

  • God's love for the world — he sent his Son to save it from sin.
  • God identifies with the poor and marginalised — a stable, not a palace.
  • The gifts of the magi point to Christ's authority (gold) and spirituality (frankincense), while the myrrh hints at his death and sacrifice — so joy and sadness sit together in the story.
  • The incarnation means God revealed both how humans should behave and his own nature — above all his omnibenevolence.

The baptism marks the start of his public ministry. Jesus went to the river Jordan to his cousin John the Baptist, who had been announcing the imminence of the messiah (God's anointed) and calling on people to repent of their sins and be baptised to mark a new way of life. Three things happened: Jesus saw the heavens open, he saw the Spirit of God descend on him like a dove, and he heard a voice from heaven.

Each of the three has a meaning drawn from the Old Testament, and this is where the marks are:

  1. The heavens open. The Jews had been longing for God's kingdom to arrive. In Isaiah 64:1 the prophet asks God "why don't you tear the sky apart and come down?" — at the baptism God has done exactly that, so the Kingdom of God is imminent.
  2. The Spirit of God descends on Jesus. The prophets had received God's messages through his Spirit, and since the last of the prophets the Spirit had been very quiet; Jews believed he would return with the messiah. By descending on Jesus in a kind of anointing, the Spirit identifies him as the messiah.
  3. The voice from heaven. God himself affirms Jesus's status as his Son and authorises his ministry. Calling him "Son" alludes to the coronation of kings in the Old Testament — "You are my son; today I have become your father" (Psalm 2:7) — but the words also carry darker connotations, because they recall the suffering servant of Isaiah: "Here is my servant, whom I uphold, my chosen, in whom my soul delights; I have put my spirit upon him; he will bring forth justice to the nations" (Isaiah 42:1). In Isaiah the suffering servant dies for the people — so the voice hints at the commission God has given Jesus, and at how it ends.

More detail

Immediately after his baptism Jesus spent forty days and nights fasting in the wilderness, and the devil tempted him three times. Learn all three, because "outline two temptations" is a stock 4-marker.

  1. Turn stones into bread — the temptation of material need and greed.
  2. Throw himself from the highest point of the temple and let the angels catch him — the temptation to prove his identity by spectacle, and to court popularity.
  3. Be given all the kingdoms of the world if he bowed down and worshipped the devil — the temptation of power.

Jesus resisted all three. The significance:

  • He decided what kind of ministry his would be: not material greed, popularity or power, but the service of God.
  • He is a model for Christians, who must resist temptation too — which is why Christians reflect on the temptations during Lent.
  • He shows superhuman ability to resist, supporting his divinity.
  • He is a second Adam: the first Adam could not resist temptation, Jesus could.
  • It shows the devil to be real and at work in the world.

More detail

The spec asks for exactly two, so learn exactly these two and what each is used to prove.

First, the general point, because it earns a mark on any miracles question: the miracles are "deeds of power" showing the might and authority of Jesus, performed in response to faith, which encourages Christians to trust God's compassion. Jesus performed healings and exorcisms, nature miracles and resurrections, and for Christians these are tangible signs of the nature of God's Kingdom — a place with no death, disease, evil or overwhelming forces of nature.

The healing of the leper (Mark 1:40–44). "A leper came to him begging him, and kneeling he said to him, 'If you choose, you can make me clean.' Moved with pity, Jesus stretched out his hand and touched him, and said to him, 'I do choose. Be made clean!'" Jesus then sternly warned him to say nothing to anyone but to show himself to the priest and make the offering Moses commanded. The context is the point: leprosy was incurable at the time, covering the skin in sores, wasting the muscles and changing the person's appearance completely before killing them, which could take anything from 9 to 30 years. Leviticus 13 and 14 set out the rules: lepers were unclean outcasts, outside God's Chosen People, living isolated on the margins and required to announce their presence in public so nobody touched them. The leper breaks the rules by approaching Jesus — and Jesus breaks them too, by touching him. So the story shows Jesus is powerful (he need only say the word), compassionate, a helper of outcasts, obedient to the Mosaic law (he sends the man to the priest, which allows him back into the community) and yet greater than that law — the Law could only label a person clean, Jesus makes them clean.

The exorcism of Legion (Mark 5:1–20). This is the first time Jesus has been out of Palestine: he crossed the Sea of Galilee to the Decapolis, the Greek city states, and the gentile country of the Gerasenes. He met a man living among the tombs, violent and dangerous, whom his community had forced to live in a burial ground — demons were believed to reside by tombs, so they probably thought he belonged there — and who had wrenched apart the shackles and chains used to restrain him, howling night and day and bruising himself with stones. Seeing Jesus, the man ran and bowed down and shouted "What have you to do with me, Jesus, Son of the Most High God?", recognising his power and authority; asked his name he answered "My name is Legion; for we are many" — a Roman legion could be as many as 6,000 soldiers, so the word implies both a huge number and a savage, destructive power. Jesus sent the demons into a herd of about two thousand pigs, which rushed down the steep bank into the sea and drowned, proving the exorcism. The local people, frightened by something beyond their control, begged Jesus to leave. The healed man, now "clothed and in his right mind", asked to become a disciple; instead Jesus told him "Go home to your friends, and tell them how much the Lord has done for you", and he proclaimed it throughout the Decapolis. Significance: Jesus' mission extends beyond the Jews to the gentiles; he has awesome power; and the battle between good and evil continues in his ministry — he is a messianic warrior fighting Satan.

The resurrection accounts vary from Gospel to Gospel, and that variation is itself worth a sentence in a 10-marker. With the Sabbath close, Joseph of Arimathea asked Pilate for permission to bury Jesus; the body was taken down, wrapped in a linen sheet and placed in a tomb sealed with a stone. The women who had witnessed the death went with him but had no time to anoint the body with spices and perfume, so they had to go home and wait for the Sabbath to end. Returning very early on Sunday morning they found the stone rolled away and the tomb empty, and were greeted by two men in shining clothes who told them Jesus had risen — in Matthew, an angel says "Do not be afraid… He has risen… He has gone ahead of you to Galilee just as he said." They rushed to the eleven disciples, some of whom thought they were hysterical with grief; Peter ran to the tomb and found it empty, exactly as the women had said.

The appearances: two disciples walking to Emmaus were joined by Jesus and did not recognise him, telling him about the recent events in Jerusalem; they invited him to stay and share a meal, and recognised him only when he sat at table and blessed the bread — a deliberate echo of the Last Supper. Thomas was out when Jesus first appeared and refused to believe unless he saw for himself; eight days later Jesus appeared again with Thomas present, and Thomas acknowledged him — "My Lord and my God." Jesus blessed all those who had not seen and yet believed: "Blessed are those who have not seen and yet believe" (John 20:29). There are also appearances in the Upper Room and at the lakeside while the disciples were fishing.

  • It shows the justice of God — the innocent victim is not left dead.
  • Jesus had predicted it, so it shows he was right about everything else.
  • It promises believers an afterlife: God's triumph over the powers of sin and death.
  • The women are the first witnesses — women were disciples too.

Cheap grace and costly grace — The Cost of Discipleship, 1937

Written while he was teaching at Finkenwalde, the book is a set of reflections on Jesus's teaching in the Sermon on the Mount: a call to more faithful and radical obedience to Christ, and a severe rebuke of comfortable Christianity.

Cheap grace is a cover for ethical laxity — the notion that God will forgive you whatever you do, as long as you keep going to church and saying sorry every week.

"Cheap grace is preaching forgiveness without requiring repentance, baptism without church discipline, communion without confession… Cheap grace is grace without discipleship, grace without the cross, grace without Jesus Christ, living and incarnate."

Costly grace is the daily task of applying the teachings of Scripture, combining faith with action and works with belief: a person has to stand up and be counted to be a real disciple of Christ.

"Costly grace is the gospel which must be sought again and again and again, the gift which must be asked for, the door at which a man must knock… It is costly because it costs a man his life, and it is grace because it gives a man the only true life. It is costly because it condemns sin, and grace because it justifies the sinner. Above all, it is costly because it cost God the life of his Son."

Used to argue that real discipleship means faith plus action — and his own death is the evidence that he meant it. If you have room for only one clause, use "it costs a man his life… it gives a man the only true life".

More detail

In Mark 1:16–20 Jesus calls four fishermen to follow him, challenging them with his first words of public ministry to change their way of life: they were no longer to catch fish but to become fishers of men.

In total he called twelve men as his closest disciples, chosen to help him and to continue the work after his ascension. They are the apostles — "ones who are sent out". He probably chose twelve to represent the twelve tribes of Israel, which suggests he was starting a renewed Israel to spread the news of God's Kingdom to all people. His teaching on discipleship is demanding, and the exam question is usually how far Christians should take it literally.

Pulling the teaching together, discipleship involves: a new start — Nicodemus must be born again; a new family, either in place of blood ties or as a worldwide network of believers; a life of service and self-sacrifice; being salt and light, a force for good guiding people to God; renunciation of worldly possessions for spiritual treasure; and the possibility of danger. The reward is promised: "You who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel" (Matthew 19:28).

Why Jesus matters to Christians today. He is God incarnate — fully God and fully human, Son of God and Messiah; the way to salvation — "I am the way, the truth and the life" (John 14:6); a moral example, so that the question "what would Jesus do?" settles behaviour; a guarantor of the afterlife, because his resurrection promises believers theirs; and the one who will return to judge the world. His impact runs wider: he set in motion the movement that became Christianity, though some argue St Paul was the real founder; Western law and ethics rest on his teaching; and he inspired much of the greatest art and music, such as Bach's Passions.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906–1945) is the "one other significant Christian". Born in Germany in 1906, he became a teacher of theology in Berlin and a minister in the Lutheran Church, and was deeply shaken when Hitler became Chancellor in 1933, above all by Nazi antisemitism. He denounced "blind obedience to a leader" on the radio and tried to get his fellow Berlin ministers to strike by refusing to conduct funerals, hoping to force the Nazis to soften their policies; he failed. On antisemitism his line was "You must remember Jesus was a Jew."

The timeline, which is what a 6-marker needs:

  • 1934 — the German Free Churches met to renounce any attempt by Hitler to make the Churches serve him rather than God, producing the Barmen Declaration: "We repudiate the false teaching that the Church can and must recognise yet other happenings and powers, personalities and truths as divine revelation alongside this one Word of God." Out of that meeting came the Confessing Church, whose ministers were acting unofficially and illegally under Nazi law. Bonhoeffer became its leading spokesman, insisting that Jews who had converted to Christianity were entitled to the same rights in the church as anyone else — which put him at odds with many other church leaders. He found a sympathetic advocate abroad in GKA Bell, bishop of Chichester.
  • 1935–1937 — he ran an underground training seminary for Confessing ministers at Finkenwalde, insisting his students spend half an hour a day in silent prayer and meditation on the scriptures, because they would need a thorough grounding in the spiritual life and the support of a loving community. The Gestapo closed it in 1937; he carried on teaching in an old vicarage and a country house until those too were closed in 1940.
  • 1939 — on a lecture tour in the USA he was welcomed with open arms, but when the Second World War began he felt he had to return: "I will have no right to participate in the reconstruction of Christian life in Germany after the war if I do not share the tribulations of this time with my people."
  • Back in Germany he grew disillusioned with the Confessing Church, which he felt lacked the courage of its convictions: many of his students had not refused military service even though they had the right to as Christians, and few Lutheran pastors had objected to swearing an oath of loyalty to Hitler.
  • Until then he had been a pacifist, opposing the Nazis by religious action and moral persuasion. He changed tactics and joined a group of conspirators against Hitler, working for the resistance under cover of employment in Germany's Military Intelligence Department (the Abwehr) and serving as a double agent: supposedly collecting information while travelling to church conferences, in fact helping Jews escape. In May 1942 he flew to Sweden to meet Bishop Bell and tried, through him, to convey the conspirators' proposals for a negotiated peace to the British government; the Allies' "unconditional surrender" policy ended that hope.
  • 1943 — the conspirators' bomb plot against Hitler failed. Bonhoeffer and his brother-in-law Hans von Dohnanyi were arrested and held in Tegel Prison to await trial. A second attempt in July 1944 also failed; documents linking him to the plot were found and he was moved to Flossenbürg concentration camp. On 9 April 1945 he and six other prisoners were hanged in the woods nearby.

The links back to Jesus are what earn the marks on "explain how one Christian has put Jesus' teaching on discipleship into action": Bonhoeffer was willing to accept all people, fighting racism and discrimination; he stood up for justice and the values of the Kingdom of God; he loved his neighbour — raising the hard question of whether that can involve violence; he was prepared to take up the cross of Christ by choosing the harder route; and he was willing to sacrifice his own life to bring about change for the better, which answers to John's "Greater love has no man than this, that he lay down his life for his friends."

02Cheat sheet

The must-knows

Three attitudes to the Bible: fundamentalist (literal, inerrant) · conservative (inspired, Gospels accurate) · liberal (contains God's word, read in context).

Church of England authority: Scripture, Tradition, Reason — in creative tension. Rejects the authority of the Pope.

Four accounts of conscience: inner voice of God (Paul, Aquinas) · self-restraint (Butler) · super-ego (Freud) · false idol (Bonhoeffer).

Jesus' life, in order: birth · baptism · temptations · healings · triumphal entry · Temple cleansing · Last Supper · Gethsemane · trials · crucifixion · resurrection · ascension.

Two healings: the leper (greater than the Law) and Legion (for gentiles too).

Bonhoeffer: cheap grace vs costly grace; Barmen Declaration 1934; Confessing Church; Finkenwalde seminary 1935–37; Tegel Prison 1943; hanged at Flossenbürg, 9 April 1945.

The Bible in numbers: 39 Old Testament books + 27 New Testament = 66; canon agreed AD 367; written c.1200 BC – AD 200; biblia = "books"; 21 epistles; Roman Catholics add the Apocrypha.

The baptism, three events and three meanings: heavens torn open (the Kingdom is imminent — Isaiah 64:1) · Spirit descends (he is the messiah) · voice from heaven (he is the Son — Psalm 2:7 — and the suffering servant — Isaiah 42:1). Reference: Mark 1:9–11.

Three named ethical theories: Kant and the categorical imperative (duty) · Bentham and the Greatest Happiness Principle (consequences) · Fletcher's situation ethics (the most loving thing).

Three words from the cross: "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34) · "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34) · "It is finished" (John 19:30).

The seven Catholic sacraments: baptism, confirmation, holy communion, confession, marriage, ordination, unction.

The 10-mark frame

  • Restate the statement and say what would have to be true for it to hold — "The Bible is the literal word of God" turns entirely on what inspired is taken to mean.
  • The strongest case for, with Christian teaching and scripture attached: name the group that holds it — fundamentalists, Roman Catholics, Aquinas — and quote the verse (2 Timothy 3:16–17, Matthew 16:18, Romans 2:15–16).
  • The strongest case against, also with teaching and scripture of its own rather than bare doubt: liberal Christians on Genesis and 1 Timothy 2, Freud on the conscience, Bonhoeffer on conscience as idolatry.
  • Where Christians disagree with each other — fundamentalist against liberal on the Bible; Roman Catholic against Anglican on the Pope, and on Scripture, Tradition and Reason. The spec calls this divergence, and the paragraph that handles it is what lifts an answer into the top band.
  • A conclusion that decides, naming the reason that decided it and giving your own opinion. A conclusion that says both sides have a point scores nothing.

The sentence starters that earn the evaluation marks: "A fundamentalist Christian would argue…" · "However, a liberal Christian would respond…" · "Roman Catholic teaching goes further, because…" · "This argument is weaker, because…" · "On balance, I agree that… because…"

03Understanding it

U1

Why do Christians believe the Bible has authority at all, when it was physically written by human beings?

Show answer

Because of its source, not its authors. Christians believe it is the revelation of God's word — "All scripture is inspired by God" (2 Timothy 3:16). God is transcendent, completely unlike humanity, so he can only be known if he makes himself known; the Bible is that self-disclosure.

It also contains the record of the incarnation: in Jesus, God himself became human and taught people directly about his kingdom. That makes the Bible ontologically different from any other book.

The follow-up: human authorship is exactly what liberals use to limit that authority — contradictions such as the two creation stories in Genesis would be unworthy of an omniscient God, so the text must contain human hands as well as God's.

U2

What is the strongest objection to the fundamentalist view of the Bible — and how would a fundamentalist answer it?

Show answer

The objection: internal contradiction. Genesis contains two different creation accounts, and the Old Testament shows signs of heavy editing. An inerrant text dictated by an omniscient, omnipotent God should not contradict itself.

The reply: what look like contradictions are failures of human interpretation, not of the text. And the alternative has its own cost — if parts of the Bible are human commentary, who decides which parts? That dilutes the authority of the whole thing.

U3

The Church of England holds Scripture, Tradition and Reason "in creative tension". Why "tension", and what happens when they disagree?

Show answer

They can point in opposite directions. 1 Timothy says women should keep silent in church; Reason says that is the product of a patriarchal age and should be rejected. Leviticus calls homosexuality an abomination; liberal Anglicans read that as out of date, conservatives as still binding.

So the three are not a hierarchy with a tie-break at the top. That is a strength — it lets the Church apply ancient texts to new problems — and a weakness, because it means Anglicans genuinely disagree with each other on ethics.

U4

Bonhoeffer said "Conscience pretends to be the voice of God… man has become the origin of good and evil." Why would a Christian say that, when St Paul seems to say the opposite?

Show answer

Because of the Fall. Bonhoeffer held that original sin broke the connection between God and humanity, so what feels like God's voice is really self-knowledge. Treating your own judgement as God's is then idolatry — setting yourself up as God.

Paul's "law written on their hearts" (Romans 2:15) assumes the connection still holds. Bonhoeffer's alternative is external and checkable: look at the person and work of Christ, which cannot be bent to suit you in the way a private conscience can.

Freud reaches a similar destination by a different road — the super-ego is built from parental morals in infancy, so a conscience reflects your upbringing, not God. And Bonhoeffer had the evidence in front of him: he watched many German Protestant churches accept the Nazi regime and its atrocities, which is hard to square with the conscience being God's own voice.

U5

Why does Luther's "priesthood of all believers" lead to a completely different church structure, not just a different opinion about priests?

Show answer

If the clergy have no unique powers and every Christian can be a channel of God's word, then ordination is not a sacrament — so there is nothing to pass down through the laying on of hands and apostolic succession becomes unnecessary. Bishops, whose job is to guard correct teaching and link back to the first Christians, lose their reason to exist, and Protestants reject the office as non-Biblical.

The knock-on effects run right through the building and the week: ministers are elected rather than ordained, may be male or female and may marry; the laity share in both worship and governance; and the minister's main task is preaching the word rather than performing sacraments.

U6

Why do Christians reflect on the temptations of Jesus specifically during Lent?

Show answer

Lent mirrors the event: forty days of fasting, matching Jesus' forty days and nights in the wilderness after his baptism. Christians give something up as their own small act of self-denial.

The content matches too. In the wilderness Jesus worked out what sort of ministry his would be, refusing material greed, popularity and power in favour of serving God. Lent is when Christians ask the same question of themselves — how they can best serve God in the coming year — which is why many join Bible study groups during it.

U7

Which matters more to Christianity, the crucifixion or the resurrection? Set out the case on each side.

Show answer

For the crucifixion: it is the sacrifice that atones for sin — "my blood of the covenant which is poured out for many". Without the death there is nothing to be raised from, and no new covenant. It is also where Jesus is most clearly a model of discipleship: suffering obedience, which is what Bonhoeffer meant by taking up your cross.

For the resurrection: St Paul is explicit that it is the load-bearing claim — if Christ has not been raised, "our proclamation has been in vain and your faith has been in vain" (1 Corinthians 15). It proves Jesus is the Son of God, vindicates his predictions, demonstrates God's triumph over sin and death, and guarantees believers an afterlife. Without it there would be no Christianity.

The honest answer is that the question is slightly false: the two are one event in Christian thought, and Easter celebrates both. But if forced, Paul's argument is the stronger one.

U8

Jesus' demands on his disciples — sell everything, leave your family, take up your cross — look impossible. How do Christians today justify not doing them literally?

Show answer

Three moves. First, context: the sayings were given to people who believed the Kingdom was arriving imminently, which would justify extreme action that is not required now. Second, interpretation: a liberal reading treats "let the dead bury their own dead" as deliberate hyperbole about priorities rather than an instruction about funerals. Third, substitution: "new family" is read as joining a worldwide network of believers, which extends your family rather than replacing it.

Bonhoeffer is the standing objection to all three. That is precisely his cheap grace — being a Christian in name only. His own answer was to take the harder route, and his execution in 1945 is why the challenge is hard to dismiss.

04Exam drill

How to answer each part — Paper 2

  • 4 marks — knowledge only. Two pieces of information, each with "a little development". If an example is asked for, give the example first and then its significance. Point + development = 2 marks each.
  • 6 marks — explain the significance of a person, event, belief or practice. Level marked, not point marked: two or three points explained in depth, drawing out the significance. An answer that only describes cannot reach the top level.
  • 10 marks — the evaluation. Draw on a number of viewpoints within Christianity, appraise the quality of each argument as you make it, consider both perspectives, give your own opinion with reasoned arguments, and finish with a balanced, justified conclusion.

Paper 2 questions come in threes: a 4-mark outline, a 6-mark explanation and a 10-mark evaluation. D1–D12 give four of each; D13–D15 add a fifth. The 6s and 10s are level marked, not point marked, so the mark points below are a target for coverage rather than a tick list.

D1

Show answer

1. One form of leadership is bishops 1; they oversee the priests working in their dioceses 1

2. Another form is the Archbishop of Canterbury 1; he oversees the work of all the other bishops as Primate of All England 1

Other acceptable pairs: the Pope, who has inherited the authority of St Peter; parish priests, who lead local congregations and give pastoral care; elected Protestant ministers or moderators; the laity as church wardens and members of the PCC.

D2

Outline two temptations experienced by Jesus in the wilderness. (4)

Show answer

1. Jesus was tempted to turn stones into bread 1; after forty days of fasting he was hungry and weak, so this was a temptation to use his power for material need 1

2. He was tempted to worship the devil 1; in return he was shown and promised authority over all the kingdoms of the world 1

The third: throw himself from the highest point of the temple so the angels would catch him, proving his identity by spectacle.

D3

Show answer

1. Some Christians regard the conscience as the inner voice of God 1; St Paul likened it to God's law written on our hearts, and Aquinas held that whoever acts against his conscience sins 1

2. Other Christians regard it as a false idol 1; Bonhoeffer argued the Fall distorted the human conscience, so treating it as God's voice is idolatry and Christians should follow the example of Christ instead 1

D4

2. Jesus exorcised Legion, a man possessed by many demons in the gentile country of the Gerasenes 1; he cast the demons into a herd of pigs, which stampeded into the sea, and the man was restored to sanity 1

D5

Explain why the Bible is important to Christians. (6)

Show answer

First point — its source. Christians believe the Bible is the revelation of God's word, "inspired by God" (2 Timothy 3:16) 1; because God is transcendent he can only be known if he makes himself known, so without Scripture Christians could not know his nature or his expectations 1

D7

Explain the significance of Jesus' resurrection. (6)

Show answer

First point — it proves who he was. The empty tomb and the appearances show Jesus to be the Son of God incarnate, not merely an executed teacher 1; he had predicted his resurrection, so it vindicates everything else he claimed, and the Roman centurion's verdict at the cross — "Truly this man was the Son of God" — is confirmed 1

Second point — it defeats sin and death. It shows God's triumph over the powers of sin and death, and his justice in not leaving an innocent victim dead 1; Christians believe Jesus' sacrifice has provided them with atonement with God 1

Third point — it promises believers an afterlife. St Paul calls Christ "the first fruits" (1 Corinthians 15) 1; so death does not mark the end of a Christian's existence — they too will be resurrected at the End Time, which is why Easter is the most important festival of the year and why Christianity would not exist without it 1

D8

Explain how one Christian has put Jesus' teaching on discipleship into action. (6)

Show answer

Name the Christian first. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, a German Lutheran pastor and theologian, executed in 1945 1

Taking up the cross. He returned to Germany when Hitler took power in 1933 rather than stay safely abroad, denouncing "blind obedience to a leader" on the radio 1; this is Mark 8:34 in practice — denying himself and taking up his cross 1

Costly, not cheap, grace. In The Cost of Discipleship he rebuked comfortable Christianity, contrasting cheap grace — being a Christian in name only — with costly grace, which combines faith with action 1; he lived it by helping write the Barmen Declaration, leading the Confessing Church and running an underground seminary when his own was closed 1

Love of neighbour to the end. He helped Jews escape Nazi oppression and joined the resistance, and was executed for it — answering John's "Greater love has no man than this, that he lay down his life for his friends" 1

D9

"The Bible is the literal word of God."

Discuss this statement, considering the arguments for and against. In your answer you should include: reference to teachings; other (divergent) points of view, either within the religion or from other religions; your opinion using reasoned arguments; and a balanced conclusion. (10)

Level marked in the real paper, so treat this as a plan for coverage rather than a tick list.

Arguments for

Fundamentalist Christians hold the Bible to be the revealed word of God, dictated to the prophets — "All scripture is God-breathed and useful for teaching" (2 Timothy) 1

In the New Testament God himself became man and revealed his expectations directly, so the Gospels are first-hand divine testimony 1

The content could only come from God: he is transcendent, completely different from humanity, so we can only know about him if he makes himself known 1

Arguments against

The text contains contradictions, such as the two different creation stories in Genesis — flaws unworthy of an omniscient and omnipotent being 1

Liberal Christians point to signs of heavy editing, particularly in the Old Testament, and conclude the Bible mixes divine revelation with human interpretation and commentary 1

Conservative Christians take a middle route: the writers were inspired but added their own personality and context, so the Bible may err on factual matters while remaining true about God and ethics 1

Christian teaching to appraise with

The liberal view explains the composition of the text, but it dilutes the authority of Scripture and leaves no way to decide which parts came from God and which from human hands 1

Even Christians who reject a literal reading still treat the text as authoritative and requiring careful contemplation; the Holy Spirit is believed to guide interpretation 1

Conclusion

Many Christians believe the Bible is the literal word of God on the strength of God's nature and the text's authority, but that view struggles to explain the contradictions within it 1

Given that division of opinion within Christianity itself, I am less inclined to accept that the Bible is the literal word of God — though I accept it is authoritative 1

D10

"The resurrection of Jesus is more important than his crucifixion."

Discuss this statement, considering the arguments for and against. (10)

Show answer

Level marked — a plan, not a tick list.

Arguments for

St Paul makes the resurrection the load-bearing claim: if Christ has not been raised, "our proclamation has been in vain and your faith has been in vain" (1 Corinthians 15) 1

It is the proof of Jesus' identity as Son of God — a crucifixion alone would show only that a teacher had been executed, which Roman Judea produced routinely 1

It is what promises believers an afterlife: Christ as "the first fruits" means Christians too will be resurrected at the End Time 1

Arguments against

The crucifixion is the sacrifice that atones for sin — "my blood of the covenant which is poured out for many" — so it is the act that actually saves 1

Without the death there is nothing to rise from; the resurrection depends on the crucifixion, not the other way round 1

The cross, not the empty tomb, is the model of discipleship — "take up their cross and follow me" (Mark 8:34), the costly grace Bonhoeffer lived and died by 1

Christian teaching to appraise with

Christian worship treats them as one event: Holy Week runs from Good Friday to Easter Sunday, and the Eucharist recalls the sacrifice while Easter celebrates the rising 1

The cross is the universal Christian symbol, which suggests the death is not the junior partner in Christian devotion 1

Conclusion

The statement forces apart two halves of a single event, and Christians do not experience them separately 1

But if a judgement is required, Paul's argument decides it: the crucifixion without the resurrection would be a tragedy rather than a salvation, so I agree the resurrection carries more weight 1

D11

"The individual conscience is the only moral guide a Christian needs."

Discuss this statement, considering the arguments for and against. (10)

Show answer

Level marked — a plan, not a tick list.

Arguments for

Many Christians hold the conscience to be the inner voice of God; St Paul speaks of God's law "written on their hearts" (Romans 2:15), which makes it divine instruction delivered personally 1

Aquinas held that "he who acts against his conscience sins", so it is not optional — it must be obeyed 1

It covers situations Scripture never addresses, and Catholic practice shows it in use: a couple may follow their conscience over the Church's teaching on contraception 1

Arguments against

Bonhoeffer argued the Fall distorted the conscience, so treating it as God's voice is idolatry — "Conscience pretends to be the voice of God… man has become the origin of good and evil" 1

Freud explains it as the super-ego, formed in infancy from parents' morals, which makes it a product of upbringing rather than revelation and explains why people's moral compasses differ 1

Catholic teaching itself holds that a conscience can be distorted by sin, and must be checked against the Bible and Natural Law — so it cannot be the only guide 1

Christian teaching to appraise with

Christians have other authorities available: for the Church of England, Scripture, Tradition and Reason; for Roman Catholics, the Magisterium — and these exist precisely because private judgement is thought insufficient 1

Bonhoeffer's alternative is stronger than a bare objection, because the example of Christ is external and checkable in a way a private conscience is not 1

Conclusion

The conscience is clearly an important guide — most Christians would say God gave it, and acting against it is itself wrong 1

But since it can be distorted and varies between people, it needs the check of Scripture, Church teaching and reason; so I disagree that it is the only guide a Christian needs 1

D12

"Ordained ministers are important for all Christians."

Discuss this statement, considering the arguments for and against. (10)

Show answer

Level marked — a plan, not a tick list.

Arguments for

For Roman Catholics ordination is a sacrament: the laying on of hands passes the Holy Spirit and links the priest back to St Peter through apostolic succession 1

Only an ordained priest can perform the seven sacraments, above all Holy Communion — so without him Catholics are cut off from the ordinary means of grace 1

Clergy lead the whole local community, not only the congregation: worship and festivals, baptisms, marriages and funerals, school assemblies, charity work, ecumenical and inter-faith work, and speaking up on local injustice 1

Arguments against

Martin Luther argued for a "priesthood of all believers": clergy have no unique powers and every Christian can be a channel of God's word 1

Most Protestant ministers are elected rather than ordained, and Protestants reject the office of bishop as non-Biblical, so the ceremony itself is not treated as important 1

Quakers hold no ordained ministry at all and worship without one, which shows it is not strictly necessary for Christian worship 1

Christian teaching to appraise with

Even Protestant churches that reject sacramental ordination still appoint ministers to preach the word, administer Communion and lead the community — so leadership of some form is near-universal, even where ordination is not 1

But a bigger lay role follows from the theology, not from convenience: where the Bible is held to contain all a Christian needs, the person who explains it matters less than the text 1

Conclusion

Ordained ministers are essential for some Christians — Roman Catholics and Anglicans — because the sacraments require them 1

But "all Christians" is too strong: Protestant and especially Quaker practice shows Christianity can function without them, so I disagree with the statement as worded while accepting leadership itself is important 1

D14

Explain why the baptism of Jesus is important for Christians. (6)

Show answer

Level marked — two or three points, each developed into significance.

First point — the heavens are torn open. In Mark 1:9–11 Jesus sees heaven being torn open as he comes up out of the water 1; the Jews had been longing for God's kingdom, and in Isaiah 64:1 the prophet asks God why he does not tear the sky apart and come down — so Christians read this as God doing exactly that, announcing that the Kingdom of God is imminent 1

Second point — the Spirit descends. The Spirit descends on him like a dove 1; the prophets had received God's messages through his Spirit and it had been silent since the last of them, and Jews believed it would return with the messiah, so descending on Jesus is an anointing that identifies him as the messiah 1

Third point — the voice from heaven. God declares "You are my Son, whom I love; with you I am well pleased", affirming Jesus's status and authorising his ministry, which is why Christians treat the baptism as his commissioning 1; the words echo the coronation of kings in Psalm 2:7 but also the suffering servant of Isaiah 42:1, who dies for the people — so the baptism already points to the cross 1

Taking the three events in order and attaching an Old Testament text to each is what lifts this out of narrative and into significance.

D15

"Jesus is an impossible example to follow."

Discuss this statement, considering the arguments for and against. In your answer you should include: reference to teachings; other (divergent) points of view, either within the religion or from other religions; your opinion using reasoned arguments; and a balanced conclusion. (10)

Show answer

Level marked — a plan, not a tick list.

Arguments for

His demands are extreme: "If any want to become my followers, let them deny themselves and take up their cross and follow me" (Mark 8:34–35), and he told the rich young man to sell everything he owned and give the money to the poor (Mark 10:21–22), which the man could not do 1

He asks for the abandonment of ordinary obligations — "Follow me, and let the dead bury their own dead" (Matthew 8:21–22) — and the Parable of the Tenants (Mark 12:1–12) warns that disciples may be persecuted and even killed 1

He resisted all three temptations in the wilderness, which Christians take as evidence of a superhuman ability to resist, and he was sinless, so no human being starts from where he started 1

Arguments against

The incarnation means he was fully human: the Passion shows him afraid in Gethsemane, sweating blood and praying "let this cup pass from me", and crying out "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" — so he experienced exactly what his followers experience 1

He was tempted in the same ways as anyone else and simply did not give in, which makes him a second Adam and therefore a better role model than Adam, not an impossible one 1

Many of the sayings can be read in context: they were given to people who believed the Kingdom was arriving imminently, and "new family" can be read as joining a worldwide family of believers rather than abandoning your own 1

Christian teaching to appraise with

Christians are not asked to succeed unaided: forgiveness is available when they fail, the Holy Spirit strengthens believers, and Lent exists precisely as a limited, achievable imitation of the forty days in the wilderness 1

Bonhoeffer is the standing objection to softening the demands: calling them impossible is exactly his cheap grace, and his own execution in 1945 shows that costly grace can in fact be lived 1

Conclusion

Taken literally and in full the example is beyond most people, which is why most Christians interpret rather than imitate the hardest sayings 1

But "impossible" is too strong: the point of the incarnation is that Jesus was fully human and was tempted as we are, and Bonhoeffer shows the demands can be met — so I disagree with the statement, while accepting that very few Christians meet them in full 1
