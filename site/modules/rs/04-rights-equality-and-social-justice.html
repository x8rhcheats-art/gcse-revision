What human rights are

Spec 4.1 · Lesson 2, Revision Booklet 4 p.6

  • Human rights defined — basic freedoms everyone is entitled to; they are universal, inalienable, and indivisible or interdependent.
  • The Universal Declaration (1948) — written by the UN after the camps and Hiroshima; 30 articles, affirmed by 58 countries.
  • The four named rights — liberty (Article 3), equality before the law (7), freedom of religion (18), freedom of speech (19).
More detail
  • Rights carry duties — the right not to be tortured brings the duty to respect it; due legal process can remove rights.
  • Guidelines, not law — the Declaration is not itself enshrined in law, though many countries have since passed their own.
  • Abuse is widespread — Amnesty International and UNICEF report torture in over eighty countries and speech limited in seventy.

Human rights are basic freedoms to which all human beings are entitled, and they have three defining characteristics. The three characteristics are a reliable 3-marker.

  1. Universal — everyone has them.
  2. Inalienable — people are entitled to them regardless of nationality, language, religion or ethnic origin; they cannot be taken away.
  3. Indivisible and interdependent — all the different rights are needed together for human beings to flourish and participate in society.

Rights must be balanced with duties: if I have the right not to be tortured, I have a duty to respect other people's right not to be tortured. Rights can be removed as a result of due legal process — being sent to prison.

They were codified in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, produced by the United Nations in 1948 after the world had seen the Nazi concentration camps, the treatment of Japanese prisoners of war and Hiroshima and Nagasaki. It contains 30 articles, and 58 countries — including African, Asian and Latin American ones — assembled to affirm it. If someone believes their rights have been abused they can have their case heard in international courts of appeal. Crucially, the Declaration only offers guidelines and is not itself enshrined in law, although many countries have since passed laws of their own.

The history is also examinable, and a date is cheap evidence. Originally people had rights only through membership of a family or tribe. Then: 539 BC, Cyrus the Great frees Babylon's slaves and declares that all may choose their own religion — the Cyrus Cylinder is held to be the first declaration of human rights. 1215 Magna Carta makes the king subject to the law. 1628 the Petition of Right to Charles I. 1776 the US Declaration of Independence — life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness. 1789 the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. 1948 the Universal Declaration. 1953 the European Convention on Human Rights, enforceable at Strasbourg. 1998 the UK Human Rights Act, which brought those rights into UK law.

Abuse of human rights is the other half. Many countries that agreed to uphold the Declaration do not: people are "disappeared", tortured and executed for disagreeing with their governments, while those with power or money carry on criminal activity and the poor and uneducated have no recourse to a fair justice system. Amnesty International and UNICEF suggest torture occurs in over eighty countries, freedom of expression is limited in at least seventy, and slavery and forced labour are common in many.

The lesson's case study is from The Times, 26 July 2021: a 29-year-old woman, believed to be an evangelical Christian, wearing a Charlie Hebdo t-shirt was stabbed in the face at Speakers' Corner in Hyde Park by a man who fled the scene. Use it for the collision between freedom of speech (Article 19) and freedom of religion (Article 18) — the point being that two rights can conflict with each other, not only with the state.

Christianity and human rights

Spec 4.1 · Lesson 3, Revision Booklet 4 p.6–8

  • Three foundations — creation, stewardship and agape: everyone bears God's image, so rights are recognised rather than granted.
  • Oscar Romero — the Salvadorean archbishop shot dead at the altar in 1980 for defending the rights of the poor.
  • Desmond Tutu — the "voice of the voiceless", Nobel Peace Prize 1984, who called abusing someone made in God's image blasphemy.
More detail
  • Galatians 3:28 — "neither Jew nor Greek… neither male nor female": the verse that makes freedom from discrimination Christian.
  • Authority comes from God — not from a state law or an agreement between countries, which is the key contrast with non-religious views.
  • Not every article unconditionally — for some Christians rights hold only where they do not contradict Christian teaching, as with abortion.

Christians support human rights because of the nature and teachings of God — the doctrine of creation, stewardship and agape. Many Christians think the Universal Declaration reflects Christian principles.

The key difference from the non-religious case: Christians argue human rights get their authority from God, not merely because countries agreed a set of guidelines or a state passed a law. Every human being has worth and dignity because every human being has been created by God — the first two chapters of Genesis teach that God created humanity in his image, set apart from the rest of creation to be in a relationship with him and to care for it. "The human rights to life, freedom, community and self-determination mirror God's right to the human being because the human being is destined to be God's image in all conditions and relationships of life" — Jürgen Moltmann.

Agape is specifically commanded: "you shall love your neighbour as yourself. There is no other commandment greater than these" (Mark 12:30–31), and the Golden Rule — "whatever you wish that men would do to you, do so to them" (Matthew 7:12). Jesus set the example by treating outcasts with kindness, healing people with leprosy and healing the Canaanite woman's daughter (Matthew 15:21–28).

But Christians do not accept every article unconditionally: for some, rights are only valid if they do not contradict Christian teaching. A right to abortion, for example, collides with the sanctity of life, and conservative Christians dispute a right to same-sex marriage.

Christians who have fought for human rights. Followers of Liberation Theology hold that they have a duty to speak out against unjust governments and abuses of rights, and that a country run on structured violence should be challenged and changed to prevent continued oppression and poverty. Oscar Romero, a Roman Catholic archbishop in El Salvador, believed Christians must defend the weak and make a stand against social injustice; in his sermons he spoke about the rights of the poor and openly criticised the government. In 1980, while he was celebrating Mass, armed men burst into the cathedral and shot him dead in front of the altar.

Tutu (1931–2021) was born in Klerksdorp, Transvaal, and became the first Black Anglican Archbishop of both Cape Town and Johannesburg. Known as the "voice of the voiceless", he was an outspoken critic of apartheid and supported the economic boycott of South Africa, urging the UK government to use sanctions, while constantly encouraging reconciliation. He won the 1984 Nobel Peace Prize for his part in ending apartheid, and when Nelson Mandela became the nation's first Black president he appointed Tutu chairperson of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission. He stated his objective as "a democratic and just society without racial divisions", demanding equal civil rights for all, a common system of education and an end to forced deportation. His reasons were explicitly religious: "all are God's children", we are a "human family", and Jesus was a man.

Romero on why abusing rights is a religious offence

"There is no dichotomy between man and God's image. Whoever tortures a human being, whoever abuses a human being, whoever outrages a human being, abuses God."

— Oscar Romero. The sharpest one-line link between imago Dei and human rights, and a natural pair with Tutu's "blasphemy".

Desmond Tutu — the human rights quotations to learn

"I am not interested in picking up crumbs of compassion thrown from the table of someone who considers himself my master. I want the full menu of rights."

Used to argue that rights are owed, not granted as charity.

"When will we learn that human beings are of infinite value because they have been created in the image of God, and that it is a blasphemy to treat them as if they were less than this and to do so ultimately recoils on those who do this?"

The single best line for linking imago Dei to human rights: abusing rights is not merely unkind, it is blasphemy.

Non-religious views of human rights

Non-religious people also argue that rights are enshrined in law: it is a legal requirement in both international and national law to respect them, and the UK Human Rights Act 1998 asserts in Article 4 that "no one should be held in slavery or servitude". They add that all humans have reason and free will to determine their own future, and that nobody — not church nor state — should intervene in that.

Named non-religious campaigners.

  • Amnesty International, founded by Peter Benenson in 1961 after he learned of two Portuguese students imprisoned for seven years for raising their glasses in a toast to freedom in a Lisbon bar. He believed that if enough people protested together, governments could not get away with such intimidation. Its four aims: to free prisoners of conscience; to gain fair trials for political prisoners; to end torture, political killings and "disappearances"; and to abolish the death penalty throughout the world. Its methods are letter-writing, publicity and protest, plus practical help — persuading governments to let doctors visit prisoners, and raising money for food and clothing on release — and campaigning to change laws, including the international ban on torture and the abolition of the death penalty in the UK. It won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1977 and the United Nations Human Rights Prize in 1978.
  • Eleanor Roosevelt (1884–1962), wife of President Franklin D Roosevelt and called the "First Lady of the World" by President Truman. She advocated equal rights for women, African-Americans and Depression-era workers; when the Black singer Marian Anderson was denied Washington's Constitution Hall in 1939 because of her race, Roosevelt saw to it that she performed on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial instead. Appointed a UN delegate in 1946, as Chair of the United Nations Human Rights Commission she was instrumental in formulating the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, submitting it with the words that it "may well become the international Magna Carta for all men everywhere".

Eleanor Roosevelt on where rights begin

"Where, after all, do universal human rights begin? In small places, close to home — so close and so small that they cannot be seen on any maps of the world… Unless these rights have meaning there, they have little meaning anywhere."

Used to argue that rights are practised in the home, school and workplace before they mean anything in law — the standard answer to "rights are just paperwork".

Can human rights be restricted?

Spec 4.1 · Lesson 5, Revision Booklet 4 p.8

  • Article 29.2 — the Declaration itself allows limits, on two grounds: the rights of others, and public order and general welfare.
  • Accepted examples — CCTV, wartime censorship, conscription, rationing, prison, and 28-day detention under the 2006 Terrorism Act.
  • The Christian objection — worth comes from being created in God's image, so no one may limit the rights of a child of God.
More detail
  • The Utilitarian case — restriction is justified where more people benefit than suffer, such as torturing one terrorist to save many.
  • Three problems with the concept — cultural neo-imperialism, impracticality, and the charge that it stifles progress.
  • Nietzsche (1844–1900) — "slave morality" protects the weak against a "master morality" of power and ambition: sheep against tigers.

The Universal Declaration itself allows it: Article 29.2 states that human rights may be suspended in order to protect people. That single reference turns a vague answer into a precise one.

Situations the lesson lists: using the army to enforce a lockdown during a pandemic; imprisoning terrorist suspects without charge; preventing a face veil when giving testimony in court; torturing a terrorist suspect; censoring subversive newspapers; testing chemical agents on an unsuspecting public; preventing a known extremist from speaking publicly.

Accepted examples in practice: CCTV — limiting liberty, but a fair price for feeling safer. Wartime censorship, to preserve the secrecy of military plans, conscription, to keep the armed forces strong, and rationing. Detention of terrorists without arrest or trial — in Britain the 2006 Terrorism Act allowed police to detain terror suspects for up to 28 days without arrest, because evidence of terrorist activity is often on encrypted files and takes time to build into a case, while the risk to large numbers of people is high. Loss of liberty as punishment — prison, and the loss of the vote while a prisoner. And restraint in speech and religious practice so society can function: having freedom of speech does not mean using it to offend or abuse.

Gender bias, the growth of women's rights, and feminism

Spec 4.2 · Lesson 6, Revision Booklet 4 p.11

  • Six reasons rights grew — the suffragettes, the World Wars, contraception, the feminist movement, economic growth and education.
  • Mary Wollstonecraft (1792) — A Vindication of the Rights of Women argued educated women decide as capably as men.
  • Emma Watson's HeForShe (2014) — the modern example: her UN speech argued gender equality is men's issue too.
More detail
  • Gender bias defined — stereotypes about how the genders should behave, limiting their capabilities and so their opportunities and rights.
  • It cuts both ways — women get less pay and promotion; men stereotyped as emotionally strong are reluctant to ask for help.
  • The milestones — the vote at 30 in 1918, equal votes 1928, the Sex Discrimination Act 1975, the Equality Act 2010.

Gender bias, or sexism, means preconceived ideas or stereotypes about how the genders should behave and think, and placing limitations on their capabilities — which restricts their opportunities and rights. "Showing preference to one sex over the other" is the short version.

Two points that lift an answer above the obvious. First, discrimination can be negative (a group is disadvantaged) or positive (a group is treated more favourably for what are believed to be good reasons) — so positive discrimination is itself contested. Second, gender bias cuts both ways: typically women are offered fewer options, receive less pay and are more likely to be judged by appearance, and are overlooked for promotion in favour of less qualified men, while suffering "everyday sexism". But men may struggle to get jobs in roles perceived as feminine such as nursing and primary school teaching, and the stereotype that men are more confident and emotionally stronger damages men's mental wellbeing by making them reluctant to express emotion or voice insecurity.

The spec wants both the history of how women's rights grew and the reasons it happened, so learn the six factors as a list.

  1. The suffragettes — campaigned for women's vote before WWI.
  2. The World Wars — women were employed in all areas, and there was no turning back afterwards.
  3. Contraception — the pill gave women control over their bodies, and so freedom and choice. The Abortion Act had the same effect.
  4. The feminist movement — campaigning for equal rights since the 1960s.
  5. Economic growth — women work, and societies generally become more liberal as they grow richer.
  6. Education — greater access has opened up opportunities.

The movement began in the eighteenth century with Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797), who published A Vindication of the Rights of Women in 1792. Influenced by the French Revolution, she argued that if all men were entitled to liberty, equality and fraternity then so were women, and that educated women would be just as capable of decision-making as men. Harriet Taylor Mill (1807–1858) added that women needed job opportunities and the vote.

The campaign for female suffrage was taken up by suffragists, who used non-violent methods, and suffragettes, who thought violence permissible given the urgency — public civil disobedience, rallies and acts of vandalism as well as leaflets. Many were arrested and went on hunger strike in prison, and the government's "Cat and Mouse Act" (1912) let hunger strikers out to recover before rearresting them. The milestones:

During the two World Wars women filled the jobs normally done by men and showed they could be more than wives and mothers; economic growth and inflation made it acceptable to carry on working, and by the 1960s women made up 40% of the workforce. Motherhood became a choice rather than, in Queen Victoria's phrase, an "occupational hazard of being a wife".

But the job is not finished — needed for the "against" side of a 12-marker. Women still tend to earn less than men and hold far fewer positions of power: they are at least half the voting population but only a small percentage of MPs. Some argue it is unreasonable for women to expect the same careers as men because pregnancy and childbirth take up time and energy; others reply that parenting should be shared and employers should support family life with flexible hours. Elsewhere in the world women face unequal access to divorce and education, forced marriage and domestic violence — in 2012 the Pakistani schoolgirl Malala Yousafzai was shot in the head by the Taliban for blogging in favour of education for girls.

Feminism is the belief in the social, economic and political equality of the sexes. The movement largely originated in the West but is manifest worldwide. Non-religious views note that gender bias is embedded in society — few women head FTSE 100 companies, and "glass ceilings" persist in business — even though it is illegal to discriminate on gender.

Emma Watson's 2014 speech to the United Nations, launching HeForShe, is the usable modern example. Her argument is that gender equality is men's issue too: she has seen a father's role as a parent valued less by society, and young men unable to ask for help with mental illness "for fear it would make them less of a man" — suicide is the biggest killer of UK men between 20 and 49. "If men don't have to be aggressive in order to be accepted, women won't feel compelled to be submissive." She also noted that at current rates it will be 2086 before all rural African girls can have a secondary education.

The role and status of women in the Christian community

All Christians agree men and women are of equal worth because both are created in the image of God — but they interpret "equality" in two different ways, and that is the whole of this topic. Jesus was sent to save all from sin, men and women alike; all will be judged at the Eschaton; and St Paul emphasised equality in Galatians 3:28. The split is over what follows.

Christian attitudes to disability split sharply: some texts treat disability as punishment, while the dominant modern view treats it as calling for compassion and equal worth. Give both, because the spec asks for "reasons for differing attitudes".

Note that Jesus' miracles sit on both sides: healing implies compassion, but also that disability is something to be removed

Jesus' healing ministry is the single strongest piece of evidence for the compassionate reading. He healed the blind, the lame, those with withered arms, lepers, the deaf and the paralysed — people whose condition had made them outcasts — and treated them as equals needing practical help and inclusion. Mark 1:40–44: a leper begs him, "If you choose, you can make me clean", and "moved with pity, Jesus stretched out his hand and touched him". The touch is the detail worth noting — touching a leper was itself forbidden.

The sanctity of life follows from creation and stewardship: all life comes from God, who created all people in his image, able-bodied and disabled, so all life must be respected. This is why the Roman Catholic Church and many other Christians oppose abortion on the grounds of disability. The figures make the issue live: around 90% of couples told their unborn baby has a disability choose to abort, and 92% of mothers diagnosed with a Down's syndrome pregnancy terminate. In 2001, Joanna Jepson, an Anglican priest with a jaw deformity herself, legally challenged the late abortion of a 28-week-old foetus with a cleft lip and palate, arguing this did not constitute a "serious handicap" under the 1967 Abortion Act. Some argue aborting disabled foetuses is a form of eugenics and breaches the Disability Discrimination Act.

The history. For much of it there was no social welfare for those unable to work; in the medieval period monasteries and convents cared for the sick and disabled, which ended with the dissolution of the monasteries. The Elizabethan Poor Law of 1601 specified that those unable to work — the "deserving poor" — should be helped in their local parish poorhouses. By the nineteenth century philanthropists established charities, but in the early-to-mid twentieth century many disabled children did not attend mainstream schools, mothers of disabled children were encouraged to place them in institutions, and shops, cinemas, theatres and workplaces rarely provided access.

Why attitudes changed: injured servicemen returning from the World Wars, who were thought to have a right to care given their sacrifice and who also changed the perception of disability — many remained fit, healthy and able to contribute fully; recognition of human rights generally; momentum from the feminist and civil rights movements; campaigns by individuals and charities such as Leonard Cheshire; changing healthcare and the end of the asylum; more mixing in society, with disabled children taught in mainstream schools; more openness about mental health; and the Paralympic movement. The shift in the later twentieth century was from looking after disabled people to helping them live independent lives.

But prejudice persists — figures for the "against" side of an evaluation. More than one billion people worldwide experience some form of disability, and 10.9 million live in Great Britain. The employment rate for disabled people is 44% against 75% for people without disabilities. 66% say they have experienced aggression, hostility or name-calling, and 46% experience discrimination daily or weekly. The poverty rate for disabled adults is twice that for non-disabled adults, and 98% of disabled children in developing countries have no access to basic services. Stigma around mental health is fuelled by stereotypes — that such people are dangerous or violent — when in fact they are at greater risk of being attacked or of harming themselves.

The named Christian example: Jean Vanier and L'Arche. In 1964, Jean Vanier (1928–2019), a Canadian academic, invited Raphaël Simi and Philippe Seux, two men with intellectual disabilities, to share his life in an old house at Trosly-Breuil in France. They called the home L'Arche — "the Ark", after Noah's: the biblical symbol of deliverance, and of God's covenant with humanity, a refuge from a prejudiced world. Inspired by the Beatitudes, which begin "Blessed are the poor in spirit" (Matthew 5:1–12), Vanier was among the first in the modern age to say it is not right to lock people up in institutions, believing instead that those the world would rather lock away have much to teach us and can even heal us.

Today there are over 120 communities in 30 countries, each of 2 to 8 houses, many running day programmes, workshops and retreat centres. The detail that earns the marks is how L'Arche differs from a group home: there are no "clients" and "staff" and no shift work. Disabled people are core members, those who assist them are assistants, the assistants live in the houses as they would with any family, and everyone is equally responsible for the life of the community. The Charter of L'Arche says the members "are called to be one body. They live, work, pray and celebrate together, sharing their joys and their suffering and forgiving each other, as in a family. They have a simple life-style which gives priority to relationships."

Other Christian work: Liveability runs care homes, provides accessible holidays and rehabilitates people with brain injuries; Through the Roof ensures the Christian message is accessible to people with any type of disability and advises churches on practical projects.

In worship — five practical actions, enough for a 6-marker on putting belief into action: buildings are made accessible with suitable facilities; large-print hymn books and service sheets are provided for the visually impaired and hearing loops for those hard of hearing; clergy visit the housebound, taking them the sacrament of communion; churches hold services of healing and prayers for the sick and disabled; and many have chaplains or trained individuals who minister to the deaf and blind, with special services to support them. Pilgrimages are made to places of healing such as Lourdes.

Many churches admit they do not do enough to welcome disabled people. In wider society there is still prejudice and discrimination, a lack of equality in employment, and limited access to some buildings, though clubs such as PHAB (Physically Handicapped and Able Bodied) bring disabled and non-disabled young people together.

The multi-ethnic society — benefits and problems

More detail

A multi-ethnic society is one in which people of many different ethnic groups live together. Britain always has been one: the Romans invaded the Celts, then the Germanic tribes collectively called the Anglo-Saxons, then the Normans in 1066; the slave trade brought increasing numbers of people from different ethnic groups; and in the twentieth century people from the Commonwealth came to fight for Britain in the World Wars and then to fill the post-war labour shortages — the Windrush Generation. Diversity is uneven: London and Leicester are among the most diverse areas, while rural Devon, Cornwall, parts of Wales and the Lake District are much less so.

The named critic is Enoch Powell, a Conservative politician, whose "Rivers of Blood" speech in 1968 warned about a lack of integration and the fragmentation of society: "As I look ahead, I am filled with foreboding: like the Roman, I seem to see the River Tiber foaming with much blood." He also claimed increased diversity would leave English people feeling "strangers in their own country". Segregation has been documented since — Ed Hussain's Among the Mosques (2021) noted separate white and Asian areas in Bradford and Blackburn. On language, there is evidence that pupils who speak English as a second language underperform in primary education but mostly catch up in secondary school, though poor communication remains a barrier to integration and employment.

Why racism occurs: socialisation — children brought up by racist parents or influenced by racist adults; fear of difference in appearance, language or customs, and low self-esteem leading people to single out a perceived weaker group to boost their own sense of worth; and poverty — a shortage of resources and jobs leading people to blame ethnic minorities rather than poor government. The consequences are worth stating too: resentment and division, less trust and community cohesion, isolated minority groups withdrawing, fewer opportunities and poorer physical and mental wellbeing — social tensions that can erupt into violence and rioting.

So also: education, teaching children about different religions, nations and ethnicities to remove ignorance, ideally by meeting and mixing with people from other cultures; positive role models in the public eye, to counteract negative stereotypes; shared values — in November 2014 the Department for Education published guidance on promoting British values in schools, naming democracy, the rule of law, individual liberty, and mutual respect and tolerance of those with different faiths and beliefs; campaigns such as football's Show Racism the Red Card; and the United Nations' continuing work against racism. Churches were instrumental in bringing in the Race Relations Act — the Church of England has bishops in the House of Lords. And Christians have used their faith to oppress. In the Crusades thousands were killed in the name of Christianity. When the Spanish navy invaded South America, tens of thousands of local people were massacred in attempts to force conversion and steal their wealth. The fullest case is apartheid, and the whole arc is worth learning because it shows the religion correcting itself. In 1948 the South African Prime Minister Daniel François Malan, a Dutch Reformed minister, introduced the apartheid legislation, supported by the Dutch Reformed Church, which used Genesis 9:18–27 — Noah's curse of his grandson Canaan — to claim that treating Black people as inferior was justified. In the early 1980s the World Alliance of Reformed Churches declared apartheid a heresy and expelled the Dutch Reformed Church; in 1986 all its congregations were desegregated; in 1994 apartheid was abolished; and the Church has since expressed repentance.

The 12-marker is usually "religion has done more harm than good in race relations", and the honest answer uses both columns:

Non-religious attitudes to racial harmony — asked directly and more than once, so it needs its own answer rather than "they agree with Christians". (1) Science: there is one human species and race has no biological basis, so racism rests on a factual mistake — the human genome shows all modern humans share a recent African origin. (2) Rights and the law: the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) opens with everyone being "born free and equal in dignity and rights", and in the UK the Race Relations Act 1976 and the Equality Act 2010 make racial discrimination illegal; non-religious campaigners therefore treat harmony as a matter of enforceable equality rather than of charity. (3) Humanism: with one life and one world, there is no reason to divide it by ancestry, and Humanists UK campaigns against discrimination of every kind; Utilitarians add that prejudice reduces total happiness, and the social contract argument of Hobbes and Rousseau says a society only works if its members accept each other as equals. (5) How they would promote it: education and the curriculum, anti-racism in sport and the media, mixed schools and workplaces, and prosecuting racist crime — essentially the same programme as the churches, minus the theology. The multi-faith society and interfaith relationships

Spec 4.4 · Lesson 11, Revision Booklet 4 p.18–19

A multi-faith society is one in which a number of different religions co-exist in one community. Historically most people lived where a single faith dominated and was tied to the law, marriage customs and education. The UK is now multi-faith; Saudi Arabia is not.

Factors that produced multi-faith societies: migration from one country to another, foreign travel, easy and speedy access to information, intercultural marriage, and increased tolerance of religious diversity.

Responsibilities of living in a multi-faith society: tolerance; respecting others' freedom of speech and religion; allowing freedom of worship and making allowances for holy days and festivals; setting aside prejudice; government legislating to protect all religions; and all faiths supporting shared values.

How the development of a multi-faith society is promoted.

  • Religious. The Revd Isaac Poobalan, a parish priest in Scotland, let Muslim worshippers from the mosque next door use his church for their prayers after seeing them worshipping in the street on a cold winter's day for lack of space: "Jesus taught his disciples to love your neighbour as yourself and this is something I cannot just preach to my congregation, I had to put it into practice." The Church of England supports the Council of Christians and Jews, the Inter Faith Network for the UK and the Christian Muslim Forum. The congregation of Southwark Cathedral visits mosques and synagogues regularly and discusses issues of mutual interest. A national Interfaith Week is supported by most churches.
  • Legislation. The Racial and Religious Hatred Act 2006 makes it illegal to do anything that might be perceived as inciting religious hatred; the Equality Act 2010 reiterates that discriminating on the basis of religion is illegal.
  • Government initiatives. In 2008 the Government published Face to Face and Side by Side, built on three core principles — partnership, empowerment and choice — aiming to encourage interfaith dialogue, increase collaborative social action between faith communities, maintain good relations with wider civil society, and overcome barriers facing young people and women. The Inter Faith Network for the UK, founded in 1987, promotes understanding and co-operation between people of different faiths and organised the UK's first Multi-Faith Week in 2009.
  • Education. Religious education is part of the basic curriculum and compulsory for pupils aged 5 to 18 in maintained schools unless withdrawn by their parents; since a change of policy in 2016, all students taking GCSE RS must explore the beliefs and practices of at least two world religions.

The counter-view, held by some non-religious people, is that religion should be a private matter: signs of religious affiliation should not be evident in public, and religious organisations and beliefs should receive no special treatment. When the racial and religious hatred bill was first proposed it would have been an offence to use insulting language about a religion; writers, comedians and actors objected, and Rowan Atkinson argued: "to criticise a person for their race is manifestly irrational and ridiculous but to criticise their religion, that is a right. That is a freedom." The Act passed in 2006 without that clause, and the advocacy group Liberty held that "free speech is far more precious than protection from being offended". Ecumenism — unity within Christianity

More detail

Ecumenism is the movement to promote unity and co-operation between the different Christian denominations. The word comes from the Greek oikoumene, meaning "whole, inhabited world". It accepts the diversity of Christians but strives to bring the different communities together in one fully committed fellowship to serve God's purpose in the world. The spec counts interdenominational relationships as a form of interfaith work, so this topic answers questions about Christians "promoting unity within their faith community".

  • Why it is necessary, and how it began. The modern movement began at the World Missionary Conference in Edinburgh in 1910. It was designed to help Christians work together in spreading the Gospel, on the view that bickering between denominations would inhibit that work — and that division would itself undermine the Christian message of agape. Division has also fed sectarian conflict, as in Northern Ireland.
  • The World Council of Churches, established in 1948, to pray for and pursue the visible unity of Christ's Church, preach the Gospel and promote justice and peace. It represents approximately 400 million Christians in more than 340 churches and denominations across 120 countries, and meets every seven years with around a thousand delegates. The Roman Catholic Church has not joined, but recognises the need to move towards mutual understanding and sends observers. In 1982 the WCC produced the Lima Document, with three conclusions: mutual recognition of each other's baptism, since all are baptised into the body of Christ; all churches should celebrate the Eucharist at least once every Sunday; and all Christians should have a role in the Church's ministry.
  • Churches Together groups — local co-operation between denominations, committing to an ongoing relationship: joint acts of worship, social gatherings, discussion groups and shared charity projects.
  • Taizé — an ecumenical monastic order founded by Roger Louis Schutz-Marsauche (Brother Roger) in 1940 in south-east France, to provide a place of reconciliation in view of the Second World War. About a hundred Roman Catholic and Protestant monks belong, living at Taizé or in poor areas of Asia, Africa and South America; they earn their own keep and give the rest of their time to the needy. After the war it became a major site of pilgrimage, and tens of thousands of young people now come to study the Bible, join discussion groups, share the chores and take part in worship three times a day. The worship uses the community's distinctive music — simple phrases, usually from the Psalms, set to music and repeated as a prayer chant to aid prayer and meditation. All are welcome regardless of religious affiliation, so unity is practised rather than merely discussed.
  • Street Pastors is also explicitly ecumenical, with many different churches involved — a useful example of denominations working together in practice.

Christianity and other religions — the four attitudes

Spec 4.4 · Lesson 13, Revision Booklet 4 p.19

  • Exclusivism — only Christianity saves: "No one comes to the Father except through me" (John 14:6).
  • Inclusivism — other faiths hold real truth; Karl Rahner's "anonymous Christians", and "My Father's house has many rooms" (John 14:2).
  • Pluralism — all religions are equally valid routes to one Reality; John Hick's blind men and the elephant. No biblical support.
More detail
  • Proselytisation — converting others, commanded in the Great Commission: "Go into all the world and proclaim the good news" (Mark 16:15).
  • The baseline is tolerance — rejected by a Samaritan village, Jesus rebuked James and John and simply moved on (Luke 9:52–56).
  • Conversion and colonisation — missionary work travelled with colonisation, reshaping whole cultures: the strongest objection, and a historical fact.

Christian attitudes to other religions come in four named positions, and using the four words correctly is the single clearest way to reach the top level in this spec area. They all start from salvation — being saved from sin — which Christians believe was made possible by Jesus' death and resurrection. The three-way division was identified by the theologian John Hick (1922–2012).

Begin from the general position, because it frames the rest: Christians believe in human rights, including the right to religious freedom, and using Jesus' Golden Rule — "do unto others as you would have them do unto you" — most think they must respect other people's faith if they expect theirs to be respected. They can also follow Jesus' example: when his message was rejected he simply moved on. In Luke 9:52–56, when a Samaritan village would not welcome him, James and John asked, "Lord, do you want us to call fire down from heaven to destroy them?" — and "Jesus turned and rebuked them", then went to another village. So the baseline Christian attitude is tolerance and mutual understanding.

How it actually works, for a 6-marker. The spec's central question is whether wealthy individuals and countries have a responsibility towards the poor. Wealth in itself is not a bad thing; it is the distribution of wealth that is the concern. Absolute poverty was defined by the United Nations in 1995 as "a severe deprivation of basic human needs, including food, safe drinking water, sanitation facilities, health, shelter, education and information".

The headline evidence is the 1980 Brandt Report, which identified an imbalance between developed and developing countries: the developed world contains 25% of the world's population but around 80% of its wealth. The Report advocated long-term action on the causes of poverty, requiring changes in both the developing and developed worlds — and little has changed since.

The 12-mark frame

Five paragraphs, in this order. The top band needs Christian teaching in every one of them, at least one named example, and a conclusion that actually decides — so write the conclusion you are heading for before you start.

  1. Restate the statement and say what turns on it. One or two sentences naming the real question — whether rights may ever be restricted, whether the Church is biased against women, whether all religions are equally true, whether the rich owe the poor anything.
  2. The strongest case for, with teaching and scripture. One argument developed, then appraised. Build it from the three foundations — the doctrine of creation (imago Dei), stewardship and agape — and anchor it in a verse: Galatians 3:28, Acts 17:26, Matthew 25:31–46, Isaiah 1:17. Attach a named example, because this section rewards them more than any other: Tutu, Romero, King, Roosevelt, Vanier, Amnesty, Christian Aid.
  3. The strongest case against, with teaching and scripture. Same again from the other side — 1 Timothy 2:11–15, Leviticus 21:17–20, Genesis 9:18–27 as the Dutch Reformed Church used it, John 14:6 — and say which of the two arguments is stronger and why. The appraisal is where the AO2 marks are, not the listing.
  4. Where Christians disagree with each other. The paragraph that reaches the top level, because it shows the disagreement is internal to the religion: complementary roles against vocation and ability; Roman Catholic and Orthodox practice against the Church of England since 1994 and 2015; exclusivism, inclusivism and pluralism — use the technical words. Then add a non-religious view for perspective: Locke, Bentham, Hobbes and Rousseau on rights, Nietzsche on protecting the weak, Utilitarianism, Toby Ord on effective giving. Remember it does not count as the reference to a religion.
  5. A conclusion that decides. Name the winning argument, say why it outweighs the other, and tie it back to the words of the statement.

Sentence starters that earn the evaluation marks:

  • "Most Christians would agree with this statement, because the doctrine of creation teaches…"
  • "A Christian who follows Natural Law would argue…"
  • "However, a Utilitarian would respond that…" / "A situation ethicist would ask only what is most loving, and so…"
  • "This argument is the stronger of the two, because…"
  • "Christians themselves are divided here: a conservative Christian citing 1 Timothy… whereas a liberal Christian citing Galatians 3:28…"
  • "An exclusivist would reject this outright, while an inclusivist such as Rahner would…"
  • "A Humanist, who grounds rights in reason rather than God, would say…"
  • "On balance, I agree with the statement, because the teaching that every person bears the image of God outweighs…"

03Understanding it

U1

Why do Christians believe in human rights, when the phrase never appears in the Bible?

Show answer

They derive them from three doctrines rather than a verse. Creation: all humans are made in the image of God, so every person has worth that no government conferred and none can revoke. Stewardship: Christians are responsible for one another, which includes defending the vulnerable. Agape: loving your neighbour requires wanting for them what you want for yourself — the Golden Rule.

Galatians 3:28 — "there's neither male nor female… all are one in Christ" — is the nearest thing to a text, and it is why many Christians think the 1948 Declaration reflects Christian principles.

The qualification to include: for some Christians rights are only valid where they do not contradict Christian teaching, which is why the same believers can champion equality before the law and still reject a right to abortion.

U2

What is the strongest objection to the idea of natural human rights, and how would a supporter answer it?

Show answer

The strongest objection is Bentham's: natural rights are a fiction — "nonsense on stilts". Nothing in nature confers a right; if everyone simply followed their natural rights the result would be anarchy. Rights exist because the state creates and enforces them through law.

Locke's answer: people are born with natural rights and dignity — life, liberty and property — and a state that denies them is unjust rather than merely unusual. Without something prior to the law, there is no standpoint from which to call a legal system evil.

The Christian answer is stronger still: rights rest on the image of God, so they are recognised rather than granted. Tutu's line is the one to quote — treating people as less than infinitely valuable is "a blasphemy".

Hobbes and Rousseau occupy the middle: rights are real but need a social contract to be secured.

U3

Both sides of the women's ordination debate quote scripture. How can the same Bible produce opposite conclusions?

Show answer

Because they differ on the authority and interpretation of the texts, not on the texts themselves.

Conservatives read 1 Timothy's "no woman to have authority over a man" and Genesis 2 (Adam first, Eve as companion) as permanent instruction, supported by Jesus choosing no female apostles. Equality then means equal worth, different roles — the Catechism's "equal dignity though in a different way".

Liberals read those texts in the context in which they were written, and give priority to Galatians 3:28 and Genesis 1:27, where male and female are created at the same time. Equality then means roles decided by vocation and ability.

The deeper reason is denominational: churches balance scripture, tradition and modern life differently. The Salvation Army and United Reformed Church always accepted women on equal terms, the Baptists began ordaining them in the 1920s, and the Church of England ordained women as priests in 1994 and as bishops in 2015 — while the Roman Catholic and Orthodox churches do not, holding that the priest represents Christ.

Liberals also point to historical context: both Testaments were written in a patriarchal society, Jesus was bound to choose male apostles, and Paul was concerned not to upset Roman society while Christianity sought legal recognition. The instruction that women keep silent may reflect the fact that the home — the wife's domain — doubled as the place of worship in the earliest churches.

U4

Jesus healed disabled people. Why is that evidence on both sides of the Christian debate about disability?

Show answer

For the positive view: he spent much of his ministry healing people whose illness or disability had made them society's outcasts. That shows compassion, inclusion and the worth of people the rest of society had written off.

For the negative view: healing implies disability is a defect to be removed rather than a way of being human. Read alongside Leviticus 21:17 and the texts treating ailments as punishment for sin, the miracles can be used to suggest disability is a problem God fixes rather than a state God blesses.

The text that settles it: in John 9, asked whether a man born blind had sinned or his parents had, Jesus answered "neither this man nor his parents sinned; he was born blind so that God's works might be revealed in him" — an explicit rejection of the punishment reading. Deuteronomy 27:18 also curses "anyone who misleads a blind person on the road".

How to use it: this is the cleanest example in the whole section of a single piece of evidence being appraised two ways — exactly what a 12-marker's level 4 descriptor asks for. The modern Christian resolution is the sanctity of life and imago Dei: Jean Vanier's L'Arche, founded in 1964, treats disabled people as core members rather than clients, with no staff and no shift work.

U5

Why do Christians who hold exclusivism and Christians who hold pluralism both claim to be following the Bible?

Show answer

Exclusivists take Jesus at his word: "I am the way and the truth and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me" (John 14:6), and "salvation is found in no one else" (Acts 4:12). Add Original Sin as a barrier and the crucifixion as the payment, and the logic is tight — which is also why they regard missionary work as urgent rather than rude, and as itself an expression of love for neighbour.

Pluralists start from God's transcendence: if God is beyond human comprehension, every religion's knowledge is partial, so each may be true but incomplete — the blind men and the elephant. They cite 2 Peter 3:9, that God does not want anyone to perish, and John 3:17, that the Son came to save rather than condemn.

The honest assessment: pluralism has no direct biblical support, which is why many Christians reject it; exclusivism has strong textual support but struggles with the fate of those who never heard of Christ. Inclusivism — Rahner's "anonymous Christians", with John 14:2's "my Father's house has many rooms" — exists precisely to keep John 14:6 while letting a loving God save the good.

Both sides can also appeal to Jesus' own conduct: in Luke 9 a Samaritan village refuses to welcome him, James and John ask whether to "call fire down from heaven to destroy them", and Jesus rebukes them and moves on. That is evidence for tolerance from an exclusivist text.

U6

Ecumenism and interfaith dialogue are often confused. What is the difference, and why does the spec treat both as relevant here?

Show answer

Ecumenism — from the Greek oikoumene, "whole, inhabited world" — is unity between Christian denominations: the World Council of Churches (1948), Churches Together groups, Taizé, the ecumenical make-up of Street Pastors. Interfaith dialogue is between different religions: the Council of Christians and Jews, the Christian Muslim Forum, Southwark Cathedral's visits to mosques and synagogues.

The spec asks for "interfaith (including interdenominational) relationships", so both count. The connection is practical, and it is the ecumenical movement's own stated reason: the movement began at the World Missionary Conference in Edinburgh in 1910 on the view that bickering between denominations would inhibit their work and that division would itself undermine the Christian message of agape. A Christianity visibly divided against itself has little credibility asking a multi-faith society to live in peace, and Northern Ireland shows denominational division producing the same harms as interfaith division.

U7

Why do Christians distinguish charity from justice when helping the poor, if both end up giving money away?

Show answer

Charity relieves the symptom: practical help, hand-outs or new skills, motivated by compassion. Justice attacks the cause: it says the poor are not unlucky but wronged, and that the distribution of resources is itself unfair — the Brandt Report's 25% of people holding 80% of the wealth.

The teachings differ accordingly. Charity: "he who gives to the poor will lack nothing", Wesley's "give all you can". Justice: "let justice flow down like a river" (Amos), "a righteous man knows the rights of the poor".

So does the action. Charity funds a food bank; justice campaigns to drop developing-world debt (Jubilee 2000), or builds base communities with no hierarchy so the poor can fight for themselves — Liberation Theology. Amos is the model for the justice strand: he condemned people who performed every religious ritual diligently while "cheating with dishonest scales, buying the poor with silver and the needy for a pair of sandals" (Amos 8:4–6), which is exactly the charge that charity without justice invites.

The evaluation question "It is more important to work for justice than to give to charity" turns entirely on this: charity helps now but can leave the system intact and the poor dependent; justice changes the system but is slow and political. Christian Aid does both, which is the strongest line for a conclusion.

U8

Many Christians have opposed racism — and many Christians defended slavery and apartheid. How should an answer handle that?

Show answer

Head on. Pretending otherwise loses marks; acknowledging it gains them, because the level descriptors reward considering different viewpoints and appraising evidence.

The teaching is unambiguous: Acts 17:26 — "from one man he made all the nations"; Galatians 2:6 — "God does not show favouritism"; Genesis 1:27; the Good Samaritan; Jesus healing Gentiles. And it produced King, Tutu, Huddleston and Pope Francis on immigration.

The failures are real: Deuteronomy 7 has God driving out other nations and discouraging intermarriage; Christians ran the Crusades, colonised, profited from the slave trade, and the Dutch Reformed Church supported apartheid.

The judgement: these were failures to follow the teaching rather than applications of it — the same scripture that was misused also produced the movement that ended apartheid, and it was Tutu's Christianity, not his politics, that produced the Truth and Reconciliation Commission.

What each part of the question actually wants

Section 4 sits on Paper 1 — 1 hour 45 minutes, four sections, each with a choice of two questions, each question worth 25 marks. The four parts are marked in completely different ways, so answer each in its own shape.

  • a) 3 marks — AO1. Three focused and distinct points. The answer does not require explanations; brief, clear reasons are ideal. Number them and stop.
  • b) 4 marks — AO1. Two points of information which require some development — point (1) + development (1), twice. The development must add value to the point while still answering the question posed; an example is often the quickest way to develop it.
  • c) 6 marks — AO1 and AO2. An explain question, marked in levels rather than by ticking points. Explain and develop the issue, drawing out the influence and significance of the religion, beliefs and values, with religious terms used appropriately. Aim to explain more than one reason even when no number is given, make sure every point is fully explained, and balance breadth against depth.
  • d) 12 marks — AO1 and AO2. The synoptic assessment, also level marked. It wants fully evaluated arguments underpinned with knowledge and understanding, drawn from across the course: consider the alternative viewpoint no matter how weak it is, refer to at least one religion, appraise the quality of each argument as you give it, and reach a fully justified conclusion. Humanist and atheist views add perspective but do not on their own count as the reference to a religion.

Twelve questions in the shape of the real thing: three each of the 3-, 4-, 6- and 12-mark parts. The 12-mark answers are written as plans — the real paper marks them in levels, not by ticking points, so treat the twelve mark points as a target for coverage rather than a tick list.

"Prejudice and discrimination are the biggest problems facing society today."

Arguments for the statement.

Acting on superficial judgements is detrimental to the smooth running of society: assuming skin colour or gender defines a person leads to inequality, resentment and ultimately conflict — most visibly in South Africa under apartheid 1

Prejudice and discrimination are not confined to one country or era: they appear in racism, gender bias, disability bias and religious hatred alike, which is why so much legislation has been needed, from the Race Relations Act 1976 to the Equality Act 2010 1

Arguments against the statement.

The environmental crisis may be greater: if global warming is not dealt with now, there will be no people left to experience prejudice 1

Current conflicts may usurp it: the war in Ukraine is devastating for those who live there and has international repercussions, and support for Ukraine could ultimately lead to a wider war and even the use of weapons of mass destruction 1

Poverty is arguably more urgent still — the Brandt Report found the developed world holds around 80% of the wealth with 25% of the population, and poverty kills directly 1

However, these can be connected back: those most affected by global warming are usually those who already face discrimination 1

Christian teaching.

Christians may well agree the statement is true, because of their teachings on creation and stewardship: God created all of humanity in his image, and the duty to care for all people obliges Christians to work against prejudice and discrimination 1

Desmond Tutu worked tirelessly against apartheid and continued afterwards with the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, showing that discrimination's consequences outlast the laws that created it 1

Christian leaders have also spoken on the other issues, urging forgiveness and compassion rather than vengeance in response to conflict, which suggests Christianity does not treat prejudice as the only great problem 1

There are several very important problems — the environmental crisis, discrimination and conflict — and all of them have a global impact from which anyone can suffer 1

D7

Explain Christian teaching on racial harmony. (6)

Show answer

Christian teaching begins with the doctrine of creation: all people are made in the image of God (Genesis 1:27), so no ethnic group can be superior 1; the book of Acts states it directly — "from one man he made all the nations, that they should inhabit the whole earth" (Acts 17:26) — which makes racial hierarchy a denial of how God made the world, and Leviticus 19:33–34 applies it to the outsider: "the foreigner residing among you must be treated as your native-born" 1

Agape extends the duty of love beyond one's own group: in the parable of the Good Samaritan the neighbour to be loved is the ethnic outsider, and Jesus himself healed and talked with Gentiles, including the Centurion's servant 1; St Paul reinforces it — "God does not show favouritism" (Galatians 2:6) — so partiality on grounds of race is incompatible with imitating God 1

Christians therefore treat racial equality as a demand of justice, following Amos' call to "let justice flow down like a river", and put it into practice: Martin Luther King led non-violent protest for civil rights, and Desmond Tutu's Truth and Reconciliation Commission helped South Africa move beyond apartheid 1; Christians also acknowledge failures to follow this teaching — the slave trade and the Dutch Reformed Church's support for apartheid — which is why they regard racial harmony as something to be actively worked for rather than assumed 1

D8

"All religions are equal."

Evaluate this statement, showing you have considered different points of view. In your answer, you should refer to at least one religion. (12)

Show answer

Arguments for the statement.

God is transcendent, so human knowledge of him is necessarily limited; each religion may therefore offer a true but incomplete understanding — the blind men and the elephant 1

What matters, pluralists argue, is being God-centred rather than self-centred, and the truth claims of all religions can produce that personal transformation 1

In a multi-faith society treating religions as equal produces tolerance, choice and co-operation on social justice, whereas ranking them produces division 1

Equality before the law already requires the state to treat all religions equally, whatever their truth claims 1

Arguments against the statement.

Religions make incompatible claims — they cannot all be straightforwardly true at once, so "equal" must mean something weaker than "equally true" 1

Pluralism has no support in the Bible, so many Christians regard it as unacceptable 1

If any path leads to God, the incarnation and crucifixion lose their point — which is a serious cost for a Christian to accept 1

Christian teaching.

Exclusivists reject the statement outright, citing John 14:6 — "I am the way and the truth and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me" — and Acts 4:12; for them proselytisation is a duty rather than an insult 1

Inclusivists take a middle position: Karl Rahner argued a loving God would not condemn good people who never heard of Christ, calling them "anonymous Christians", so other religions contain real truth even though Christianity understands God best 1

Pluralists such as John Hick accept the statement, arguing from God's transcendence with the analogy of the elephant and the blind men — each report was true but partial — and citing 2 Peter 3:9 and John 3:17 that God wants to save rather than condemn 1

Conclusion.

Judgement: as a claim about truth the statement is weak, since religions contradict one another and pluralism cannot be derived from Christian scripture 1

As a claim about respect and treatment it is strong, and the inclusivist position is the most defensible — it keeps Christian conviction while recognising the good in other faiths, which is why interfaith dialogue, rather than either proselytisation or indifference, is the response most churches now take 1

D10

Choose one religion. Outline two of its attitudes to the role of women. (4)

Show answer

Traditional Christians hold that men and women have equal worth but complementary roles 1 — the Catechism speaks of "equal dignity though in a different way", so women may take pastoral duties and contribute to worship but, in Roman Catholic teaching, cannot be priests, citing St Paul's instruction that no woman should have authority over a man 1

D11

Explain why many Christians feel they have an obligation to help the poor. (6)

Show answer

Christians believe they are stewards of a world God created and of the people in it, so wealth is held in trust rather than owned outright 1; agape then requires that it be used for the neighbour in need — Isaiah 58 commands believers to "share your bread with the hungry, and bring the homeless poor into your house" 1

Jesus' teaching makes the obligation unusually sharp: in the parable of the sheep and goats, people are judged on how they helped the poor, and in helping the poor they help God himself 1; the parable of the rich man and Lazarus shows the rich man condemned for ignoring the poor man at his gate, and Jesus told the rich young man to sell all he had, warning it is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the Kingdom of God 1

Christians also treat poverty as an issue of justice, not only kindness: the prophets demanded that justice "flow down like a river", and today Christians regard global inequality as unjust and campaign for change 1; this is why Christian Aid, founded in 1947, works for long-term development and lobbies governments to drop developing-world debt rather than only providing emergency relief, and why Liberation Theology set up base communities so the poor could fight for justice themselves 1

D12

"Religions are biased in favour of men."

Evaluate this statement, showing you have considered different points of view. In your answer, you should refer to at least one religion. (12)

Show answer

Arguments for the statement.

The structures are patriarchal: the Roman Catholic and Orthodox churches restrict ordination to men, holding that the priest represents Christ, so the most senior roles are closed to women by rule rather than ability 1

The texts are used to support it: St Paul instructs that no woman should have authority over a man (1 Timothy), Genesis 2 has Eve created as Adam's companion, and Jesus chose no female apostles 1

Teaching about the home has pushed women towards domestic roles — Luther's "to remain at home, sit still, keep house and bring up children", Ephesians 5:23 making the husband the head of the wife, and Pope John Paul II's "the family comes first" 1

Society has moved faster than the churches: the Equality Act 2010 made gender discrimination illegal, yet some denominations retain exemptions 1

Arguments against the statement.

Christianity's foundational claim is equality of worth: both sexes are made in the image of God, Jesus came to save men and women alike, and all will be judged equally at the Eschaton 1

Galatians 3:28 — "there's neither male nor female… all are one in Christ" — is an explicit statement of equality from the same apostle cited on the other side 1

Practice is changing: the Salvation Army and United Reformed Church have always accepted women on equal terms, the Baptists began ordaining women in the 1920s, and the Church of England ordained women as priests in 1994 and bishops in 2015 1

The Bible contains strong women — Deborah, Mary Magdalene — and Jesus and Paul treated women with respect in a culture that did not 1

Christian teaching.

Traditionalists do not claim women are inferior: the Catechism's formula is "equal dignity though in a different way", and a Roman Catholic declaration speaks of women's "special ability to humanize the family" — so the claim is difference, not bias 1

Liberals reply that the patriarchal texts must be read in the context in which they were written, and that Genesis 1:27 has men and women created at the same time, with Eve made from Adam's rib so that they work side by side 1

Conclusion.

Judgement: "complementary roles" is a coherent position — the Catechism's "equal dignity though in a different way" is not a claim of inferiority — but where it consistently assigns authority to men and domestic duty to women, the practical effect is bias whatever the stated intention, which is why many women found Mulieris Dignitatem patronising 1

So I partly agree: Christianity's teaching is not biased in favour of men, but some of its institutions historically have been — and the fact that churches have changed, ordaining women on the basis of the same scripture, shows the bias lies in interpretation rather than in the religion itself 1
