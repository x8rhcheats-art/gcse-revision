02How it actually works

Photosynthesis is how a plant makes its own food. It uses light energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into glucose, and gives off oxygen. Everything else in this topic — the graphs, the leaf, the practicals — is about what speeds that up, and how you prove it happened.

The equations

Spec 2.18, 2.19 · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q9

Photosynthesis turns carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen, using light energy absorbed by chlorophyll.

Photosynthesis — word equationcarbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygenlight and chlorophyll go above the arrow, not on the left
Photosynthesis — balanced symbol equation6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂correct formulae one mark, balancing numbers the second

It is an endothermic reaction: light energy is taken in and stored as chemical energy in the glucose. That conversion — light energy to chemical energy — is what "importance" questions want.

✗ "light + carbon dioxide + water → glucose" — light is not a reactant.
✗ "6CO₂ + H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + O₂" — correct formulae, unbalanced: one mark of two.
✓ "6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂", with light and chlorophyll written above the arrow.

Where it happens — the plant cell

Spec 2.4 · 2024 Y10 Q1(a) · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q3

Photosynthesis happens in chloroplasts, which contain the green pigment chlorophyll that absorbs the light.

Chlorophyll absorbs the light; the chloroplast is the container it sits in. Questions that ask "which part contains chlorophyll" want the chloroplast. Questions about "what absorbs light" want chlorophyll.

What the plant does with the glucose

Glucose is the raw material for almost everything a plant makes.

  • Respiration — to release energy
  • Starch — insoluble storage, so it does not affect osmosis
  • Cellulose — for cell walls
  • Sucrose — the form transported in the phloem
  • Amino acids and proteins — combined with nitrate ions from the soil
  • Lipids — stored in seeds

That list is why the starch test works as proof of photosynthesis: no photosynthesis, no glucose, no starch.

Limiting factors — the graph that always comes up

Spec 2.20 · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q8

A limiting factor is whichever condition is in shortest supply, holding the rate back. There are three: light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature.

The classic graph rises and then flattens. Every answer about it has the same two parts:

  1. Where the line rises, the factor on the x-axis is limiting. More of it gives a faster rate.
  2. Where the line is flat, that factor is no longer limiting. Another factor now is — and you must name one.

The plateau answer — two marks

1. Light intensity is no longer the limiting factor 1

2. Another factor, such as carbon dioxide concentration or temperature, is now limiting 1

"Something else is limiting" does not score. Name it.

If a graph shows two lines — one at high CO₂, one at low — the higher plateau proves CO₂ was the limiting factor on the lower line.

Temperature — different from light and CO₂

Spec 2.20

Photosynthesis is controlled by enzymes, so temperature has a peak and then a collapse.

Below the optimum, more kinetic energy means more frequent collisions and a faster rate — exactly as in module 1. Above it, the enzymes denature, their active sites change shape, and the rate falls.

Light and carbon dioxide never cause a fall. More of them simply stops helping once something else limits the rate. Temperature is the only one of the three that can make things worse.

Light intensity and the inverse square law

2024 Edexcel 1B Q6 · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q9

Light intensity falls with the square of the distance from the lamp.

Light intensity and distancelight intensity ∝ 1 ÷ distance²double the distance → a quarter of the light · treble it → a ninth

Moving a lamp from 10 cm to 20 cm does not halve the light — it cuts it to a quarter. If the question gives a table of distances, the rate should fall faster than the distance grows, at least while light is limiting.

Stomata and the trade-off with water

Spec 2.43B · 2024 Y11 mock Q4

Stomata must open to let carbon dioxide in, but open stomata also let water vapour out.

That is why closing them has a cost. The mock scheme's two marks: stomata closed or smaller 1, so less carbon dioxide absorbed, so less photosynthesis 1.

Water leaving through the stomata is transpiration. It pulls water up the xylem from the roots:

  1. Root hair cells absorb water from the soil by osmosis.
  2. Water moves up the xylem to the leaves.
  3. It evaporates from the leaf cells and diffuses out of the stomata.
  4. That loss pulls more water up behind it — the transpiration stream.

Transpiration is faster when it is warmer, windier, drier or brighter — bright light opens the stomata.

Gas exchange — day, night and the word "net"

Spec 2.40B, 2.41B, 2.44B

Plants respire all the time, but only photosynthesise in light — so what changes through the day is the net gas exchange.

LightWhat happensNet result
DarkRespiration onlyNet O₂ in, net CO₂ out
Dim (compensation point)Photosynthesis = respirationNo net exchange
BrightPhotosynthesis faster than respirationNet CO₂ in, net O₂ out

✗ "Plants photosynthesise in the day and respire at night."
✓ "Plants respire all the time. In bright light photosynthesis is faster than respiration, so there is a net uptake of carbon dioxide."

The word net is where the mark lives. Leave it out and the answer implies respiration stopped.

Set up tubes with a leaf in each, one in the dark, one in dim light, one in bright light, plus a tube with no leaf as the control. The control shows the colour change is caused by the leaf.

The pondweed practical

Spec 2.23 · 2024 Edexcel 1B Q6

Counting oxygen bubbles from pondweed measures the rate of photosynthesis.

  1. Put pondweed (Elodea) in a test tube of water with sodium hydrogencarbonate to supply CO₂.
  2. Place a lamp a measured distance away and leave it a few minutes to adjust.
  3. Count the bubbles of oxygen in one minute — or collect the gas in a syringe and measure its volume.
  4. Move the lamp to new distances and repeat, three times each, and take a mean.

Control the temperature — use an LED, or a beaker of water between the lamp and the pondweed as a heat shield — and keep the CO₂ concentration and the same piece of pondweed. Counting bubbles is inaccurate because bubbles differ in size; collecting the gas volume is better.

Proving photosynthesis made starch

Spec 2.23

A leaf that turns blue-black with iodine has made starch, so it has photosynthesised.

  1. Destarch the plant — 48 hours in the dark — so any starch found was made during the experiment.
  2. Expose it to the condition being tested.
  3. Boil the leaf in water to kill it and break the membranes.
  4. Boil it in ethanol, in a water bath, to remove the chlorophyll so the colour is visible.
  5. Rinse in warm water to soften it, then add iodine. Blue-black = starch.

Three versions test the three needs. Light: cover part of the leaf with foil — the covered part stays orange-brown. Chlorophyll: use a variegated leaf — only the green parts go blue-black. Carbon dioxide: seal the plant in a bag with soda lime, which absorbs CO₂ — no starch.

04Understanding it

U1

A graph of rate of photosynthesis against light intensity rises steeply, then flattens. Explain both parts.

Show answer

Rising: light intensity is the limiting factor. More light means more energy absorbed by chlorophyll, so the rate increases.

Flat: light is no longer limiting — adding more has no effect. Another factor is now limiting, such as carbon dioxide concentration or temperature.

The test question: how would you prove which one? Repeat at a higher CO₂ concentration. If the plateau rises, CO₂ was the limiting factor.

U2

A lamp is moved from 10 cm to 30 cm. Bubbles fall from 45 to 5 per minute. Is that what you would expect?

Show answer

Yes. Treble the distance gives 1 ÷ 3² = 1/9 of the light. A ninth of 45 is 5.

That also tells you light was the limiting factor across the whole range. If the count had only fallen to 30, something else would have been limiting at the start.

U3

Hydrogencarbonate indicator around a leaf is purple in bright light and yellow in the dark. Explain, using the word "net".

Show answer

Purple: photosynthesis is faster than respiration, so there is a net uptake of carbon dioxide. CO₂ falls.

Yellow: no photosynthesis, but respiration continues, so there is a net release of carbon dioxide. CO₂ rises.

The plant was respiring in the light too — photosynthesis was simply using CO₂ faster than respiration produced it.

U4

Why is a variegated leaf a fair test of whether chlorophyll is needed?

Show answer

The green and white parts are on the same leaf, so they get the same light, carbon dioxide, water and temperature. The only difference is chlorophyll.

After the iodine test, only the green parts turn blue-black. The white parts, with no chlorophyll, made no starch.

U5

An antitranspirant spray partly closes the stomata. Why does it reduce water loss but also slow growth?

Show answer

Smaller stomatal pores let less water vapour diffuse out, so transpiration falls.

But the same pores let carbon dioxide in. With less CO₂ absorbed, there is less photosynthesis, less glucose, and less growth. The plant cannot save water without also cutting its CO₂ supply.

U6

A plant grown without magnesium has yellow leaves and grows poorly. A plant without nitrate is green but small. Explain each.

Show answer

No magnesium: it cannot make chlorophyll, so the leaves are yellow and absorb less light. Less photosynthesis means less glucose and poor growth.

No nitrate: it can still make chlorophyll and glucose, but cannot turn the glucose into amino acids and proteins, so growth is stunted.

U7

Why must a plant be destarched before a starch-test experiment, and why is the ethanol heated in a water bath?

Show answer

Destarching removes starch made before the experiment. Otherwise a blue-black result could come from old starch and would prove nothing.

Water bath: ethanol is flammable, so it must never be heated over a naked flame. The Bunsen is turned off and the ethanol sits in hot water.

U8

Why does a very high temperature reduce the rate of photosynthesis, when a high light intensity never does?

Show answer

Photosynthesis is controlled by enzymes. Too hot, and their active sites change shape — they denature — so the rate falls.

Light is not an enzyme. Once another factor limits the rate, extra light simply has no effect. It never damages the process in the way heat does.
