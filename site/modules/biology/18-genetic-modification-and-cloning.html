02How it actually works

Modules 15 to 17 showed that DNA codes for proteins and that organisms can be changed over generations by breeding. This module is about changing or copying organisms directly: moving a gene from one species to another, and making genetically identical copies.

What genetic modification is

Spec 5.16

Genetic modification means cutting a gene out of one organism and inserting it into another, which then makes the protein that gene codes for.

An organism that contains a gene from a different species is transgenic. A bacterium carrying the human insulin gene, a crop carrying a bacterial insecticide gene, and a fish carrying a growth gene are all transgenic.

It works because the genetic code is the same in all organisms: a bacterium's ribosomes read the human codons exactly as a human cell would, and make the human protein.

Restriction enzymes — the scissors

Spec 5.12 · 2022 Edexcel 2B Q1(c)

Restriction enzymes cut DNA at specific base sequences, leaving short single-stranded "sticky ends".

Each restriction enzyme recognises one particular sequence of bases, so it always cuts in the same place. It cuts the two strands a few bases apart, leaving a few unpaired bases hanging off each end. These unpaired bases are the sticky ends: they will base-pair with any complementary sticky end.

The key rule: cut the gene and the plasmid with the same restriction enzyme. Then their sticky ends are complementary and can pair up.

✗ "Ligase cuts out the gene." — the 2022 multiple choice put ligase in as a wrong answer.
✓ "Restriction enzymes cut; ligase joins."

Ligase — the glue

Spec 5.12

DNA ligase joins pieces of DNA together, sealing the gene into the plasmid.

Once the sticky ends of the gene and the cut plasmid have paired up, the backbones of the two pieces still have gaps in them. Ligase joins the sugar–phosphate backbones, so the gene becomes a permanent part of the plasmid. The result — DNA combined from two different organisms — is recombinant DNA.

Vectors — plasmids and viruses

Spec 5.13

A vector carries the new gene into the cell being modified; plasmids and viruses can both act as vectors.

VectorWhat it isHow it delivers the gene
PlasmidA small, circular ring of DNA found in bacteria, separate from the main chromosomeTaken up by a bacterium, which copies it every time it divides
VirusA particle that injects its genetic material into a host cellThe gene is inserted into the virus's DNA; the virus carries it into the host cell

Making human insulin in bacteria

Spec 5.14 · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q9(a)

The human insulin gene is cut out, sealed into a bacterial plasmid, and put into bacteria, which multiply in a fermenter and make insulin.

  1. A restriction enzyme cuts the insulin gene out of human DNA, leaving sticky ends.
  2. The same restriction enzyme cuts open a bacterial plasmid, giving complementary sticky ends.
  3. The sticky ends pair up, and ligase joins the gene into the plasmid — recombinant DNA.
  4. The plasmid, now a vector, is taken up by a bacterium.
  5. The bacteria are grown in a fermenter with nutrients, at the right temperature and pH. They multiply rapidly; every new bacterium carries the gene.
  6. The bacteria make human insulin, which is extracted and purified for people with diabetes.

The four marks the 2023 scheme gave

1. A restriction enzyme is used to cut out the insulin gene 1

2. The same restriction enzyme cuts the bacterial plasmid 1

3. Producing complementary sticky ends 1

4. Ligase joins the gene into the plasmid 1

A fifth creditworthy point: the plasmid is inserted into a bacterium. Five points for four marks — write all five.

Why bacteria, and why human insulin

Spec 5.14

Bacteria make a good factory because they reproduce very fast and read the human gene exactly.

  • Rapid reproduction — one modified bacterium becomes billions in a fermenter within days.
  • Same genetic code — the insulin they make is identical to human insulin, so fewer allergic reactions than insulin taken from pigs or cattle.
  • Large amounts, cheaply and reliably, without depending on animals from slaughterhouses.
  • Plasmids are easy to cut, modify and put back into bacteria.

The same method is used for other human proteins, such as human growth hormone.

Genetically modified crops

The 2022 passage used a gene for suberin, a slow-decaying, carbon-rich substance, so crops would lock up carbon from the air. Carbon dioxide is taken in for photosynthesis and stored in the suberin, which decomposes very slowly — so less carbon dioxide returns to the air.

Weighing up genetic modification

An evaluation needs points on both sides, taken from the data in front of you, plus a comment on how good that data is.

The GM tilapia question is the model. The GM fish grew faster and wasted less food, so there were fewer faeces, less decomposition by bacteria, less oxygen used up, and less eutrophication — points for "better for the environment". Points against: no data on disease, the fish are genetically similar so a disease could spread easily, escaped GM fish could breed with wild fish or outcompete them, and the study was not repeated.

Clones and micropropagation

Spec 5.17B · 2023 Edexcel 2B Q6(a)(ii)

A clone is a genetically identical copy; micropropagation makes many plant clones from tiny pieces of one plant.

Clones are made by mitosis, which copies the chromosomes exactly. Micropropagation (tissue culture):

  1. Take small pieces of tissue from the plant — explants, often from the shoot tips.
  2. Sterilise them — e.g. wash in dilute bleach or ethanol — to kill microorganisms.
  3. Place them on sterile agar in a container.
  4. The agar contains nutrients — glucose, mineral ions such as nitrate, amino acids — and plant hormones such as auxin, which make the cells divide and grow roots and shoots.
  5. Keep sterile and warm, in the light. Each explant grows into a small plant.
  6. Transfer the plantlets to compost to grow on.

✗ "Put it on agar with nutrients."
✓ "Agar containing glucose and mineral ions, and plant hormones such as auxin."

The 2023 scheme ignored "nutrient" on its own. Name them.

Why growers use micropropagation

Spec 5.18B · 2022 Edexcel 2B Q1(e) · 2023 Edexcel Jan 2B Q4(a)

Micropropagation gives large numbers of genetically identical plants, quickly, from a single valuable plant.

  • Large numbers of plants from one parent — thousands from a few cells.
  • Fast, and can be done at any time of year.
  • All the plants are genetically identical, so all have the desired characteristic — e.g. all make suberin, all have low saturated fat.
  • No need for pollination — useful when only one transgenic plant exists, with nothing to breed it with, and it avoids cross-pollinating wild plants.
  • Can save rare or endangered plant species.

The downside is the same as for any clone: no variation, so a single disease or change in conditions could kill every plant.

The 2022 scheme ignored "faster growth of plants". Micropropagation is a faster way to produce plants; the plants themselves do not grow any faster.

Cloning a mammal — Dolly the sheep

Spec 5.19B · 2024 Edexcel 2B Q1(d)(ii)

To clone a mammal, the nucleus of a body cell is put into an egg cell whose own nucleus has been removed, and the embryo is grown in a surrogate mother.

  1. Take an egg cell from a female and remove its nucleus — it is now enucleated.
  2. Take a body cell (for Dolly, an udder cell) from the adult to be cloned, and remove its nucleus. It is diploid — it has a full set of chromosomes.
  3. Insert the body-cell nucleus into the empty egg cell.
  4. Give an electric shock, which stimulates the cell to divide.
  5. It divides by mitosis into an embryo.
  6. Implant the embryo into the uterus of a surrogate mother, where it develops.

The lamb is a clone of the animal that gave the body cell — not the egg donor and not the surrogate. All its chromosomes came from that one nucleus. The 2024 passage applied the same steps to cells stored in a cryozoo: an endangered banteng was cloned using its stored body cells and an egg from another species.

Cloned transgenic animals

Spec 5.20B

Genetic modification and cloning can be combined, so one transgenic animal becomes a herd that makes a useful human protein.

  1. A human gene for a useful protein is inserted into an animal — often so that the protein is made in its milk.
  2. That one transgenic animal is cloned, using the method above.
  3. Every clone carries the gene, so the whole herd produces the protein, which is extracted from the milk.

Sheep and goats have been used this way to produce human proteins for treating diseases, such as proteins that control blood clotting.

For cloning animalsAgainst cloning animals
Many identical copies of an animal with a valuable featureNo variation — one disease could wipe out all of them
Faster than breeding; the feature is not diluted by a mate's allelesLow success rate — many embryos fail
Could save endangered — or even extinct — speciesClones may have health problems and shorter lives; ethical concerns

04Understanding it

U1

Why must the same restriction enzyme cut both the human DNA and the plasmid?

Show answer

Each restriction enzyme cuts at one specific base sequence, leaving particular sticky ends. Using the same enzyme gives the gene and the plasmid complementary sticky ends.

The bases pair up, holding the gene in place so ligase can join it into the plasmid. Different enzymes would leave ends that do not match.

U2

A body cell is taken from a black sheep, an egg from a white sheep, and the embryo implanted into a brown sheep. What colour is the lamb, and why?

Show answer

Black. The lamb's DNA all comes from the nucleus inserted into the egg — from the black sheep's body cell.

The white sheep's egg had its nucleus removed, so contributes no genes. The brown surrogate only provides the uterus.

U3

How can a bacterium make a human protein when it has never had human genes before?

Show answer

The genetic code is the same in all organisms: the same codons code for the same amino acids in bacteria and humans.

Once the human gene is in the bacterium, its ribosomes read the gene's code and join the amino acids in exactly the same order as a human cell would. So the protein made is identical to human insulin.

U4

Why is the nucleus for cloning taken from a body cell and not from a sperm or egg?

Show answer

A body cell is diploid — its nucleus holds the full set of chromosomes, the complete genome of the animal being cloned.

Sperm and eggs are made by meiosis, so they are haploid — half the chromosomes, and each gamete a different mix. An embryo from one would have too few chromosomes and would not be identical to the parent.

U5

Scientists have produced one transgenic plant. Why do they copy it by micropropagation instead of letting it reproduce sexually?

Show answer

There is only one transgenic plant, so there is nothing of the same kind to breed it with. Sexual reproduction would also mix its alleles with another plant's, so not every offspring would carry the new gene.

Micropropagation uses mitosis, so every new plant is genetically identical — all carry the gene. It is fast, gives large numbers, works at any time of year, and needs no pollination, so the gene is not spread to wild plants.

U6

Why must the explants and agar be kept sterile in micropropagation?

Show answer

The agar contains glucose and nutrients, so it is ideal food for bacteria and fungi. Any microorganism that gets in would grow fast and compete with, infect or kill the tiny plant pieces.

Sterilising the explants and working with sterile equipment stops this, so the plant cells get the nutrients.

U7

A farmer grows only one variety of GM, herbicide-resistant maize. Give one way this increases yield and one risk it creates.

Show answer

Yield: the farmer can spray weedkiller that kills the weeds but not the maize. With fewer weeds there is less competition for light, water and mineral ions, so the maize grows better.

Risk: the plants are genetically very similar — little variation — so a new disease could affect them all. The resistance gene could also spread to wild relatives by pollination, making herbicide-resistant weeds.

U8

A goat is genetically modified so that its milk contains a human protein, then cloned. Why clone it rather than breed from it?

Show answer

Breeding would mix the transgenic goat's alleles with a mate's, so only some offspring — about half, if the goat has one copy of the gene — would inherit the new gene, and it takes a generation to find out which.

Every clone is genetically identical to the transgenic goat, so every one carries the gene and makes the protein. The herd grows faster and reliably.
