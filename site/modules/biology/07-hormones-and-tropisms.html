02How it actually works

Animals coordinate responses two ways: nervously, using electrical impulses, and hormonally, using chemicals carried in the blood. Plants use chemicals only. Both systems are used to keep conditions inside the body steady — that is homeostasis.

Responding to change

Spec 2.79, 2.80

Organisms survive by detecting changes in their surroundings and responding to them.

A change is a stimulus. It is picked up by a receptor, and the response is carried out by an effector — a muscle or a gland. In animals, nerves or hormones carry the message from one to the other.

  1. Stimulus — a change, such as a rise in temperature or a bright light.
  2. Receptor — detects the change.
  3. Coordination — by the nervous system or by hormones.
  4. Effector — a muscle contracts or a gland secretes.
  5. Response — the action that follows.

Plants respond too, but more slowly. They have no nerves, so they respond by growing — towards light, or down with gravity. That is a tropism.

Hormones — the definition that earns marks

Spec 2.89 · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q1

A hormone is a chemical messenger made in an endocrine gland, carried in the blood, that acts on target organs.

All four elements matter. Students routinely lose a mark by saying hormones travel "around the body" rather than in the blood, or by leaving out that they act on specific target organs rather than everything.

The three-mark definition

1. A chemical messenger 1

2. Made by an endocrine gland and carried in the blood 1

3. Acts on a target organ 1

Nervous versus hormonal

Spec 2.80 · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q1(b)

Nerves are fast, short-lived and precise; hormones are slower, longer-lasting and widespread.

FeatureNervousHormonal
SignalElectrical impulseChemical in the blood
Carried byNeuronesThe bloodstream

DurationShort-livedLong-lasting
TargetPrecise — one muscle or glandWidespread — any organ with the right receptors

Speed and duration are the two comparisons worth having ready, and they must be phrased as comparisons: "nervous is faster than hormonal", not "nervous is fast".

The 2022 real paper turned this into a calculation. Hormones travel at 420 cm per minute; nerve impulses at 55 m per second. Get both into the same units first:

Comparing two speeds as a ratioratio = faster speed ÷ slower speed, written as n : 1420 cm/min = 4.2 m/min = 0.07 m/s · 55 ÷ 0.07 = 786 : 1

Adrenaline, explained rather than listed

Spec 2.89 · 2024 Y10 Q2(c)

Every effect of adrenaline gets more oxygen and glucose to the muscles, so more respiration releases more energy for "fight or flight".

Adrenaline is released when you are frightened or stressed. Increased heart rate and increased breathing rate deliver more oxygen and glucose to the muscles, so more respiration can occur and more energy is released for movement. Blood glucose rises to supply that respiration. Pupils dilate to let more light into the eye, so you can see the danger.

The school's five marks for adrenaline's advantages

More light into the eye 1; to see danger 1; more blood to the leg muscles 1; more oxygen and glucose to the muscles 1; more respiration, more energy released, run away faster 1

A list of effects with no reason attached scores far less.

Homeostasis and negative feedback

Spec 2.81

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a constant internal environment. The spec's two examples are body water content and body temperature. Blood glucose is controlled the same way.

All three work by negative feedback:

  1. A change away from the normal level is detected.
  2. A response is triggered that opposes the change.
  3. The level returns towards normal.
  4. Once it is back to normal, the response is switched off.

Why bother? Enzymes work best at about 37 °C, and cells need the right amount of water to avoid shrinking or bursting by osmosis. Keep conditions steady and the enzymes and cells keep working.

Blood glucose — insulin and glucagon

Spec 2.89 · 2022 Y10 Q6(b) · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q9(b)

The pancreas detects blood glucose and releases insulin to lower it or glucagon to raise it.

Blood glucosePancreas releasesWhat the liver doesResult
Too high — after a mealInsulinConverts glucose into glycogen and stores it; cells take up more glucoseBlood glucose falls
Too low — after exercise or fastingGlucagonConverts glycogen back into glucose and releases itBlood glucose rises

✗ "The liver releases glucagon."
✗ "Insulin turns glucose into glucagon."
✓ "The pancreas releases glucagon. Glucagon makes the liver convert glycogen into glucose."

Glucagon is the hormone. Glycogen is the stored carbohydrate. One letter apart — check which one you wrote.

In type 1 diabetes the pancreas makes too little insulin, so blood glucose rises too high. It is treated with insulin injections. Insulin cannot be swallowed: it is a protein, so it would be digested by proteases in the stomach and small intestine. Diabetics also limit carbohydrates in their diet and monitor exercise, because exercise uses up glucose and could make it fall too low.

Reading temperature data — the cyclist

2022 Y10 Q6(a)

During exercise core temperature rises because respiration releases heat; skin temperature can fall because sweat evaporates.

The 2022 paper gave a graph of a cyclist's core and skin temperatures. The six marks were a chain for each line:

  1. Core temperature rises — the muscles contract more, so there is more respiration, which releases energy as heat.

Plant tropisms

Spec 2.79 · 2024 Y10 Q1(b)

A tropism is a growth response towards or away from a stimulus. "Positive" means towards; "negative" means away. Two to know:

TropismStimulusShootsRoots
PhototropismLightPositive — grow towardsNegative
Gravitropism (geotropism)GravityNegative — grow awayPositive — grow down

The advantages: shoots growing towards light get more light for photosynthesis; roots growing down reach water and mineral ions, and anchor the plant.

Auxin — the mechanism in shoots

Spec 2.79

Auxin is a plant hormone made at the shoot tip that makes cells elongate.

  1. Auxin is made at the tip of the shoot.
  2. Light comes from one side, and auxin moves to the shaded side.
  3. That gives an unequal distribution: more auxin on the shaded side than the lit side.
  4. The cells on the shaded side elongate more.
  5. The shaded side grows longer, so the shoot bends towards the light.

Answers saying the plant "grows towards the light because it wants light" score nothing — plants have no intent, and the mark is for the mechanism.

Auxin in roots, and the tip experiments

Spec 2.79

In roots, auxin inhibits elongation, so the side with more auxin grows less.

Gravity makes auxin collect on the lower side of a root. The lower side elongates less than the upper side, so the root bends downwards. Same hormone, same kind of unequal distribution, opposite effect.

04Understanding it

U1

A shoot on a windowsill bends towards the window. Explain fully, in terms of auxin.

Show answer

Auxin is produced at the tip of the shoot. Because light is coming from one side, the auxin moves away from the light and accumulates on the shaded side.

This gives an unequal distribution of auxin — a higher concentration on the shaded side than the lit side.

Auxin causes cell elongation, so the cells on the shaded side grow longer than those on the lit side. The shaded side of the shoot therefore becomes longer, and the shoot bends towards the light.

The advantage is that the leaves receive more light, so the plant can photosynthesise more.

U2

Why does a root grow downwards when auxin also collects on its lower side?

Show answer

Gravity causes auxin to accumulate on the lower side of the root, just as it collects on the shaded side of a shoot.

The difference is the effect: in roots, auxin inhibits cell elongation rather than promoting it. So the lower side, with more auxin, elongates less than the upper side.

The upper side grows more, so the root bends downwards — positive gravitropism. This takes it towards water and mineral ions, and anchors the plant.

The same hormone, the same distribution, opposite effect. That reversal is the whole point of the question.

U3

A student is startled by a loud bang. Their heart races for several minutes. Explain why the response involves both control systems.

Show answer

Nervous: the sound is detected and an impulse travels along neurones to produce the immediate jump or flinch. This is very fast but short-lived.

Hormonal: adrenaline is released from the adrenal glands into the blood. It travels to target organs including the heart, increasing heart rate and breathing rate.

Because the hormone must be carried in the blood, this response is slower to start but lasts much longer — which is why the heart is still racing minutes after the bang, long after the nervous response has finished.

U4

If the tip is cut off a shoot, it stops bending towards light. What does this show?

Show answer

It shows that the tip is where auxin is produced. Removing it removes the source of the hormone, so no unequal distribution can build up and no unequal growth occurs — the shoot grows straight.

Covering the tip with an opaque cap gives the same result, showing that the tip is also where the light is detected. Covering the shoot below the tip while leaving the tip exposed still produces bending, which confirms it.

U5

Why does vasodilation cool you down, and why is "the blood vessels move nearer the skin" wrong?

Show answer

The arterioles that supply the skin capillaries widen. More blood flows through the capillaries just under the surface. That blood is warm, so more heat is lost by radiation to the air.

The vessels stay exactly where they are. What changes is how much blood goes through them. Examiners reject "vessels move" because it describes something that never happens.

U6

A person with type 1 diabetes must inject insulin. Why can't they just swallow it as a tablet?

Show answer

Insulin is a protein. In the stomach and small intestine, proteases would digest it into amino acids — exactly as they do with the protein in food.

The amino acids have no hormone effect. Injecting puts the insulin straight into the blood, where it can travel to its target organs, such as the liver.

U7

During a long ride, a cyclist's core temperature rises but their skin temperature falls. How can both happen at once?

Show answer

Core rises: the leg muscles contract much more, so there is much more respiration. Respiration releases energy as heat, faster than the body can lose it.

Skin falls: the body responds by sweating. The sweat evaporates, and evaporation takes heat from the skin surface, cooling it.

The skin is where heat is being lost; the core is where it is being made. So they can move in opposite directions.

U8

After a meal rich in pasta, blood glucose rises, then returns to normal. Explain why this is negative feedback.

Show answer

Starch in the pasta is digested to glucose, which is absorbed into the blood. The pancreas detects the rise and releases insulin.

Insulin makes the liver convert glucose into glycogen, and cells take up more glucose. Blood glucose falls back to normal, and less insulin is released.

It is negative feedback because the response opposes the change: a rise causes a fall. If blood glucose dropped too low, glucagon would do the reverse.
