From the school's January 2023 mock scheme, on the conditions for germination:

"Light for photosynthesis does not score for germination — seeds do not photosynthesise yet."

The three conditions are water, oxygen and a suitable temperature — each with its reason: water activates enzymes, oxygen for respiration, warmth for enzyme action.

02How it actually works

A flower is a plant's reproductive organ. Its job is to get pollen from one flower to another, then turn a fertilised ovule into a seed that can grow somewhere new. Plants can also skip all of that and clone themselves.

Parts of a flower

Spec 3.3 · 2024 Edexcel 1B Q2(a) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1B Q2(a)

The male parts (stamens) make pollen; the female parts (carpel) receive it and hold the ovules.

PartJob
AntherMakes pollen, which contains the male gamete
FilamentStalk holding the anther (anther + filament = stamen)
StigmaWhere pollen lands — and where the pollen grain germinates
StyleStalk the pollen tube grows down
OvaryContains the ovules; becomes the fruit
OvuleContains the female gamete; becomes the seed
PetalsAttract insects in insect-pollinated flowers
SepalsProtect the flower while it is a bud
NectaryMakes sugary nectar to attract insects

Stigma + style + ovary = the carpel. On a diagram, the stigma is at the top of the central column and the ovary is the swelling at the bottom.

Insect-pollinated flowers

Spec 3.3 · 2024 Y11 mock Q9(b) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1B Q1(b)

An insect-pollinated flower is built to attract an insect and make it brush against the anthers and stigma.

  • Large, brightly coloured, scented petals — attract insects
  • Nectary — sugary nectar as a reward
  • Anthers inside the flower, firmly attached — pollen rubs onto the insect as it reaches the nectar
  • Stigma inside the flower and sticky — pollen from the insect sticks to it
  • Pollen grains larger, sticky or spiky — cling to the insect's body

Wind-pollinated flowers

Spec 3.3 · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q5(a) · 2024 Edexcel 1B Q2(b)

A wind-pollinated flower is built to release pollen into the wind and catch pollen from it — so everything hangs outside.

FeatureInsect-pollinatedWind-pollinated (e.g. grass)
PetalsLarge, bright, scentedSmall, green or absent
NectarNectary presentNone
AnthersInside the flower, firmly attachedHang outside on long, loose filaments
StigmaInside, stickyOutside, large and feathery to catch pollen
PollenLarger, sticky or spiky, less of itSmall, light, smooth, huge amounts

"How are W, X and Y adapted for wind pollination?" (2022 1B Q5(a)(i))

Stigma: feathery, large surface area, outside the flower — to catch pollen 1

Anther: outside the flower, exposed — so wind can blow the pollen away 1

Filament: long and loose — the anther hangs out and shakes in the wind 1

✗ "Wind-pollinated flowers do not attract insects." — that is the definition, not a structural difference.
✓ Compare the structure: "Petals are small and green; the stigma is outside and feathery."

Pollination — self and cross

Spec 3.3 · 2023 Y11 mock Q8(a) · 2024 Y11 mock Q9(a)

Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma.

  • Self-pollination — anther to stigma of the same plant. Less variation.
  • Cross-pollination — anther of one plant to stigma of another plant of the same species. Gametes with different alleles fuse, so the offspring show more variation.

On a diagram, the pollination arrow goes from the anther to the stigma — not to the ovary, and not from the stigma back to the anther.

From pollen to fertilisation

Spec 3.2, 3.4 · 2024 Edexcel 1B Q2(a)(iii)

Fertilisation happens after pollination, when the male nucleus travels down a pollen tube and fuses with the female nucleus in the ovule.

  1. Pollen lands on the stigma.
  2. The pollen grain germinates and grows a pollen tube down through the style to the ovary.
  3. The tube enters an ovule (through a tiny hole, the micropyle).
  4. The male nucleus travels down the tube into the ovule.
  5. It fuses with the female nucleus — that fusion is fertilisation. A zygote forms.

✗ "Pollination is when the male and female gametes fuse."
✗ "Pollen grains germinate in the ovary."
✓ "Pollination is the transfer of pollen. The pollen grain germinates on the stigma. Fertilisation is the fusion of nuclei in the ovule."

Seeds and fruits form

Spec 3.4 · 2024 Y11 mock Q9(a)(iii)

After fertilisation, the ovule becomes the seed and the ovary becomes the fruit.

BeforeAfter fertilisation
ZygoteDivides by mitosis into the embryo (young root and shoot)
OvuleThe seed — embryo, food store and a tough seed coat (testa)
OvaryThe fruit, containing the seeds
Petals, stamens, stigmaWither and fall off

One ovary can hold many ovules, so one fruit can hold many seeds — each from a separate pollen grain.

Why seeds are spread

2023 Y11 mock Q8(b), (d)–(g) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1B Q1(c)

Seeds that land far from the parent do not compete with it for light, water and mineral ions. Dispersal is not a named spec point, but both the mock and a real paper asked it.

  • Animals: bright, sweet fruit is eaten; the seeds pass through the gut undigested and are egested somewhere else. Hooked fruits catch on fur.
  • Wind: light seeds with wings or fluffy parachutes fall slowly, so the wind carries them further.

The mock also gave one mark for the downside: a seed that lands near the parent grows in conditions that already suited the parent.

Conditions for germination

Spec 3.5 · 2023 Y11 mock Q8(c)

Seeds need water, oxygen and a suitable temperature to germinate — and not light.

ConditionWhy it is needed
WaterMakes the seed swell; activates enzymes that break down the food store
OxygenFor aerobic respiration, releasing energy for growth
WarmthA suitable temperature for the enzymes to work

Light is not needed — seeds live on their stored food until the first leaves appear. The Year 11 mock explicitly refused "light for photosynthesis" as a germination condition.

The germination practical

Spec 3.5

Remove one condition from each tube and keep the others; only the tube with all three germinates.

TubeSet-upTestsResult

DMoist cotton wool in a fridge, 4 °CToo coldNo or very slow germination

Use the same number and type of seeds in each tube, repeat the set-up, and count the percentage that germinate after a set time, e.g. five days. An alternative for tube C is a sealed flask with pyrogallol or soda lime to absorb oxygen.

How a germinating seed feeds itself

Spec 3.6

A germinating seed uses its stored food for respiration and growth until its leaves can photosynthesise.

  1. The seed absorbs water, which activates its enzymes.
  2. Amylase digests stored starch into sugars; proteases digest stored protein into amino acids.
  3. The sugars are used in respiration to release energy, and to build new cells by mitosis.
  4. The root grows down, the shoot grows up towards the light.
  5. Once the first leaves open, the seedling photosynthesises and makes its own food.

So the dry mass of a seedling falls for the first few days — respiration turns stored food into carbon dioxide — then rises once photosynthesis makes more glucose than respiration uses.

Asexual reproduction — runners

Spec 3.7 · 2024 Edexcel 1B Q2(c)(i) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1B Q2(c)(i)

A runner is a sideways stem that grows along the ground and roots to form a new, genetically identical plant — a natural method of asexual reproduction.

Strawberries and spider plants do this. The new plant grows from the parent's cells by mitosis, with no gametes and no fertilisation, so it is a clone. Once rooted, the runner can be cut and the new plant lives on its own.

Asexual reproduction — cuttings

Spec 3.7 · 2024 Edexcel 1B Q2(c)(ii) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1B Q2(c)

A cutting is a piece of stem that a gardener plants so it grows roots — an artificial method that makes clones.

  1. Cut a short piece of stem with a few leaves from the parent plant.
  2. Remove the lower leaves, to reduce water loss by transpiration.
  3. Dip the cut end in rooting powder (plant hormone) to make roots grow.
  4. Plant it in damp compost and cover with a plastic bag to keep the air humid.
Cloning plants: advantagesDisadvantages
Every plant has the same desired characteristics — colour, flavour, yieldNo genetic variation — one disease or change in conditions can kill them all
Faster than growing from seed; no pollination neededNo new combinations of characteristics
Works for plants whose seeds are not viable, or rare plants

The 2024 scheme accepted cuttings, grafting or layering as artificial methods — but rejected tissue culture for that part. Say cuttings.

04Understanding it

U1

Explain why wind-pollinated flowers produce far more pollen than insect-pollinated ones.

Show answer

Wind carries pollen randomly, so almost all of it lands nowhere useful. Producing huge amounts makes it likely that some reaches a stigma.

Insects carry pollen directly from flower to flower, so much less is wasted and less needs to be made.

U2

In the germination practical, why is the water in one tube boiled, cooled and covered with oil?

Show answer

Boiling removes dissolved oxygen from the water. Cooling avoids killing the seeds with heat. The oil layer stops oxygen from the air dissolving back in.

So that tube tests whether oxygen is needed, with water and warmth still present. The seeds do not germinate because they cannot respire aerobically to release energy for growth.

U3

Some fruits are brightly coloured and sweet. Suggest how this helps the plant.

Show answer

The colour attracts animals, which eat the fruit for its sugars. The seeds pass through the gut undigested and are egested far from the parent.

That disperses the seeds, so the new plants do not compete with the parent for light, water and minerals.

U4

A bee carries pollen from a rose to an apple flower. The apple flower is pollinated, but no seeds form. Why?

Show answer

Pollen landing on a stigma is pollination — the transfer. Fertilisation only happens if the pollen grain germinates and grows a tube down the style to the ovule.

Pollen from a different species does not grow a working pollen tube on the apple's stigma, so no male nucleus reaches an ovule, no nuclei fuse, and no seed forms. Pollination and fertilisation really are two separate events.

U5

A pea pod contains six peas. How many ovules, ovaries and pollen grains were involved, and which part of the plant is the pod?

Show answer

The pod is the fruit, so it came from one ovary. Each pea is a seed, so there were six ovules, each fertilised.

Each fertilisation needs its own male nucleus, so at least six pollen grains must have germinated and grown six pollen tubes.

U6

Dry seeds can be stored for years, but once they are soaked their oxygen uptake rises sharply. Explain why.

Show answer

In a dry seed, enzymes are inactive and respiration is extremely slow — the seed is dormant.

Water activates the enzymes. Amylase digests the starch store into sugars, and the seed respires them aerobically to release energy for growth. Aerobic respiration uses oxygen, so oxygen uptake rises. That is also why oxygen is one of the three germination conditions.

U7

The dry mass of a batch of seedlings falls for the first five days, then rises. Explain both parts.

Show answer

Falling: the seedlings have no leaves yet. They respire their stored starch, and the carbon leaves as carbon dioxide, so mass is lost.

Rising: once the leaves open, the seedlings photosynthesise. When they make glucose faster than they respire it, they build new material and the dry mass increases.

U8

A grower has one apple tree with perfect fruit. Why would she take cuttings rather than plant its seeds, and what is the risk?

Show answer

Seeds come from sexual reproduction: meiosis and fertilisation mix alleles, so the new trees would vary and most would not have the perfect fruit. Cuttings grow by mitosis, so they are clones with exactly the same characteristics — and they grow faster.

The risk: every tree is genetically identical, so if one is susceptible to a disease or a change in climate, they all are.
