02How it actually works

An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a reaction without being used up. Everything else in this topic follows from one fact: an enzyme works because of its shape. Change the shape and it stops working.

What an enzyme is

Spec 2.10

An enzyme is a biological catalyst. "Biological" because it is a protein made by living cells. "Catalyst" because it speeds up a reaction and is not used up.

Every chemical reaction in a living organism — building molecules, breaking them down, respiration — is controlled by enzymes. These are called metabolic reactions. Without enzymes they would happen far too slowly at body temperature to keep you alive.

The two-mark definition of a catalyst

1. A substance that increases the rate of a reaction 1

2. and is not used up / is unchanged at the end 1

"Makes reactions happen" is not enough. The reaction would happen anyway — just slowly.

The active site and the lock-and-key model

Spec 2.10

Each enzyme has an active site with a specific shape, and only one substrate fits it.

The substrate is the molecule the enzyme acts on. Its shape is complementary to the active site — it fits the way a key fits a lock. The reaction happens in four steps:

  1. The substrate collides with the enzyme and fits into the active site.
  2. They form an enzyme–substrate complex.
  3. The reaction takes place — the substrate is broken down, or joined to something.
  4. The products are released, and the enzyme is unchanged and ready to be used again.

Because the fit is so exact, each enzyme works on one substrate only. That is called specificity. Amylase cannot digest protein for the same reason a house key will not open a car.

Temperature, below the optimum — the half everyone forgets

Spec 2.11 · Y10 2023 Q7 · Y10 2024 Q4

Warming an enzyme up to its optimum makes the reaction faster, because molecules collide more often. Nothing about the enzyme's shape is involved yet.

  1. Higher temperature gives the enzyme and substrate molecules more kinetic energy.
  2. They move faster, so they collide more frequently.
  3. More collisions means more enzyme–substrate complexes form per second.
  4. So the rate of reaction increases.

This is exactly what the snail question wanted, and exactly what students missed. The scheme's marking points were "enzymes · more kinetic energy · more frequent collisions / enzyme substrate complexes form faster · faster respiration".

✗ "The snails at 23 °C are nearer the optimum temperature."
✓ "At higher temperature the enzymes and substrates have more kinetic energy, so they collide more often and more enzyme–substrate complexes form, so respiration and growth are faster."

Temperature, above the optimum — denaturing

Spec 2.11

Past the optimum, heat breaks the enzyme's shape, so the substrate no longer fits.

Human enzymes have an optimum of about 37 °C. Above it:

  1. The heat breaks the bonds that hold the protein in its folded shape.
  2. The active site changes shape.
  3. The substrate is no longer complementary to it, so it cannot fit.
  4. No enzyme–substrate complexes form — the enzyme is denatured and the rate falls to zero.

Denaturing is permanent. Cooling a denatured enzyme back down does not fix it, in the same way you cannot un-boil an egg.

✗ "The enzyme dies." — An enzyme is a protein, not a living thing. It cannot die.
✗ "The enzyme changes shape." — Too vague. Say the active site changes shape.
✓ "The active site changes shape, so the substrate no longer fits and the enzyme is denatured."

Reading a temperature graph

A rate–temperature graph rises gradually, peaks at the optimum, then falls steeply.

The two sides are not symmetrical, and the difference is worth a mark. The rise is gradual because kinetic energy increases steadily. The fall is steep because once the shape starts breaking, activity collapses quickly.

A question that shows only the rising half — like the snail data, 8 °C to 23 °C — is a kinetic energy question. A question that goes past the peak needs both explanations, one for each side.

pH — the same ending by a different route

Spec 2.13

Every enzyme has an optimum pH, and moving away from it in either direction denatures the enzyme.

EnzymeWhere it worksOptimum pH
Salivary amylaseMouthAbout 7
Pepsin (a protease)Stomach — hydrochloric acidAbout 2
Pancreatic enzymesSmall intestine — bile neutralises the acidAbout 8

Too acidic or too alkaline, the charges holding the protein's folds together are disrupted, the active site changes shape, and the substrate no longer fits. Same result as heat, different cause.

Note the direction does not matter. Students often assume only acid is damaging — but putting pepsin into pH 7 denatures it just as surely as putting amylase into pH 2.

Why herbivores need cellulase

Y11 Jan 2023 Q1(b)

Plant cell walls are made of cellulose, and only an enzyme called cellulase can break it down.

The three-mark chain from the mock scheme:

  1. Plants contain cellulose 1
  2. It is digested by cellulase 1
  3. into glucose, which releases energy in respiration 1

Humans do not make cellulase, so cellulose passes through us undigested as fibre. Specificity again: no enzyme with the right active site, no digestion.

The iodine test — how every enzyme practical is measured

Spec 2.12, 2.14B

Iodine turns blue-black when starch is present and stays orange-brown when it is gone. So it tells you when amylase has finished.

  1. Put single drops of iodine in the wells of a spotting tile.
  2. Mix amylase and starch solution in a test tube, in a water bath at a set temperature, and start a stopwatch.
  3. Every 10 seconds, move one drop of the mixture into a new well of iodine.
  4. While starch is present, the iodine turns blue-black.
  5. Record the time when the iodine stays orange-brown — all the starch has been digested.
  6. Repeat at other temperatures, or with buffer solutions of different pH.

A shorter time means a faster reaction. Rate and time go in opposite directions: rate ∝ 1 ÷ time. That is why a graph of time against pH is a valley, while a graph of rate against pH is a peak. Check which is on the axis before describing the shape.

Designing an enzyme investigation — six marks

Y11 Jan 2024 Q8(b) — effect of vinegar on starch digestion

Six-mark "design an investigation" questions are marked against a fixed checklist, so write to the checklist. This school uses CORMMSS:

CORMMSS — one mark each

C — Change: what you vary, with a range. "Use different concentrations of vinegar, and no vinegar." 1

O — Organism / object: same material each time. "Same mass of starch." 1

R — Repeat: "Repeat three times at each concentration and calculate a mean." 1

M1 — Measure what: "Test samples with iodine to see if starch remains." 1

M2 — Measure how: "Record the time until the iodine stays orange-brown." 1

S — Same: two controlled variables. "Same temperature using a water bath; same volume and concentration of amylase." 1

"Keep everything else the same" scores nothing. Every control variable must be named.

When the rate levels off without denaturing

A reaction can slow and stop even at the perfect temperature and pH. Two reasons, neither of which is denaturing:

  • The substrate is used up — nothing left to bind.
  • All the active sites are occupied — the enzyme is working flat out. Adding more substrate does nothing; adding more enzyme would speed it up.

If the question says conditions did not change, do not reach for "denatured".

04Understanding it

U1

Snails grow faster at 23 °C than at 8 °C. A student writes "because 23 °C is closer to the enzymes' optimum." The teacher gives 1 of 3. What was missing?

Show answer

The student described where 23 °C sits, not why that speeds things up.

The explanation below the optimum is about kinetic energy. At 23 °C the enzyme and substrate molecules move faster, so they collide more frequently, more enzyme–substrate complexes form, and reactions such as respiration run faster — so the snails grow more.

The test question: would the student's answer have scored better at 45 °C? No — there the snails would grow less, because the enzymes denature. "Closer to the optimum" never explains anything by itself.

U2

An enzyme is heated to 70 °C for five minutes, then cooled back to 37 °C. Does it start working again? Explain.

Show answer

No. At 70 °C the bonds holding the protein's shape broke, so the active site changed shape and the enzyme was denatured.

Denaturing is permanent. Cooling does not re-fold the protein into its original shape, so the substrate still does not fit.

The contrast worth knowing: an enzyme cooled to 5 °C does recover when warmed. Cold only slows the molecules down — it never changes the shape.

U3

Pepsin works in the stomach at pH 2. When food moves into the small intestine, pepsin stops working. Why, and why does that not matter?

Show answer

Why it stops: bile neutralises the stomach acid, so the pH rises to about 8. That is far from pepsin's optimum, so its active site changes shape and it is denatured.

Why it does not matter: the pancreas releases its own proteases into the small intestine, and their optimum is alkaline. Protein digestion simply carries on with a different enzyme suited to the new pH.

U4

In a pH practical the iodine stays orange-brown after 40 s at pH 6, 20 s at pH 7 and 55 s at pH 8. Which pH is fastest, and why is the shortest time the fastest rate?

Show answer

pH 7 — the shortest time.

The iodine stops going blue-black at the moment all the starch has been digested. Getting there in 20 s means the amylase worked twice as fast as at pH 6. Time and rate are inversely related.

Plot time on the y-axis and you get a valley with its lowest point at the optimum. Plot rate and you get a peak. Always read the axis label before describing the shape.

U5

Why can one enzyme not digest starch, protein and fat?

Show answer

Because of specificity. The active site has one particular shape, and only a substrate with a complementary shape can fit and form an enzyme–substrate complex.

Starch, protein and lipid molecules all have different shapes, so each needs its own enzyme — amylase, protease and lipase.

U6

A reaction slows and stops, although the temperature and pH have not changed. Give two possible reasons — neither of them denaturing.

Show answer

1. The substrate has run out. Nothing is left to bind, so no more product forms.

2. Every active site is occupied. The enzyme is saturated. More substrate would change nothing; more enzyme would speed it up.

How to tell them apart: add more substrate. If the reaction restarts, it had run out. If nothing happens, the enzyme was the limit.

U7

Humans eat plenty of plants but cannot release energy from the cellulose in them. Cows can. Explain the difference.

Show answer

Cellulose can only be broken down by the enzyme cellulase, because only cellulase has an active site complementary to it.

Humans do not produce cellulase, so cellulose passes through undigested. Cows have microorganisms in their gut that make cellulase, which digests the cellulose into glucose. The glucose is absorbed and used in respiration to release energy.

U8

Why is the enzyme–temperature graph steeper on the right of the peak than on the left?

Show answer

The two sides have different causes.

Left side: kinetic energy increases steadily with temperature, so collision frequency rises gradually and so does the rate.

Right side: once the temperature passes the optimum, bonds holding the protein's shape start breaking and more and more enzyme molecules denature at once. Activity collapses quickly — hence the steep drop.
