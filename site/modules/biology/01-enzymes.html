01How it actually works

An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a reaction without being used up — a biological catalyst. Its power comes entirely from its shape, which is why every mark in this topic traces back to one idea: change the shape, lose the function.

The active site and the lock-and-key model

Spec 2.10

Each enzyme has a pocket called the active site. The substrate is the molecule it acts on. The substrate's shape is complementary to the active site — it fits the way a key fits a lock — so each enzyme works on one substrate only. This is enzyme specificity.

When they meet, they form an enzyme–substrate complex. The reaction happens, the products leave, and the enzyme is unchanged and ready to go again. That last clause is a mark in its own right: an enzyme is not used up.

Why temperature has two halves to its answer

Spec 2.11 · practical 2.12

Below the optimum, heating speeds the reaction up for an ordinary reason: molecules gain kinetic energy, so enzyme and substrate collide more often. Nothing biological about it yet.

Above the optimum — around 37 °C in humans — the rate falls off a cliff. The heat breaks the bonds holding the protein in its folded shape, so the active site changes shape, the substrate no longer fits, and no enzyme–substrate complex can form. The enzyme is denatured.

A full-mark temperature answer therefore has two distinct halves, and students who write only the second half lose the first.

pH does the same thing by a different route

Spec 2.13 · practical 2.14B

Most enzymes have an optimum near pH 7, but ones that work in acid do not: pepsin in the stomach has an optimum around pH 2. Move the pH away from the optimum in either direction and the forces holding the protein's folds are disrupted, the active site changes shape, and the enzyme denatures — same ending, different cause.

The digestive enzymes

Spec 2.29

Three families, and the exam wants the named enzyme, not the family. "Carbohydrase" when the question wanted "amylase" is a common way to drop a mark.

StarchAmylaseMaltose
MaltoseMaltaseGlucose

Starch goes to glucose in two steps, not one: amylase takes it to maltose, then maltase finishes the job. Writing "amylase breaks starch into glucose" skips a stage the spec names explicitly.

The two practicals

Spec 2.12, 2.14B

Both use the same trick. Amylase digests starch; iodine goes blue-black when starch is present and stays orange-brown when it is gone. So you sample the mixture into iodine every 10 seconds, and the time for the blue-black to stop appearing measures how fast the enzyme worked.

For the temperature practical you vary temperature with a water bath and keep pH fixed. For the pH practical you add buffer solutions and keep temperature fixed in a water bath — the water bath is the control variable there, not the independent one. Examiners ask which is which.

02Cheat sheet

The sentence the whole topic is built on

Heat or wrong pH → the active site changes shape → the substrate no longer fits → no enzyme–substrate complex → the enzyme is denatured. Learn that chain and most of the marks follow.

03Understanding it

U1

A student says "as temperature rises, enzymes work faster, so the reaction is fastest at 80 °C." Identify what is right, what is wrong, and rewrite it as a full-mark answer.

Show answer

Right: rising temperature does speed the reaction up — at first. Wrong: that only holds below the optimum, and 80 °C is far above it.

Full-mark version: As temperature rises towards the optimum, the molecules gain kinetic energy and collide more frequently, so the rate increases. Above the optimum the bonds holding the enzyme's shape break, so the active site changes shape and the substrate is no longer complementary to it. No enzyme–substrate complexes form, the enzyme is denatured, and the rate falls to zero.

The reason this is worth practising: the mark scheme splits those into separate marks, so an answer that only describes the collapse still drops the first mark.

U2

Pepsin works in the stomach at pH 2. Amylase in the mouth works at pH 7. If you moved pepsin into the mouth, would it work? Explain in terms of the active site.

Show answer

No. pH 7 is far from pepsin's optimum of pH 2. The change in pH disrupts the forces holding the protein in its folded shape, so the active site changes shape. The substrate (protein) is no longer complementary to it, no enzyme–substrate complex forms, and pepsin is denatured.

Note the direction does not matter — moving an enzyme to a pH that is too alkaline denatures it just as moving it somewhere too acidic would. Students often assume only acid is damaging.

U3

In the pH practical, iodine stops turning blue-black after 40 s at pH 6, 20 s at pH 7 and 55 s at pH 8. What is the optimum, and why is a shorter time the faster reaction?

Show answer

Optimum ≈ pH 7 — the shortest time.

The iodine stops going blue-black at the moment the starch has all been digested. A shorter time therefore means the amylase cleared the starch faster, so the rate was higher. Rate and time are inversely related: rate ∝ 1 ÷ time.

That inversion catches people out — a graph of "time taken" against pH is a valley with its lowest point at the optimum, whereas a graph of "rate" against pH is a peak. Read which one the axis says before describing the shape.

U4

Why can one enzyme not digest starch, protein and fat?

Show answer

Because of specificity. The active site has one particular shape, and only a substrate whose shape is complementary to it can bind and form an enzyme–substrate complex. Starch, protein and lipid molecules have different shapes, so each needs an enzyme with a matching active site — amylase, protease and lipase respectively.

U5

An enzyme reaction is left running and the rate levels off, even though the temperature and pH have not changed. Give a reason.

Show answer

The substrate has been used up — with none left to bind, no more product can form.

The other acceptable reason, if the graph levels off early and substrate remains, is that all the active sites are occupied: the enzyme is saturated, so adding more substrate cannot speed things up. Adding more enzyme would.

Neither of these involves denaturing, which is why "the enzyme denatured" is the wrong reach here — nothing changed the conditions.

04Exam drill

D1

State what is meant by the term catalyst. (2)

Show answer

A substance that increases the rate of a reaction 1; and is not used up / is chemically unchanged at the end 1.

D2

Explain why an enzyme can only act on one type of substrate. (3)

Show answer

The enzyme has an active site 1; the substrate is complementary to / fits the shape of the active site 1; only a substrate with that shape can bind to form an enzyme–substrate complex 1.

D3

Explain the effect of increasing temperature from 20 °C to 60 °C on the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction. (4)

Show answer

The rate increases up to the optimum 1; because the molecules gain kinetic energy so collisions are more frequent 1; above the optimum the rate decreases 1; because the active site changes shape so the substrate no longer fits / the enzyme is denatured 1.

D4

Name the enzyme that digests each of the following, and give the products: starch, protein, lipid. (3)

Show answer

Starch — amylase (to maltose, then maltase to glucose) 1; protein — protease, to amino acids 1; lipid — lipase, to fatty acids and glycerol 1.

D5

A student investigates amylase activity at different pH values. State the independent variable, the dependent variable, and two control variables. (4)

Show answer

Independent: pH 1; dependent: time taken for the iodine to stop turning blue-black / for the starch to be digested 1; one control from temperature, volume of amylase, volume or concentration of starch 1; a second control from that list 1.

"Keep everything else the same" on its own scores nothing — the variables must be named.

D6

Explain why the term "denatured" is more accurate than saying the enzyme has been killed. (2)

Show answer

An enzyme is a protein, not a living organism, so it cannot be killed 1; denatured means its active site has changed shape so the substrate no longer fits 1.

D7

Describe how you would show that amylase digests starch, and how you would know when the reaction was complete. (4)

Show answer

Mix amylase and starch solution and keep at a constant temperature in a water bath 1; at regular intervals remove a drop with a pipette and add it to iodine solution on a tile 1; while starch is present the iodine turns blue-black 1; the reaction is complete when the iodine stays orange-brown 1.
