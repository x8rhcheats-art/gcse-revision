From the Edexcel mark scheme for the January 2023 translation question:

"Ignore to make protein unqualified"

"The ribosome makes the protein" scores nothing. The marks are for the mechanism: the ribosome reads the codons, tRNA brings the amino acids, the anticodon pairs with the codon, and the amino acids are joined into a chain.

02How it actually works

DNA is an instruction list. Its only job is to say which proteins to make — and the proteins (enzymes, haemoglobin, hormones like insulin) do everything else. This module is how the list is written, how it is read, and what happens when it is misprinted.

From genome to gene

Spec 3.14, 3.15 · 2024 Y11 mock Q3(a) · 2023 Edexcel 2B Q6(b)(ii)

The genome is all the DNA of an organism; a gene is a section of DNA that codes for a specific protein.

Read it as a zoom: genome → chromosome → gene → base sequence. Chromosomes are in the nucleus, and the genes are located on them.

✗ "A gene codes for a characteristic, like eye colour."
✓ "A gene is a section of DNA that codes for a specific protein." The protein then affects the characteristic.

The structure of DNA

Spec 3.16B · 2024 Edexcel 2B Q5(a)(i)

DNA is two strands coiled into a double helix, held together by pairs of bases.

Each strand is a long chain of units called nucleotides. Every nucleotide has three parts:

  • a sugar (deoxyribose)
  • a phosphate group
  • one of four bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) or guanine (G)

Sugar and phosphate alternate to form the backbone of each strand. The bases stick inwards and pair up across the middle, like the rungs of a twisted ladder.

Complementary base pairing

Spec 3.16B · 2024 Edexcel 2B Q5(a)(i)

A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G — so one strand fixes the other.

Base pairing — memoriseA – T    C – Gin RNA, U takes the place of T, so A pairs with U

Worked, the way the 2024 paper set it:

  1. Given strand: TAC CGT AAT TAT
  2. Swap each base for its partner: T→A, A→T, C→G, G→C.
  3. Complementary DNA strand: ATG GCA TTA ATA

The wrong options in that question contained U. DNA never contains uracil, so any DNA answer with U in it is out straight away.

✗ "A pairs with G" — both are the larger bases; they never pair.
✓ A–T and C–G in DNA; A–U when one side is RNA.

The mock gave one mark each for three differences. Each difference must be a comparison: "RNA has uracil" is half a point; "DNA has thymine, RNA has uracil instead" is the whole point.

Why the code has to be copied

Spec 3.18B · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q3(a)(iv)

DNA stays in the nucleus, but proteins are made at ribosomes in the cytoplasm — so a mobile copy, mRNA, carries the code out.

Think of DNA as the master copy of a recipe book that is never taken out of the library. You photocopy the one page you need and take the copy to the kitchen. The photocopy is mRNA; the kitchen is the ribosome.

That gives protein synthesis its two stages, and each has a fixed place:

StageWhereWhat goes inWhat comes out
TranscriptionNucleusDNA of one genemRNA
TranslationRibosome, in the cytoplasmmRNA + amino acids carried by tRNAA chain of amino acids — the protein

The 2024 paper tested the order as one line: DNA → transcription → RNA → translation → protein.

Transcription — DNA to mRNA

Spec 3.18B · 2022 Edexcel Jan 2B Q6(a) · 2022 Edexcel 2B Q6(b)

In transcription, one strand of a gene is used as a template to build a matching strand of mRNA.

  1. The DNA unzips at the gene — the bonds between the base pairs break and the two strands separate.
  2. Free RNA nucleotides line up against the exposed bases of one strand, by complementary base pairing.
  3. They join into a strand of messenger RNA (mRNA) — with U wherever T would have gone.
  4. The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a pore and travels to a ribosome.
  5. The DNA zips back up, unchanged.

Worked, from the January 2022 paper: DNA strand ATTCCGAGT gives mRNA UAAGGCUCA. Pair as normal, then check: is there any T left in your mRNA? If yes, it is wrong.

Translation — mRNA to protein

Spec 3.18B · 2023 Edexcel Jan 2B Q6(b)(iii) · 2024 Y11 mock Q3(c)

In translation, a ribosome reads the mRNA three bases at a time, and tRNA brings the matching amino acid for each triplet.

  1. The mRNA attaches to a ribosome.
  2. The ribosome reads the mRNA one codon — a triplet of three bases — at a time.
  3. Each tRNA (transfer RNA) molecule carries one specific amino acid, and has three bases of its own, the anticodon.
  4. A tRNA whose anticodon is complementary to the codon binds to it.
  5. Its amino acid is added to the chain; the ribosome moves on to the next codon.
  6. The amino acids are joined together into a chain — a polypeptide — which folds into the protein.

Translation — the four marks the 2023 scheme gave

1. Ribosomes bind to the mRNA; this is translation 1

2. Each triplet of bases, a codon, codes for one amino acid 1

3. tRNA brings amino acids; its anticodon binds to the codon 1

4. The amino acids are joined together to form a chain — a polypeptide 1

"To make a protein" on its own was ignored.

✗ "mRNA brings the amino acids to the ribosome."
✓ "tRNA brings the amino acids. mRNA carries the code."

✗ "The codon is on the tRNA."
✓ Codon on mRNA; anticodon on tRNA.

The triplet code

Spec 3.18B · 2024 Edexcel 2B Q5(b)(i)

Every three bases code for one amino acid, so the order of bases decides the order of amino acids.

Count your way through it: a gene 300 bases long codes for 300 ÷ 3 = 100 amino acids. Change the order of bases and you change the order of amino acids; change the order of amino acids and the protein folds into a different shape.

The code has more codons than amino acids, so many amino acids have more than one codon. If a paper gives you a codon table, it is usually testing this. For example:

Amino acidmRNA codonsNumber of codons
PhenylalanineUUU, UUC2
GlycineGGU, GGC, GGA, GGG4
MethionineAUG1

To count the codon combinations that give "phenylalanine – glycine – methionine", multiply: 2 × 4 × 1 = 8. If the question asks for the number of other combinations, take away the one you were given: 7.

From gene to characteristic

Spec 3.35B · 2022 Edexcel 2B Q6

A gene affects a characteristic because it decides the shape of a protein, and the shape decides what the protein can do.

This is the enzymes idea from module 1 again. An enzyme's active site only fits its substrate because its amino acids fold in one exact way. The same goes for haemoglobin, which carries oxygen: in beta thalassaemia a mutation in a haemoglobin gene means less working haemoglobin is made, so less oxygen reaches the muscles — less respiration, less energy released.

The chain of cause is always the same length:

  1. base sequence of DNA
  2. → codons in mRNA
  3. → sequence of amino acids
  4. → shape of the protein
  5. → how well it works — and so the phenotype

Mutations and how they change the phenotype

Spec 3.34, 3.35B · 2022 Edexcel Jan 2B Q6(c)

A mutation is a rare, random change in the base sequence of DNA, and it can be inherited.

A mutation can swap one base for another, add a base, or lose one. If it changes a codon, a different amino acid may go into the chain. That can change how the protein folds — for an enzyme, the active site may no longer fit the substrate.

How a mutation affects the phenotype — three marks

1. A mutation is a rare, random change in the DNA / base sequence 1

2. It changes the sequence of amino acids in the protein 1

3. So a different or non-working protein or enzyme is made, changing the phenotype 1

Mutations are also where new alleles come from. Without them there would be no variation for natural selection to act on — that story is module 17.

Why most mutations have no effect

Spec 3.36B · 2024 Edexcel 2B Q5(b)(ii) · 2023 Edexcel 2B Q6(b)(iii)

Most mutations have no effect on the phenotype, some have a small effect, and only rarely is the effect significant.

The 2024 scheme gave marks for any three of these reasons:

  • The new codon may code for the same amino acid — many amino acids have more than one codon.
  • So the protein is unchanged and works exactly as before.
  • Even if one amino acid changes, the protein's shape may not change — an enzyme's active site can still fit its substrate.
  • The new allele may be recessive, so it is not expressed while a dominant allele is present.
  • The change may be in DNA that does not code for a protein.

That is why, in the 2023 snapdragon question, the number of DNA changes rose faster with radiation than the number of new phenotypes: plenty of the mutations were silent.

What makes mutations more likely

Spec 3.37B · 2024 Edexcel 2B Q5(b)(iii) · 2022 Edexcel Jan 2B Q6(d)

Mutations happen naturally, but ionising radiation and some chemicals make them more frequent.

CauseExamples
Ionising radiationGamma rays, X-rays, ultraviolet rays, radioactive material
Chemical mutagensChemicals in tobacco smoke, such as tar

The butterfly question is the model for evaluating data on this. Abnormal butterflies near a radioactive leak became more common over time, and more of their offspring were abnormal — the mutations were being passed on, and recessive ones appeared when two carriers mated. But good answers also doubt the data: no control population, no data from before the leak, radiation not measured, sample size not given, and some abnormalities might not be genetic at all.

✗ "Radiation causes mutations, so all the abnormal butterflies were caused by the radiation."
✓ "Radiation increases the rate of mutation. Without a control group we cannot be sure the abnormalities were caused by it."

04Understanding it

U1

One strand of DNA reads ATTGCA. Write the complementary DNA strand and the mRNA made from the original.

Show answer

Complementary DNA: TAACGT — A pairs with T, C with G.

mRNA from ATTGCA: UAACGU — the same pairing, except U replaces T, because RNA has uracil instead of thymine.

Notice the mRNA is identical to the complementary DNA strand except for U in place of T. That is a quick way to check your answer.

U2

A single base change in a gene makes an enzyme stop working. Explain how.

Show answer

The mutation changes one codon, so a different amino acid may be placed in the chain during translation.

The sequence of amino acids decides how the protein folds. A different amino acid can change the folding, and so the shape of the active site. The substrate no longer fits, so the enzyme cannot work.

U3

Why can't the DNA itself go to the ribosome? Why does the cell bother making mRNA?

Show answer

DNA is an enormous molecule — a whole chromosome — and it stays in the nucleus, where it is kept safe. Ribosomes are out in the cytoplasm.

mRNA is a short, single-stranded copy of just one gene. It is small enough to leave the nucleus and carry the code to a ribosome. The master copy never leaves, so it is not damaged, and many mRNA copies can be made from one gene when a lot of protein is needed.

U4

A mutation changes the codon GGU to GGC. Both code for glycine. What happens to the protein and the phenotype?

Show answer

Nothing changes. The new codon still codes for glycine, so the same amino acid goes into the chain.

Same amino acid sequence → same shape → the protein works as before → no change to the phenotype. This is the commonest reason why most mutations have no effect.

U5

A mutation does change one amino acid in an enzyme, but the enzyme still works. How is that possible?

Show answer

The changed amino acid may be in a part of the enzyme that does not affect its shape much, or it may be similar to the one it replaced.

If the active site keeps its shape, the substrate still fits, and the enzyme still catalyses the reaction. What matters is the shape, not the exact list of amino acids.

U6

A person carries a harmful mutation in one allele of a gene but is perfectly healthy. Explain how.

Show answer

The mutated allele is recessive. The person is heterozygous: their other allele is normal and dominant, and it still codes for a working protein.

The mutation only shows in the phenotype if the person inherits two copies. It can still be passed on to their children — that is how carriers work (module 16).

U7

A gene is 1200 bases long on one strand. How many amino acids does it code for, and why is that the answer?

Show answer

1200 ÷ 3 = 400 amino acids.

The code is read in triplets: each codon of three bases codes for one amino acid. The ribosome moves along the mRNA one codon at a time, adding one amino acid per codon.

U8

Scientists find more abnormal butterflies near a nuclear accident than elsewhere. Why can't they be certain the radiation caused the abnormalities?

Show answer

Ionising radiation increases the rate of mutation, so the link is likely — but the study cannot prove it.

There is no control group or data from before the accident, so they do not know the normal level of abnormality. The radiation dose was not measured. Other factors — diet, disease, pollution — could cause abnormalities that are not genetic at all. Larger, repeated samples would make the conclusion stronger.
