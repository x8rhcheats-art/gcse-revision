The structure of DNA

Spec 3.16B

DNA is a double helix — two strands twisted together. Each strand is a chain of nucleotides, each made of a sugar (deoxyribose), a phosphate and a base.

There are four bases, and they pair by complementary base pairing: A with T, C with G. The pairs hold the two strands together. Knowing one strand lets you write the other.

Every three bases — a triplet, or codon — code for one amino acid. The order of bases decides the order of amino acids, which decides the protein.

Protein synthesis — two stages

Spec 3.17B, 3.18B

DNA is too large to leave the nucleus, but proteins are made at ribosomes in the cytoplasm. So the code is copied first.

Transcription (in the nucleus): the DNA unzips at the gene, and free RNA nucleotides pair with the exposed bases, forming mRNA — a copy of the code. The mRNA leaves the nucleus.

Translation (at the ribosome): the mRNA attaches to a ribosome, which reads its codons three bases at a time. tRNA molecules bring specific amino acids; each tRNA has an anticodon complementary to a codon. The amino acids are joined into a chain, which folds into the protein.

Mutations

Spec 3.34 – 3.36

A mutation is a rare, random change in the base sequence of DNA. It can change a codon, so a different amino acid is added, which can change the shape of the protein. An enzyme's active site may no longer fit its substrate.

Most mutations have no effect; some have a small effect; very few are significant. The rate rises with ionising radiation (UV, X-rays, gamma) and chemical mutagens such as those in tobacco smoke. Mutations are the source of new alleles.

03Understanding it

U1

One strand of DNA reads ATTGCA. Write the complementary DNA strand and the mRNA made from the original.

Show answer

Complementary DNA: TAACGT — A pairs with T, C with G.

mRNA from ATTGCA: UAACGU — the same pairing, except U replaces T, because RNA has uracil instead of thymine.

U2

A single base change in a gene makes an enzyme stop working. Explain how.

Show answer

The mutation changes one codon, so a different amino acid may be placed in the chain during translation.

The sequence of amino acids decides how the protein folds. A different amino acid can change the folding, and so the shape of the active site. The substrate no longer fits, so the enzyme cannot work.
