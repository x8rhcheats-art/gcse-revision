From the school's 2022 Year 10 mark scheme, on the axolotl and emphysema questions:

"'No respiration occurs' and 'energy production' are both rejected."

Less oxygen means less aerobic respiration, not none. And the energy is released. Those two words cost marks on two separate questions in the same paper.

02How it actually works

Respiration is the release of energy from glucose, in every living cell, all the time. The energy is used to make ATP, which cells then use for movement, growth, keeping warm and building large molecules. Respiration is not breathing: breathing is ventilation, covered in module 4.

What respiration is — and what ATP does

Spec 2.34, 2.35 · 2022 Y10 Q2(d)

Respiration is a chemical reaction in cells that releases energy from glucose and uses it to make ATP.

Energy cannot be created. It was stored in the glucose all along, and respiration releases it. That is why mark schemes reject "produces energy" outright.

ATP is the molecule that provides energy for cells. The energy released by respiration is used to make ATP, and ATP then carries it to whatever process needs it:

  • Muscle contraction — movement
  • Active transport — moving substances against a concentration gradient
  • Building large molecules — proteins from amino acids, for growth and repair
  • Cell division
  • Keeping warm — in mammals and birds some of the energy is released as heat

✗ "Respiration produces energy."
✗ "Respiration happens in the lungs."
✓ "Respiration releases energy from glucose, in every living cell, and the energy is used to make ATP."

Aerobic respiration — the equations

Spec 2.37 · 2024 Edexcel 1B Q4(a)

Aerobic respiration uses oxygen to break glucose down completely into carbon dioxide and water. It happens in the mitochondria.

Aerobic respiration — word equationglucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + waterenergy released and used to make ATP · in the mitochondria
Aerobic respiration — balanced symbol equationC₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂Ocorrect formulae one mark, balancing numbers the second

It is photosynthesis run backwards. Learn one and you have both.

✗ "C₆H₁₂O₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O" — correct formulae, unbalanced: one mark of two.
✗ "glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy" — energy is not a substance. Write it under the arrow, or leave it out.
✓ "C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O"

Anaerobic respiration in animals

Spec 2.38 · 2023 Y11 mock Q3

Without oxygen, animal cells break glucose down incompletely into lactic acid only.

Anaerobic respiration in animalsglucose → lactic acidone product · no carbon dioxide, no water

This happens in muscle during hard exercise, when oxygen cannot reach the cells fast enough. Some bacteria do the same thing: the bacteria in yoghurt respire anaerobically and make lactic acid, which is what makes the milk go sour and thick.

The animal equation has one product only. Adding carbon dioxide to it is one of the most common errors in the topic.

Anaerobic respiration in plants and yeast

Spec 2.38 · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q2 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1B Q8

Without oxygen, plant and yeast cells break glucose down into ethanol and carbon dioxide.

Anaerobic respiration in plants and yeastglucose → ethanol + carbon dioxidein yeast this is called fermentation

Yeast is a single-celled fungus. People use its anaerobic respiration in two ways:

  • Bread — the carbon dioxide makes the dough rise. The ethanol evaporates when it is baked.
  • Beer and wine — the ethanol is the product wanted.

Plant roots in waterlogged soil respire this way too, because the water pushes the air out of the soil.

Oxygen debt — why you keep panting

Spec 2.36 · 2022 Y10 Q4(b)

After hard exercise, extra oxygen is needed to break down the lactic acid that built up. That extra oxygen is the oxygen debt.

  1. During hard exercise the muscles need more energy than aerobic respiration alone can supply.
  2. They respire anaerobically as well, making lactic acid.
  3. Lactic acid builds up, causing fatigue and cramp.
  4. After exercise, breathing stays fast and deep to take in extra oxygen.
  5. The oxygen is used to break down the lactic acid. When it is gone, breathing returns to normal.

Less oxygen, less energy — the chain answer

2022 Y10 Q2(d) · 2022 Y10 Q4(b) · 2022 Edexcel 2B Q6(a)

Anything that cuts the oxygen supply to cells cuts aerobic respiration, so less energy is released.

This chain turns up inside questions on other topics — emphysema, a hole in the heart, low-oxygen water, anaemia. The marks are for each link:

The low-oxygen chain — three marks

1. Less oxygen diffuses into the blood / reaches the cells 1

2. So less aerobic respiration 1

3. So less energy released — for movement, keeping warm, building molecules — or more anaerobic respiration and lactic acid 1

"No respiration" is wrong. So is "less energy produced".

Temperature and the rate of respiration

2024 Edexcel 1B Q4(c) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1B Q8(c) · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q2(b)

Respiration is controlled by enzymes, so temperature affects it exactly as it affects any enzyme. This is module 1 again, and it came up in three real papers.

  1. Below the optimum: warmer means the molecules have more kinetic energy.
  2. They collide more often, forming more enzyme–substrate complexes.
  3. So the rate of respiration increases — more oxygen is used, or more carbon dioxide made, per minute.
  4. Above the optimum: the enzymes denature. The active site changes shape, the substrate no longer fits, and the rate falls.

✗ "The yeast works harder when it is warm."
✓ "More kinetic energy, so more frequent collisions between enzyme and substrate, so a faster rate of respiration."

Practical — heat from respiring seeds

Spec 2.39

Germinating seeds release heat as they respire, so a flask of them warms up.

  1. Soak peas so they start to germinate. Boil a second batch to kill them.
  2. Rinse both batches in disinfectant to kill microorganisms on their surface.
  3. Put each batch in a vacuum flask with a thermometer, plugged with cotton wool.
  4. Record the temperature at the start and every few hours for two or three days.
  5. The living seeds' flask gets warmer. The dead seeds' flask — the control — does not.

The vacuum flask insulates, so the heat is not lost. The disinfectant stops microbes on the seeds respiring and warming the flask, which would ruin the comparison. Cotton wool, not a bung, lets oxygen in so the seeds keep respiring aerobically.

Practical — detecting carbon dioxide

Spec 2.39 · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q8(b)

Limewater turns milky, and hydrogencarbonate indicator turns yellow, when carbon dioxide is added.

IndicatorNormal airMore carbon dioxide
LimewaterClearTurns milky / cloudy
Hydrogencarbonate indicatorOrange / redTurns yellow

Put small organisms — germinating seeds, woodlice, maggots — on gauze above the indicator in a sealed tube. Set up an identical tube with glass beads instead of organisms as the control. Only the tube with living things changes colour.

Or draw air through limewater, then past the organisms, then through a second limewater. The first stays clear, proving the carbon dioxide came from the organisms.

The respirometer

2024 Edexcel 1B Q4

A respirometer measures the rate of respiration by how fast the organisms use up oxygen.

  1. Small organisms sit on gauze in a sealed tube. Below them is soda lime, which absorbs carbon dioxide.
  2. The organisms take in oxygen. The carbon dioxide they give out is absorbed.
  3. So the volume of gas in the tube falls, and the pressure falls.
  4. A drop of coloured liquid in a capillary tube is pulled towards the organisms.
  5. The distance it moves in a set time measures the rate of oxygen use.

Why the soda lime is needed — two marks

1. Respiration releases carbon dioxide, which would replace the oxygen used 1

2. So the bubble would not move; with it absorbed, only the oxygen taken in is measured 1

Keep the tube in a water bath to control the temperature. A tube with glass beads of the same volume is the control.

Practical — yeast and anaerobic respiration

2023 Edexcel 1B Q8 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1B Q8 · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q2

The rate of anaerobic respiration in yeast is measured by the carbon dioxide it gives off.

  1. Boil the glucose solution, then cool it — boiling drives out dissolved oxygen.
  2. Add yeast, then pour a layer of oil on top to stop oxygen getting in, so the yeast must respire anaerobically.
  3. Put the tube in a water bath at a set temperature, checked with a thermometer.
  4. Lead the gas into a gas syringe or an upside-down measuring cylinder of water, or count the bubbles.
  5. Measure the volume in a set time with a timer. Repeat and take a mean.

Change one thing — temperature, or glucose concentration — and keep everything else the same: mass of yeast, volume of solution, time.

In one real paper an oxygen indicator started blue and turned pink. The mark scheme's reading: blue while oxygen was present and the yeast respired aerobically; pink once the oxygen was used up, so the yeast was now respiring anaerobically.

04Understanding it

U1

A student writes: "Respiration happens in the lungs and produces energy for the body." Give three corrections.

Show answer

1. Location. Respiration happens in every living cell (aerobic respiration in the mitochondria), not in the lungs. The lungs do gas exchange.

2. "Produces". Respiration releases energy that was already stored in glucose. Energy cannot be created.

3. What the energy does. It is used to make ATP, which supplies energy for cell processes — movement, growth, keeping warm, building molecules.

U2

A sprinter's muscles ache after a 200 m race, and they keep breathing heavily for several minutes. Explain both, naming the substance responsible.

Show answer

During the race the muscles could not get oxygen fast enough, so they respired anaerobically as well as aerobically. This produced lactic acid, which builds up and causes the ache and fatigue.

Afterwards the sprinter continues to breathe heavily to take in extra oxygen — the oxygen debt — which is used to break down the lactic acid. Once it is cleared, breathing returns to normal.

U3

Why does anaerobic respiration release so much less energy than aerobic respiration from the same amount of glucose?

Show answer

Because the glucose is broken down incompletely. Without oxygen the molecule is only partly broken apart, so most of the chemical energy is still locked in the products — lactic acid in animals, ethanol in plants and yeast.

Aerobic respiration breaks glucose down completely to carbon dioxide and water, releasing far more of the stored energy.

Evidence for this: ethanol burns. If anaerobic respiration had released all the energy, there would be none left in the product to burn.

U4

In the respiring seeds practical, why must the dead seeds be rinsed in disinfectant, and why is a vacuum flask used?

Show answer

Disinfectant: it kills microorganisms on the seeds. Those microbes would respire and release heat, so the "dead" flask would warm up too and the control would prove nothing.

Vacuum flask: it insulates, so heat released by respiration is not lost to the surroundings. Without it the temperature rise would be too small to measure.

U5

Yeast is used to make both bread and beer. Explain, using one equation, why the same organism does two apparently different jobs.

Show answer

Both rely on anaerobic respiration in yeast: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide.

In bread, the useful product is the carbon dioxide — the bubbles make the dough rise. The ethanol evaporates during baking.

In beer, the useful product is the ethanol, and the carbon dioxide provides the fizz.

One reaction, two different products being harvested.

U6

In a respirometer, the coloured drop moves towards the maggots. Why towards them, and why would it hardly move if the soda lime were left out?

Show answer

The maggots take in oxygen from the air in the tube. The carbon dioxide they give out is absorbed by the soda lime. So the amount of gas in the tube falls, the pressure falls, and the drop is pulled in towards the maggots.

Without soda lime, each molecule of oxygen used is replaced by a molecule of carbon dioxide — look at the equation: 6O₂ in, 6CO₂ out. The volume of gas barely changes, so the drop barely moves. Absorbing the carbon dioxide means the drop measures oxygen uptake only.

U7

Yeast gives off carbon dioxide faster at 30 °C than at 20 °C, but almost none at 60 °C. Explain both results.

Show answer

20 → 30 °C: respiration is controlled by enzymes. More heat gives the molecules more kinetic energy, so enzyme and substrate collide more often and more enzyme–substrate complexes form. Respiration speeds up, so more carbon dioxide is released per minute.

60 °C: this is above the optimum. The enzymes denature — the active site changes shape so the substrate no longer fits. Respiration almost stops, and the yeast is killed.

Same two halves as the enzyme graph in module 1. Check which side of the optimum the data is on before you write.

U8

If anaerobic respiration releases so little energy, why do muscles use it at all?

Show answer

Because during hard exercise the heart and lungs cannot deliver oxygen fast enough for aerobic respiration to meet the demand.

Anaerobic respiration does not need oxygen, so it tops up the energy supply in the short term. The price is lactic acid, which causes fatigue and has to be broken down later using extra oxygen — the oxygen debt.

It is a short-term emergency supply, not a replacement. That is why you cannot sprint for long.
