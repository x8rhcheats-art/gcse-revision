From the teacher's comments on the 2023 Year 10 paper, on why the right ventricle wall is thinner:

"'walls are thinner because the blood is deoxygenated' was a common conceptual error."

Wall thickness has nothing to do with oxygen. It is about pressure: the right ventricle only pumps to the lungs, the left pumps to the whole body. That question was lifted straight from a real Edexcel paper, so expect it again.

02How it actually works

Blood carries oxygen, food and waste around the body, pumped by the heart through arteries, capillaries and veins. Humans have a double circulatory system: blood passes through the heart twice for every complete circuit of the body. The blood also carries the white cells that defend the body against disease.

Why large organisms need a transport system

Spec 2.52

Large organisms have a small surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion alone is too slow to supply their cells.

A single-celled organism can get all its oxygen by diffusion through its surface — no cell is far from the outside. In a large animal most cells are deep inside, a long way from any surface. Diffusion over that distance would take far too long.

So a transport system — blood pumped through vessels — brings substances close to every cell. Diffusion only has to work over the last tiny gap, from a capillary to the cell.

The double circulation

Spec 2.69 · 2022 Y10 Q2

Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit: once to go to the lungs, once to go to the body.

  • The pulmonary circuit: right side of the heart → lungs → back to the heart.
  • The systemic circuit: left side of the heart → body → back to the heart.

The advantage: blood loses pressure passing through the lung capillaries. Returning it to the heart lets it be pumped again at high pressure, so it reaches the body quickly and delivers oxygen and glucose faster. The lungs themselves get blood at lower pressure, which protects their delicate capillaries.

The axolotl in the 2022 Year 10 paper has one ventricle, so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mix and less oxygen reaches its tissues. A hole between the ventricles in a human baby does the same.

The heart — chambers, valves and vessels

Spec 2.65 · 2022 Y10 Q3 · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q7(a) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1B Q2(a)

Blood enters the heart into an atrium and leaves from a ventricle; valves stop it flowing backwards. The heart wall is cardiac muscle.

  1. Blood from the veins fills the atria.
  2. The atria contract, pushing blood through valves into the ventricles.
  3. The ventricles contract, pushing blood out into the arteries. The valves between atria and ventricles close so blood cannot flow back.
  4. Valves at the base of the arteries close as the ventricles relax, so blood cannot fall back in.

The septum is the wall between the left and right sides. It keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood apart.

On a heart diagram the heart's right is on your left — you are looking at someone facing you. Diagrams in the real papers use letters, so find the aorta (the big arch) first and work from there.

Heart rate — exercise and adrenaline

Spec 2.66 · 2023 Y11 mock Q5(a)(i)

The heart beats faster when the muscles need more oxygen and glucose, or when adrenaline is released.

During exercise: muscles contract more and respire faster. They need more oxygen and glucose and make more carbon dioxide. The heart beats faster and more strongly, so blood is delivered faster and carbon dioxide removed faster.

Adrenaline: a hormone released by the adrenal glands when you are frightened or excited. It increases heart rate, getting more oxygen and glucose to the muscles, ready for "fight or flight".

The volume pumped per beat is the stroke volume. If a heart condition reduces it, less blood — so less oxygen — reaches the muscles per beat, and there is less respiration.

Coronary heart disease

Spec 2.67 · 2023 Jan Edexcel 1B Q2(b) · 2022 Y10 Q3(c)

The coronary arteries supply the heart muscle itself; if fatty deposits narrow them, the muscle is starved of oxygen.

  1. Fatty deposits build up in the wall of a coronary artery.
  2. The lumen narrows, so less blood reaches the heart muscle.
  3. The muscle gets less oxygen and glucose, so less aerobic respiration.
  4. If a clot blocks the artery completely, part of the muscle dies — a heart attack.

The coronary artery carries oxygenated blood to the muscle; the coronary vein carries deoxygenated blood away. A stent is a mesh tube that keeps the coronary artery open, so blood and oxygen can reach the heart muscle.

Look for sample size, how long the study ran, where it was done, and whether the pattern holds for every age group.

  • Arteries have thick, elastic walls to withstand the high pressure and stretch and recoil with each beat.
  • Veins have valves to stop backflow, because the pressure is low. The wide lumen makes flow easier.
  • Capillaries are one cell thick for a short diffusion distance — this is where substances are exchanged with the cells.

Red blood cells

Red blood cells are built to carry as much oxygen as possible and pass it on quickly.

No nucleus also means no chromosomes — which is why the 2023 January paper said white blood cells, not red ones, are used to look at chromosomes. And people living at high altitude, where there is less oxygen in the air, make more red blood cells and haemoglobin so they can still carry enough.

Too little haemoglobin — as in anaemia or beta thalassaemia — means less oxygen carried to the muscles, so less respiration, less energy released, and tiredness.

White blood cells — phagocytes and lymphocytes

Spec 2.62 · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q5(b) · 2024 Y10 Q5(b) · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q5(c)

Phagocytes engulf and digest pathogens; lymphocytes release antibodies specific to one pathogen.

A pathogen is a microorganism that causes disease. On its surface are antigens — molecules the body recognises as foreign.

Phagocytes — phagocytosis:

  1. The phagocyte moves towards the pathogen.
  2. It engulfs it, surrounding it with its cytoplasm.
  3. The pathogen is held inside a vesicle.
  4. Enzymes are released into the vesicle and digest the pathogen.

Lymphocytes make antibodies. Each antibody has a shape complementary to one antigen, so it is specific to one pathogen. Antibodies bind to the antigens and clump the pathogens together, making them easier for phagocytes to destroy.

Red versus white — the three differences the 2024 paper wanted: white cells have a nucleus; red cells are biconcave and white are not; red cells are smaller; red cells contain haemoglobin.

An allergy such as hay fever is the same system reacting to pollen: white blood cells treat its antigens as foreign and produce antibodies.

Vaccination and memory cells

Spec 2.63B · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2B Q6(b)(i)

A vaccine makes the body produce memory cells, so antibodies are made sooner, faster and in greater quantity if the real pathogen arrives.

  1. The vaccine contains a weakened or inactivated form of the pathogen, carrying its antigens.
  2. Lymphocytes recognise the antigens and make specific antibodies.
  3. Memory cells are made, and stay in the blood.
  4. If the real pathogen infects you, the memory cells make antibodies sooner, faster and in greater quantity.
  5. The pathogen is destroyed before you become ill.

The rabies mark scheme ignored "phagocytes" and "a small amount of virus". The marks were for antigens, lymphocytes and memory cells.

Platelets and clotting

Spec 2.64B · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2B Q1(e)

Platelets start the clotting process, which stops blood loss and keeps microorganisms out.

  1. At a wound, platelets trigger a series of reactions.
  2. Soluble fibrinogen is turned into insoluble fibrin.
  3. Fibrin forms a mesh that traps red blood cells, making a clot.
  4. The clot dries into a scab.

Two reasons it matters, and the spec names both: it prevents blood loss, and it stops microorganisms entering. Artificial blood with no platelets could not clot — the point one real paper tested.

04Understanding it

U1

Explain the advantage of a double circulatory system over a single one, as found in fish.

Show answer

In a single circulation, blood goes through the gill capillaries and then straight on to the body. Capillaries are narrow, so blood leaves them at low pressure and travels slowly to the tissues.

In a double circulation the blood returns to the heart after the lungs and is pumped again at high pressure. It reaches the body much faster, so oxygen and glucose are delivered faster and a higher rate of respiration is possible.

That matters for mammals: they keep warm by respiring, so they need a lot of energy released.

U2

A baby is born with a hole between the left and right ventricles. Explain why they tire easily.

Show answer

The hole lets oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mix in the ventricles.

Blood leaving the left ventricle for the body therefore carries less oxygen than normal. Less oxygen reaches the respiring tissues, so less aerobic respiration occurs and less energy is released.

More anaerobic respiration takes place instead, producing lactic acid, so the baby tires quickly. The axolotl, with its single ventricle, has the same problem.

U3

Explain why veins need valves but arteries do not.

Show answer

Blood in arteries is at high pressure, straight from the heart. That pressure keeps it moving forwards.

By the time blood reaches the veins it has passed through the capillaries, and the pressure is low. It could flow backwards, especially going up from the legs against gravity. The valves close if blood starts to move the wrong way.

Veins also rely on nearby muscles contracting to squeeze blood along.

U4

Why does someone who has had measles usually show no symptoms if they catch it a second time?

Show answer

After the first infection, memory cells specific to the measles antigen stay in the blood.

On the second infection these memory cells recognise the antigen at once and the specific antibodies are made sooner, faster and in far greater quantity.

The pathogen is destroyed before it can multiply enough to cause symptoms. That is immunity. A vaccine produces the same memory cells on purpose, using a weakened or inactivated pathogen.

U5

A red blood cell has no nucleus and a biconcave shape. Explain how each suits its job.

Show answer

No nucleus: more room for haemoglobin, so each cell carries more oxygen.

Biconcave disc: a large surface area to volume ratio, so oxygen diffuses in and out quickly. It also makes the cell flexible enough to squeeze through capillaries.

The cost: with no nucleus the cell cannot divide or repair itself, so red blood cells last only about four months. The body keeps making new ones.

U6

Explain how a blood clot forms, and give two reasons clotting is important.

Show answer

Platelets at the wound trigger reactions that turn soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin. The fibrin forms a mesh across the wound, trapping red blood cells to form a clot, which dries into a scab.

Why it matters: it prevents blood loss, and it seals the wound so microorganisms cannot enter.

U7

Your heart pounds when you get a fright, even though you are standing still. Explain why this is useful.

Show answer

A fright makes the adrenal glands release adrenaline into the blood. Adrenaline increases the heart rate.

Blood is pumped faster, so more oxygen and glucose reach the muscles. The muscles can respire faster and release more energy the moment you need to run or fight.

It prepares the body before the exercise starts. During exercise itself, the heart rate rises because the muscles are already respiring faster.

U8

People who live high in the mountains have more red blood cells than people at sea level. Explain why.

Show answer

At high altitude the air has less oxygen in each breath. Less oxygen diffuses into the blood.

Making more red blood cells, with more haemoglobin, lets the blood pick up and carry enough oxygen anyway. So the tissues still get enough for aerobic respiration.

The real 2024 paper gave data like this. Say what the data shows first — the trend with altitude, and any difference between men and women — before explaining it.
