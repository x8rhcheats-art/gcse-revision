02How it actually works

Every technique here increases yield by removing something that was limiting growth — or, with microorganisms, by giving them ideal conditions to grow. Name the limiting factor or the condition, follow it through, and the mark follows.

Glasshouses and polytunnels

Spec 5.5

A glasshouse raises yield by controlling the conditions that limit photosynthesis.

What is controlledWhy yield rises
TemperatureWarmer conditions increase the rate of photosynthesis (and of the enzymes controlling it), so more glucose is made — until the optimum
Carbon dioxideBurning paraffin heaters or adding CO₂ removes it as a limiting factor
LightArtificial lighting extends the growing day, and light is no longer limiting
Pests and diseaseEnclosure keeps pests out, so less of the crop is damaged or eaten
WaterIrrigation gives a reliable supply, needed as a raw material for photosynthesis

A polytunnel — polythene over hoops — does the same job more cheaply, trapping heat and keeping off wind and some pests. Crops can be grown out of season and earlier in the year.

Carbon dioxide and temperature

Spec 5.6

More carbon dioxide and a higher temperature both increase yield — but only up to a point.

  1. More CO₂ or a higher temperature removes a limiting factor.
  2. So the rate of photosynthesis increases.
  3. More glucose is made.
  4. It is converted to starch, cellulose and proteins for growth.
  5. The plants grow larger and faster, so the yield is higher.

The limits: once CO₂ is high enough, another factor such as light or temperature limits the rate, and adding more CO₂ is wasted money. Raising temperature only helps up to the optimum; beyond it the enzymes controlling photosynthesis denature and the rate falls. Heating also costs money, so growers stop where the extra crop no longer pays for the fuel.

A paraffin heater removes two limiting factors at once: it releases heat and carbon dioxide as it burns.

Designing a glasshouse investigation

2024 Edexcel 1B Q10

Change one factor, keep everything else the same, measure the crop, and repeat. The real mark scheme follows a pattern you can learn:

StepWhat to write, for "best CO₂ concentration"
C — changeUse at least three different CO₂ concentrations, one per glasshouse
O — organismSame species and variety of crop plant, same age
R — repeatRepeat each concentration
M — measureMeasure the mass of crop (fruit or seeds) after a stated time
S — sameKeep temperature and light the same; same soil, water and fertiliser

Six marks, six points. Name the variables — "keep everything else the same" scores nothing.

Fertilisers

Spec 5.7

Fertilisers supply the mineral ions that harvested crops remove from the soil.

IonNeeded for
NitrateMaking amino acids, and so proteins for growth
MagnesiumMaking chlorophyll
PhosphateDNA and cell membranes
PotassiumEnzymes for photosynthesis and respiration

The chain to state: fertiliser supplies nitrate → more amino acids → more protein → more growth → higher yield. Say nitrate ions, not "nitrogen", and do not say fertiliser "feeds" the plant — plants make their own food.

Why crops need pest control

Spec 5.8 · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1B Q10(b)

Pests eat or damage crops, so less of the crop can be harvested.

The damage can be indirect. Aphids push their mouthparts into the phloem and take sucrose and amino acids. The plant then has:

  • less sugar for respiration, so less energy for growth and for active transport of mineral ions
  • less sugar to store as starch in grain, fruit or tubers
  • fewer amino acids to make proteins

Pests can also spread disease from plant to plant, and eaten leaves mean less photosynthesis.

Pesticides versus biological control

Spec 5.8 · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1B Q10(a) · 2024 Y11 mock Q2

Pesticides are fast but unselective; biological control is specific and self-sustaining but slow.

FeaturePesticidesBiological control
What it isChemicals that kill the pestIntroducing a natural predator of the pest
SpeedFast, kills most of the pest quicklySlow to take effect
Effect on other speciesMay kill harmless or useful species too, such as beesSpecific — targets the pest
PersistenceCan accumulate along food chains, poisoning predatorsDoes not accumulate
RepetitionMust be reappliedPredator population is self-sustaining
ResistancePests can become resistantResistance does not develop
RiskPollutes soil and waterThe predator may become a pest itself, or eat other species

Biological control reduces the pest population rather than eliminating it — some pests must remain or the predator would have no food. That point is worth a mark on its own and is often missed.

Pesticides in food chains, and resistance

Spec 5.8 · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q2(b) · 2024 Y11 mock Q2(d)

A persistent pesticide builds up along a food chain, and pests that survive it pass on their resistance.

Build-up: the pesticide is not broken down or excreted. Each animal eats many from the level below, so the concentration increases at each trophic level. Top predators get the highest dose. Even if they survive, killing one population changes the rest of the web — fewer prey for the animals above, less predation on the ones below.

Resistance is natural selection:

  1. A mutation makes a few pests resistant.
  2. The pesticide kills the others; the resistant ones survive.
  3. They reproduce and pass on the allele for resistance.
  4. Over generations, most of the population is resistant, and the pesticide stops working.

Reading a pest-control graph

2024 Y11 mock Q2 · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1B Q10(c)

Compare each treatment with the untreated control, then describe what happens over time.

In the fire-ant question, three plots were followed for a year: untreated, pesticide, and phorid flies (a parasite of the ants). The scheme's marks:

  • The untreated plot is the control — ant numbers increase.
  • Both treatments cut numbers at first.
  • With pesticide, numbers recover after about six months; with phorid flies they level off low.
  • So biological control is long-term; the pesticide wears off, or the ants became resistant by natural selection.

When judging a study, also ask how valid it is: one larva tested, no repeats, or a lab instead of a field all weaken the conclusion.

Yoghurt

Spec 5.3 · 2024 Edexcel 1B Q3 · 2023 Y11 mock Q3 · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1B Q5

Lactobacillus respires anaerobically, turning lactose in milk into lactic acid, which thickens and preserves it.

  1. Equipment is sterilised.
  2. Milk is pasteurised — heated to about 85–95 °C — to kill unwanted microorganisms.
  3. It is cooled to about 40–45 °C, the optimum for the bacteria.
  4. It is inoculated with Lactobacillus bacteria and kept warm for several hours.
  5. The bacteria respire anaerobically, converting the sugar lactose into lactic acid.
  6. The acid lowers the pH, so the milk proteins coagulate — the milk thickens into yoghurt.

The acid also preserves the yoghurt by stopping other microorganisms growing. Two real papers asked what happens with less oxygen: there is more anaerobic respiration, so acid is made and the acidity rises faster.

The industrial fermenter — why each condition

A fermenter grows microorganisms on a large scale, and every feature keeps them growing as fast as possible.

Aseptic precautionsSterilising the vessel prevents contamination by unwanted microbes that would compete for nutrients or spoil the product

Optimum pHEnzymes work fastest and do not denature

The cooling jacket is the one to understand rather than memorise: the microorganisms are respiring, respiration releases heat, and that heat would build up and denature their own enzymes. It is there to remove heat, not to add it.

Fish farming

2022 Edexcel Jan 2B Q4

Fish farms produce protein-rich food by controlling what the fish eat, where they live and how they breed.

  • Water quality — waste food and faeces are removed, so decomposers do not use up the oxygen.
  • Predation — nets keep out predators, and different sizes or species are kept apart so they do not eat each other.
  • Disease — fish are kept at a sensible density and treated, as disease spreads fast in crowded tanks.
  • Feeding — the right food, in the right amount, at the right times, so little is wasted.
  • Selective breeding — choose fish that grow quickly, breed them, choose the fastest-growing offspring, and repeat over many generations, so the alleles for fast growth are passed on.

The real scheme wanted "grow quickly", not just "large" or "with the desired characteristics".

04Understanding it

U1

A grower burns a paraffin heater in a glasshouse. Give two ways this increases the tomato yield.

Show answer

Heat: it raises the temperature, increasing the rate of photosynthesis and of the enzymes controlling it, up to the optimum.

Carbon dioxide: burning paraffin releases CO₂, which is a raw material for photosynthesis and is often the limiting factor in a sealed glasshouse.

Both increase the rate of photosynthesis, so more glucose is made, more is converted to starch and cellulose for growth, and the yield rises.

The elegance of the paraffin heater is that one device removes two limiting factors at once.

U2

A farmer uses a pesticide and finds that bird populations nearby fall sharply, even though birds are not pests. Explain.

Show answer

The pesticide is persistent — it does not break down quickly, so it stays in the bodies of the organisms that absorb it.

It passes along the food chain as each organism eats many of the level below, so the concentration accumulates and increases at each trophic level.

Birds are near the top of the chain, so they receive the highest dose, which poisons them. Their food supply may also have been reduced if the pesticide killed the insects they eat.

U3

Why does biological control never completely remove a pest, and why is that acceptable to a farmer?

Show answer

The predator depends on the pest as its food source. If it ate every pest it would starve and its own population would collapse, allowing the pest to return.

Instead the two populations settle into a predator–prey cycle, with the pest held at a low level.

That is acceptable because the farmer only needs the pest population low enough that crop damage is small. The advantage over pesticides is that the control is self-sustaining — no repeated application, no resistance, and no harm to other species.

U4

Explain why an industrial fermenter needs a cooling jacket rather than a heater.

Show answer

The microorganisms inside are respiring, and respiration releases heat to the surroundings. In a large vessel with billions of organisms, that heat builds up quickly.

If the temperature rose above the optimum, the microorganisms' enzymes would denature — their active sites would change shape — and growth would stop.

The cooling jacket removes the excess heat to hold the temperature at the optimum. The vessel may be warmed at the start, but once the culture is growing the problem is always too much heat, not too little.

U5

Describe how milk is turned into yoghurt, and explain why the yoghurt keeps longer than the milk did.

Show answer

The equipment is sterilised and the milk is pasteurised to kill unwanted microorganisms, then cooled. Lactobacillus bacteria are added.

They respire anaerobically, converting the lactose in the milk into lactic acid. The acid lowers the pH, causing the milk proteins to coagulate and thicken the mixture into yoghurt.

Why it keeps: the low pH prevents the growth of other microorganisms, including the bacteria that would spoil the milk. The acid acts as a preservative.

U6

Scientists make yoghurt with normal oxygen and with reduced oxygen. The acidity rises faster with reduced oxygen. Why?

Show answer

Lactic acid is only made by anaerobic respiration. With plenty of oxygen, the bacteria do more aerobic respiration, which makes carbon dioxide and water instead — no acid.

With less oxygen, more anaerobic respiration happens, so lactose is turned into lactic acid faster, and the acidity rises sooner.

U7

A pesticide and a parasitic fly both cut fire-ant numbers at first. Six months later, the pesticide plot has as many ants as before, but the fly plot does not. Explain.

Show answer

Pesticide: it is applied once and then breaks down or washes away, so its effect wears off. Any ants that survived — possibly because they carry an allele for resistance — reproduce and the population recovers.

Flies: they are living. They reproduce as long as there are ants to use, so the control carries on by itself. The ant population is held low in a steady balance.

That is the "long-term" advantage of biological control that the mark scheme wanted.

U8

A grower doubles the carbon dioxide in a glasshouse and the yield rises. They double it again and the yield barely changes. Why?

Show answer

At first, CO₂ was the limiting factor. More of it meant more photosynthesis, more glucose and more growth.

After the first increase, CO₂ is no longer limiting. Another factor — light intensity or temperature — now limits the rate, so extra CO₂ has no effect. It also costs money, so it is wasted.

To get more yield now, the grower must raise the factor that is limiting, such as adding lighting.

D1

Describe how a glasshouse can be used to increase crop yield. (3)

Show answer

The temperature can be kept higher / at the optimum 1; carbon dioxide concentration or light can be increased so they are no longer limiting 1; so the rate of photosynthesis increases, making more glucose for growth 1.

D2

Explain why adding nitrate fertiliser increases the yield of a wheat crop. (3)

Show answer

Nitrate ions are needed to make amino acids 1; amino acids are used to make proteins 1; proteins are needed for growth, so the plants grow larger and the yield increases 1.

D3

Give two advantages and one disadvantage of biological control compared with pesticides. (3)

Show answer

One advantage from: it is specific and does not harm other species / it does not need reapplying / pests do not become resistant / no chemical pollution 1; a second advantage from that list 1; disadvantage: it is slower to take effect, or the predator may become a pest itself 1.

D4

Name the bacterium used to make yoghurt and describe the reaction it carries out. (3)

Lactobacillus 1; it converts lactose into lactic acid by anaerobic respiration 1; the acid lowers the pH, causing the milk proteins to coagulate and thicken 1.

To prevent contamination by unwanted microorganisms 1; which would compete with the culture for nutrients, reduce the yield or spoil the product 1.

To keep the microorganisms in contact with the nutrients and oxygen 1; and to distribute the heat evenly so no part overheats 1.

For aerobic respiration by the microorganisms 1; which releases more energy than anaerobic respiration, so they grow and reproduce faster 1.
