02How it actually works

Ecology is about how living things depend on each other and on their surroundings. Two ideas carry most of the marks: how you measure a population fairly, and why energy runs out as it passes along a food chain.

The four terms, in order of size

Spec 4.1 · 2024 Y10 Q9(a)

A population is one species; a community is every species; an ecosystem adds the non-living surroundings.

LevelWhat it covers
HabitatThe place where an organism lives
PopulationAll the organisms of one species in a habitat
CommunityAll the populations of different species in a habitat
EcosystemThe community plus the non-living environment it interacts with

The ecosystem definition needs the non-living part — water, soil, temperature, light. "All the living things in an area" defines a community, not an ecosystem, and that is the most common mix-up.

Biodiversity

Spec 4.3B · 2024 Y10 Q7(b)

Biodiversity is the number of different species in an area — and, more fully, how evenly the organisms are spread among them.

The 2024 Year 10 question gave counts of three plant species in two fields. The marks went to:

  • Field X has all three species; field Y does not.
  • Field X has an even spread; field Y is dominated by one species.
  • So field X has the greater biodiversity — but three counts is a small sample.

Two fields can have the same number of species, yet one is more diverse because no single species dominates it.

Quadrats — estimating a population

Spec 4.2 · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q4(a)(ii) · 2024 Y11 mock Q1(b)(ii)

You count organisms in a few random squares, take a mean, and scale up to the whole area.

  1. Mark out the area with two tape measures at right angles, like the axes of a graph.
  2. Use a random number generator to pick coordinates, and place a quadrat there.
  3. Count the organisms of the species inside the quadrat.
  4. Repeat for many quadrats — ten or more — and calculate the mean per quadrat.
  5. Scale up to the total area.
Estimating population size from quadratspopulation = mean number per quadrat × (total area ÷ area of one quadrat)mean of several quadrats first · place them randomly · the answer is an estimate

To compare two areas, do exactly the same in each: same quadrat size, same number of quadrats, same time of year. Then compare the means.

✗ "Put the quadrat where the plants are." — biased.
✗ "Use a quadrat and repeat." — the school rejected "repeat" without "count the producers".
✓ "Place quadrats at random coordinates, count the plants in each, take a mean and scale up to the area."

Transects — how distribution changes

Spec 4.4B

A transect is a line across a habitat, with quadrats placed at regular intervals along it.

Use one when conditions change across an area — from a hedge into a field, or up a beach. At each point along the line, record which species are present and how many, and measure an abiotic factor such as light intensity or soil moisture. You can then link the change in distribution to the change in conditions.

Random quadrats are for estimating how many. A transect is for showing where, and why.

Abiotic and biotic factors

Spec 4.5

Abiotic factors are the non-living conditions; biotic factors are the effects of other living things. Both change the size and distribution of a population.

Abiotic — non-livingBiotic — living
Light intensityAvailability of food
TemperaturePredators
Water availabilityCompetition for food, space or light
Soil pH and mineral ionsPathogens — disease
Oxygen or carbon dioxide levelsPollinators

Always link the factor to the organism. "Less light under the trees, so less photosynthesis, so fewer grass plants" scores; "light affects plants" does not.

Trophic levels and food chains

Spec 4.6 · 2024 Edexcel 1B Q1(a) · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1B Q7(a)

A food chain starts with a producer, and each arrow shows the direction of energy transfer. The arrows point from the eaten to the eater. Drawing them backwards is a standard lost mark.

Trophic levelRoleExample
1 — ProducerMakes its own food by photosynthesisOak tree, grass
2 — Primary consumerEats the producer — a herbivoreCaterpillar, zebra
3 — Secondary consumerEats the primary consumerBlue tit, cheetah
4 — Tertiary consumerEats the secondary consumerSparrowhawk
DecomposersBreak down dead material and waste from every levelBacteria and fungi

A trophic level is the position an organism feeds at. In a food web, one animal can sit at two levels — a blackbird eating earthworms is a secondary consumer, and eating centipedes that eat woodlice makes it a tertiary consumer too.

Food webs and interdependence

Spec 4.7 · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q2(b) · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1B Q7(a)(iii)

Change one population in a food web and the populations that eat it, and that it eats, change too.

Work outwards along each arrow, one step at a time:

  1. What did the removed organism eat? That population increases — fewer are eaten.
  2. What ate it? That population decreases — less food. Unless it has another food source in the web.
  3. Follow the knock-on effect one more step.

An animal with many food sources is least affected when one prey species falls — it just eats more of the others. Real papers test this as a multiple-choice question on a food web.

Decomposers — how they get energy

Spec 4.6 · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q2(a)(ii) · 2022 Edexcel Jan 2B Q2(a)

Decomposers release enzymes onto dead material, absorb the small molecules, and respire them.

  1. Bacteria and fungi feed on dead organisms and waste — they are saprotrophic.
  2. They secrete enzymes onto the material — digestion happens outside their cells.
  3. They absorb the soluble products.
  4. They use them in respiration to release energy, and to grow.

Decomposers recycle the elements locked in dead bodies back into the soil — see module 11.

Pyramids of number, biomass and energy

Spec 4.7 · 2022 Y10 Q8(a) · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q4(a)(i)

A pyramid shows the size of each trophic level, producers at the bottom.

PyramidWhat each bar showsAlways a true pyramid?
NumbersHow many organismsNo — one oak tree feeds thousands of caterpillars
BiomassTotal dry mass of organismsAlmost always
EnergyEnergy passed on per m² per yearAlways — energy is lost at every level

When you draw one, put the producer at the bottom, make each bar the right relative width, and label every level. The school scheme gave a mark just for the labels, and some students forgot them.

Why only about 10% passes on

Spec 4.9 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1B Q7(b)(i) · 2024 Y11 mock Q1(b)(i)

At each transfer between trophic levels most of the energy is lost, so only about 10% is available to the next level. The named routes:

  • Respiration — energy released and lost as heat, and used in movement
  • Not all of the organism is eaten — roots, bones, fur
  • Not all is digested — lost in faeces (egestion)
  • Excretion — energy lost in urea and urine
  • Some organisms die and are not eaten — decomposers get that energy

This is why food chains are short, rarely more than four or five levels, and why a shorter chain delivers more energy to the top. Mammals and birds lose more than snails or insects, because they respire more to keep warm.

Producers are worse still. They capture only a few per cent of the light that falls on them: some is reflected, some misses the chlorophyll, and some is the wrong wavelength.

Energy transfer calculations

2023 Y10 Q3(b) · 2024 Y10 Q4(b) · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1B Q7(b)

Efficiency is the energy (or biomass) passed on, divided by what was there, times 100.

Percentage transfer between trophic levelsefficiency (%) = (energy or biomass at the higher level ÷ energy or biomass at the lower level) × 1002.40 ÷ 10.60 × 100 = 22.6% · 1.4 ÷ 87 × 100 = 1.6%

A variation: assimilation efficiency — how much of what was eaten was actually absorbed. A snail eats 1.2 g and egests 0.3 g, so it absorbs 0.9 g: 0.9 ÷ 1.2 × 100 = 75%. Herbivores score low on this because plant material is hard to digest, so much is egested.

Transfer of substances, and predator–prey cycles

Spec 4.8

Substances pass up a food chain along with energy — carbon compounds, and also poisons.

When an animal eats, it takes in the proteins, carbohydrates and fats of its food, and builds them into its own body. Anything that is not broken down or excreted — such as some pesticides — builds up. Each animal eats many from the level below, so the concentration increases at each trophic level, and top predators get the highest dose.

Predator and prey numbers rise and fall in cycles. More prey means more food, so the predator population rises. More predators eat more prey, so the prey population falls. With less food the predators fall, and the prey recover. The predator peak always comes after the prey peak.

04Understanding it

U1

A student counts daisies in ten 0.25 m² quadrats in a 200 m² field, finding a mean of 6 per quadrat. Estimate the population and state one assumption.

Show answer

Number of quadrat-sized areas in the field = 200 ÷ 0.25 = 800.

Estimated population = 6 × 800 = 4800 daisies.

Assumption: that the quadrats were placed randomly, so the sample is representative of the whole field. If the daisies were clustered in one damp corner and the quadrats missed it, the estimate could be badly wrong.

U2

Explain why a food chain rarely has more than four or five trophic levels.

Show answer

Only about 10% of the energy at each trophic level is transferred to the next. The remaining 90% is lost through respiration as heat, in egestion as faeces, in excretion, and in parts not eaten.

After four or five levels so little energy remains that it could not support a population of another organism — there would not be enough food energy to sustain their numbers.

U3

In the chain grass → rabbit → fox, all the foxes are removed. Predict what happens to the rabbits and the grass, and explain.

Show answer

Rabbits increase. Their predator has gone, so fewer are eaten and more survive to reproduce.

Grass decreases. The larger rabbit population eats far more grass.

Then rabbits fall again. With the grass depleted there is not enough food, so rabbits starve and their numbers drop — the population is now limited by food rather than by predation.

Working the chain through in both directions, and then following the knock-on effect, is what separates a 3-mark answer from a 1-mark one.

U4

A pyramid of numbers for oak tree → caterpillar → bird is inverted at the base. Explain why, and why a pyramid of biomass would not be.

Show answer

A pyramid of numbers counts individuals regardless of size. A single oak tree can feed thousands of caterpillars, so the producer bar is narrower than the level above it.

A pyramid of biomass measures the total dry mass at each level. One oak tree has an enormous mass — far more than all the caterpillars feeding on it — so the pyramid is the normal shape.

Biomass pyramids are almost always true pyramids because each level can only build its mass from the level below, and most of that is lost on the way.

U5

On a predator–prey graph, the predator peak always comes slightly after the prey peak. Explain why.

Show answer

The predators depend on the prey for food. When prey numbers rise there is more food available, so more predators survive and reproduce — but that takes time, because the young must be born and grow.

By the time the predator population has risen, the increased predation has already begun to reduce the prey numbers, which is why the prey peak has passed.

The lag is the cause-and-effect made visible: the predator population is responding to the prey population, so it must follow it.

U6

In a forest web, a fox gets its food through acorns → mouse → fox and through grass → grasshopper → shrew → fox. Why does the fox get more of the energy from the oak chain?

Show answer

The oak chain is shorter: there is only one trophic level between the producer and the fox, instead of two.

Energy is lost at every transfer between trophic levels — as heat from respiration, in faeces, in excretion, and in parts not eaten. Fewer transfers means fewer losses, so a bigger share of the producer's energy reaches the fox.

The school noted that most students named the losses but missed the first mark — saying the chain is shorter.

U7

A mouse and a snail eat the same mass of plant material. The snail turns more of it into new body mass. Why?

Show answer

A mouse is a mammal. It keeps its body temperature constant, so it respires much more to keep warm, and it moves around a lot. That energy is released as heat and lost.

A snail's body temperature just follows its surroundings, and it moves slowly. It uses less of its food energy for respiration, so more is left to build new tissue.

U8

Only about 1–2% of the light falling on a field of grass ends up in new grass tissue. Where does the rest go?

Show answer

Much of the light never gets used: some is reflected from the leaves, some passes through or misses the chlorophyll, and some is a wavelength chlorophyll does not absorb.

Of the energy that is captured in glucose, the grass uses a lot in its own respiration, which releases it as heat. Only what is left is built into new tissue — the biomass a herbivore can eat.

05Exam drill

D1

Define the terms population and community. (2)

Show answer

Population: all the organisms of one species in a habitat 1; community: all the populations of different species in a habitat 1.

D2

Explain why quadrats must be placed randomly. (2)

Show answer

To avoid bias in choosing where to sample 1; so the sample is representative of the whole area and the estimate is valid 1.

D3

A field is 500 m². Twelve quadrats of 0.5 m² give a mean of 8 plants each. Estimate the population. (2)

Show answer

500 ÷ 0.5 = 1000 quadrat areas in the field 1; 8 × 1000 = 8000 plants 1.

D4

Give three ways energy is lost between one trophic level and the next. (3)

Show answer

Respiration, released as heat to the surroundings 1; egestion as faeces / undigested material 1; excretion in urine, or parts of the organism not being eaten 1.

D5

State what the arrows in a food chain represent. (1)

Show answer

The direction of energy transfer, from the organism eaten to the organism eating it 1.

D6

Explain the difference between an ecosystem and a community. (2)

Show answer

A community is all the living organisms / populations in a habitat 1; an ecosystem is the community together with the non-living parts of the environment 1.

D7

In the chain algae → shrimp → fish → seal, the shrimp are removed by disease. Describe the effect on the fish, the algae and the seals. (3)

Show answer

Fish decrease, because they have lost their food source 1; algae increase, because fewer shrimp are eating them 1; seals also decrease, because there are fewer fish to eat 1.

D8

State what is meant by biodiversity, and suggest why a farmer removing hedgerows reduces it. (2)

Show answer

Biodiversity is the number of different species in an area 1; removing hedgerows destroys habitats, so species lose food and shelter and their populations fall or disappear 1.

D9

Describe how a scientist could compare the population size of star grass in two different areas. (4)

Show answer

Use a quadrat 1; placed at random, using a random number generator for coordinates 1; count the star grass plants in each quadrat, repeat and take a mean 1; scale up to the area — using the same number and size of quadrats in both areas 1

D10

The biomass of producers is 10.60 kg per m². The biomass of primary consumers is 2.40 kg per m². Calculate the percentage of biomass transferred. (2)

Show answer

2.40 ÷ 10.60 × 100 1; = 22.6% 1

D11

Explain why only a small proportion of the energy in one trophic level is passed to the next. (4)

Show answer

Not all organisms / parts are eaten — bones, roots 1; some food is not digested and is egested in faeces 1; energy is released in respiration and lost as heat, or used in movement 1; some energy is lost in excretion, as urea 1

D12

Describe how bacteria and fungi obtain energy from dead organic matter. (3)

Show answer

They secrete enzymes onto the dead material 1; which digest / break it down into small soluble molecules that they absorb 1; the products are used in respiration to release energy 1

D13

Name two abiotic factors and two biotic factors that could affect the population of a plant species in a field. (4)

Show answer

Abiotic — one of light intensity, temperature, water availability, soil pH, mineral ions 1; a second abiotic factor 1; biotic — one of grazing animals, competition from other plants, disease, pollinators 1; a second biotic factor 1

D15

A snail eats 1.2 g of lettuce and produces 0.3 g of faeces. Calculate the percentage of the food it absorbs, and explain why this figure is lower for herbivores than for carnivores. (4)
