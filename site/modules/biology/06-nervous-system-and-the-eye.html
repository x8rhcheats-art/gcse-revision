02How it actually works

The nervous system detects stimuli and produces responses, using electrical impulses that travel fast. It has two parts: the central nervous system — the brain and spinal cord — and the nerves that connect it to the rest of the body. The eye is the receptor this module looks at in detail.

Stimulus, receptor, effector

Spec 2.80, 2.82

Every coordinated response needs a stimulus, a receptor to detect it, and an effector to respond.

PartWhat it isExample
StimulusA change in the environmentA hot object touching the hand
ReceptorCells that detect the stimulusTemperature or pain receptors in the skin
EffectorA muscle or gland that carries out the responseThe biceps muscle
ResponseWhat the effector doesThe hand pulls away

Organisms respond to changes so they can find food, avoid danger and keep conditions inside the body steady. Keeping the inside of the body constant — its water content and temperature — is called homeostasis, and it is module 8.

Nervous and hormonal communication

Spec 2.83, 2.86

Nerves carry fast, short-lived electrical impulses to one place; hormones travel slowly in the blood and last longer.

FeatureNervousHormonal
SignalElectrical impulsesChemicals — hormones
Carried byNeuronesThe blood
SpeedVery fastSlower
How long the effect lastsShortLonger
Where it actsA precise place — one muscle or glandTarget organs, often spread around the body

The central nervous system and nerves

Spec 2.87, 2.88

The brain and spinal cord form the CNS; nerves link it to the sense organs and the effectors.

  1. A receptor in a sense organ is stimulated.
  2. It sends electrical impulses along nerves into the CNS.
  3. The CNS coordinates the response.
  4. Impulses travel out of the CNS along nerves to an effector.

A nerve is a bundle of many neurones. A neurone is a single nerve cell. Use the neurone names in reflex answers.

The three neurones

Spec 2.88 · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q4(a)

Sensory neurones carry impulses into the CNS, relay neurones pass them on inside it, and motor neurones carry them out to effectors.

NeuroneCarries impulsesFeature
SensoryReceptor → CNSCell body part-way along
RelayWithin the CNSShort; links sensory to motor
MotorCNS → effectorLong axon; cell body inside the CNS

The long fibres are wrapped in a fatty myelin sheath, which insulates them and speeds up the impulse.

The reflex arc, in order

Spec 2.90 · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q4

A reflex is a rapid, automatic response that does not involve conscious thought. The brain is not part of the pathway, which is why it is fast.

The reflex arc — learn this orderstimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (spinal cord) → motor neurone → effector → responseeffector = muscle or gland · impulses cross a synapse between each neurone

The spec example is pulling a finger away from a hot object:

The two words most often left out are relay and effector. Each stage in a long answer carries its own mark.

Why reflexes are useful

2023 Y11 mock Q2(c) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1B Q7(c) · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q4(c)(i)

A reflex is fast because the impulse takes a short route through the spinal cord, so it limits harm.

Pulling away from heat, blinking at dust, a woodlouse rolling up when touched — each protects the body before you could think about it. For the woodlouse, rolling up shows only its hard shell and protects it from predators.

A reflex is automatic — you cannot choose not to do it. That is the one-mark answer to "why is this a reflex".

Pain itself is useful too: it stops you using an injured part, so it can heal, and teaches you to avoid what hurt you.

The synapse

Spec 2.89 · 2023 Edexcel 1B Q4(b)(ii) · 2024 Y10 Q1(c)

A synapse is a tiny gap between two neurones, crossed by a chemical, not by electricity.

  1. An impulse arrives at the end of the first neurone.
  2. It causes a neurotransmitter to be released into the gap.
  3. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the synapse.
  4. It binds to receptors on the next neurone.
  5. This starts a new electrical impulse.

The neurotransmitter is only released on one side, and the receptors are only on the other. So synapses make impulses travel in one direction only.

Drugs such as painkillers can work by stopping receptors detecting the stimulus, stopping impulses in the sensory or relay neurones, or blocking transmission at synapses — the four-mark ideas in the 2023 paper.

✗ "The impulse jumps across the gap."
✓ "A neurotransmitter is released, diffuses across the synapse and binds to receptors on the next neurone."

The eye — structure and function

Rods and cones

Spec 2.91

Rods work in dim light but see no colour; cones see colour but need bright light.

ReceptorWorks inDetects

That is why everything looks grey at night: there is not enough light to stimulate the cones.

The pupil reflex — bright and dim light

Spec 2.92 · 2023 Y10 Q8(a)(iii) · 2024 Y11 mock Q10(a)(ii) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2B Q2(a)(ii)

In bright light the pupil constricts to protect the retina; in dim light it dilates to let more light in. The iris has two sets of muscle that work in opposite directions — they are antagonistic.

FeatureBright lightDim light
Circular musclesContractRelax
Radial musclesRelaxContract
PupilConstricts — smallerDilates — wider
Light enteringLess — protects the retinaMore — enough to see

Memory hook: circular muscles make a circle smaller, like pulling a drawstring. Radial muscles run outwards like spokes and pull the pupil wide.

The albino rats in the 2022 paper have an iris with no pigment, so it does not block light. Changing the pupil size then has little effect: too much light reaches the retina, which can damage it and gives poor vision.

Focusing — near and distant objects

Spec 2.92 · 2022 Y10 Q5(a) · 2024 Y10 Q8(a)(ii) · 2023 Y10 Q8(a)(ii)

A near object needs more refraction, so the lens becomes fatter; a distant object needs less, so the lens becomes thinner. This is called accommodation.

FeatureNear objectDistant object
Ciliary musclesContract — the ring gets smallerRelax — the ring gets bigger
Suspensory ligamentsSlackenPulled tight
LensThicker, more curvedThinner
RefractionMoreLess

The near-object answer — the school's six marks

1. Ciliary muscles contract 1

2. The ring gets smaller in diameter 1

3. Suspensory ligaments slacken 1

4. Lens becomes thicker / more curved 1

5. So light is refracted more 1

6. And focused on the retina 1

The part that feels backwards: contracting the ciliary muscle loosens the ligaments. The ciliary muscle is a ring. When it contracts, the ring gets smaller and moves closer to the lens, so the ligaments stop pulling. The lens then springs into a fatter shape.

When focusing goes wrong

2022 Y10 Q5(b) · 2024 Y10 Q8(b)

If the lens cannot bend light enough, or light cannot get through it, the image on the retina is blurred or dim. The school has asked about both.

Long sight: the eyeball is too short, or the lens cannot become thick enough. Light from a near object is focused behind the retina, so near things look blurred.

Cataract: the lens goes cloudy. Less light passes through to the retina, so fewer impulses are sent to the brain and vision is dim and blurred.

Two eyes — judging distance

2024 Y11 mock Q10(b) · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2B Q2(b)

Two eyes facing forward see the same area from slightly different angles, which lets the brain judge distance.

In the practical, a student tries to touch pins on a board using one eye, then both. The independent variable is the number of eyes used. Keep the lighting, the distance from the board and the pins the same.

More pins hit with two eyes shows better distance judgement. Predators such as owls and cats have forward-facing eyes with a large overlapping field of view, which helps them catch prey. "A bigger field of view" on its own is ignored — it is the overlap that matters.

04Understanding it

U1

Describe the pathway of a reflex when someone treads on a drawing pin, naming every stage.

Show answer

The pin is the stimulus. Pain receptors in the skin of the foot detect it and start an electrical impulse.

The impulse travels along a sensory neurone to the spinal cord, crosses a synapse to a relay neurone, then another synapse to a motor neurone.

The motor neurone carries the impulse to the effector — a leg muscle — which contracts. The response is that the foot is lifted away.

The pathway goes through the spinal cord, not the conscious part of the brain, so it is very quick and limits the damage.

U2

You walk from bright sunshine into a dark room and cannot see for a moment. Explain what your iris does, and why.

Show answer

In the dark the radial muscles contract and the circular muscles relax. This dilates the pupil.

A wider pupil lets more light reach the retina, so enough light falls on the rods to form an image.

The moment of blindness is because the change is not instant — the muscles take a moment to respond, and the rods need time to adjust to dim light.

U3

Explain how the eye changes focus from a distant hill to a book held close.

Show answer

Looking at the hill, the ciliary muscles are relaxed, the suspensory ligaments are tight and the lens is thin — little refraction is needed.

Light from the book needs bending more. The ciliary muscles contract, which slackens the suspensory ligaments. With the pull removed, the lens becomes thicker and more curved, so it refracts light more and focuses it on the retina.

The ciliary muscle is a ring: contracting makes the ring smaller, so the ligaments attached around it go slack.

U4

Why can a nerve impulse cross a synapse in only one direction?

Show answer

Because the neurotransmitter is only released from one side — the end of the first neurone — and the receptors it binds to are only on the other side, on the next neurone.

There is nothing to release transmitter going backwards, and no receptor to receive it. So the signal can only cross one way.

U5

Someone loses colour vision in dim light but can still see shapes. Which receptor cells are involved, and why?

Show answer

Cones detect colour but need bright light. Rods work in dim light but give only black and white.

In dim light there is not enough light to stimulate the cones, so only the rods respond. Shapes and movement are still visible, but with no cone stimulation there is no colour.

U6

A painkiller stops a person feeling pain from an injured knee. Suggest three places in the nervous system where it could be working.

Show answer

At the receptor: it stops the pain receptors detecting the stimulus, so no impulses start.

In the neurones: it stops impulses being sent along the sensory or relay neurones.

At the synapses: it blocks the neurotransmitter being released, or binding to receptors, so the impulse does not cross to the next neurone.

Any of these means fewer impulses reach the brain, so the pain is not felt. This was a four-mark question in the 2023 real paper.

U7

Albino rats have an iris with no coloured pigment. Explain why they have poor vision in bright light.

Show answer

A normal iris is pigmented, so it blocks light everywhere except the pupil. That is how changing the pupil size controls how much light gets in.

An iris with no pigment lets light straight through it. So even when the circular muscles contract and the pupil constricts, too much light still reaches the retina.

The retina is over-stimulated and can be damaged, so vision is poor in bright light.

U8

Students touched pins on a board. With one eye the median was 2 pins; with both eyes it was 6. What does this show, and why does it matter to an owl?

Show answer

Using both eyes gives much better distance judgement — you can tell how far away something is.

Two forward-facing eyes see the same area from slightly different angles. That overlapping field of view is what lets the brain judge distance.

An owl is a predator. It needs to judge exactly how far away its prey is to catch it. It does not need to see behind itself, because few animals hunt it.
