01How it actually works

The nervous system detects stimuli and produces responses, using electrical impulses that travel fast. It splits into the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) and the peripheral nervous system (all the nerves connecting to it).

The reflex arc, in order

Spec 2.86 – 2.88

A reflex action is rapid, automatic and does not involve conscious thought — the brain is not part of the pathway. That speed is protective.

The reflex arc — learn this orderstimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (spinal cord) → motor neurone → effector → responseeffector = muscle or gland · the brain is NOT in the pathway

Every stage carries a mark in a long-answer question. The two words most often missing are relay and effector.

The synapse

Spec 2.89

A synapse is the gap between two neurones. An electrical impulse cannot jump it, so the signal is carried chemically:

  1. The impulse arrives at the end of the first neurone.
  2. It triggers the release of a neurotransmitter into the gap.
  3. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the synapse.
  4. It binds to receptors on the next neurone.
  5. A new electrical impulse is started.

Because the transmitter is released on one side only, synapses make the signal travel in one direction.

The pupil reflex

In bright light the pupil must get smaller to protect the retina. In dim light it widens to let more light in.

03Understanding it

U1

Describe the pathway of a reflex when someone treads on a drawing pin, naming every stage.

Show answer

The pin is the stimulus. Pain receptors in the skin of the foot detect it and start an electrical impulse.

The impulse travels along a sensory neurone to the spinal cord, where it passes across a synapse to a relay neurone, then across another synapse to a motor neurone.

The motor neurone carries the impulse to the effector — a leg muscle — which contracts. The response is that the foot is lifted away.

Because the pathway goes through the spinal cord and not the brain, it is far quicker, which limits the damage.

U2

You walk from bright sunshine into a dark room and cannot see for a moment. Explain what your iris does, and why.

Show answer

In the dark, the radial muscles contract and the circular muscles relax. This dilates the pupil, making it wider.

A wider pupil lets more light enter and reach the retina, so enough light falls on the rod cells to form an image.

The moment of blindness happens because the change is not instant — the muscles take a short time to respond, and the rods need time to adjust to dim light.

U3

Explain how the eye changes focus from a distant hill to a book held close.

Show answer

Looking at the hill, the ciliary muscles are relaxed, so the suspensory ligaments are pulled tight and the lens is thin — little refraction is needed for nearly parallel light rays.

Switching to the book, light rays arrive diverging and need bending more. The ciliary muscles contract, which slackens the suspensory ligaments. With the tension released the lens becomes fatter and more curved, refracting light more strongly so it focuses on the retina.

The counter-intuitive part is that contracting a ring of muscle releases tension on the ligaments. The ciliary muscle is a ring: contracting makes the ring smaller, so the ligaments attached around it go slack.

U4

Why can a nerve impulse travel in only one direction across a synapse?

Show answer

Because the neurotransmitter is only stored and released on one side — the end of the first neurone — and the receptors that detect it are only on the other side, on the second neurone.

There is nothing to release transmitter travelling backwards and no receptor to receive it, so the signal cannot cross in the opposite direction.

U5

Someone loses colour vision in dim light but sees shapes fine. Which receptor cells are involved, and why does this happen?

Show answer

Cones detect colour but need bright light to work. Rods work in dim light but only give black and white.

In dim light there is not enough light to stimulate the cones, so only the rods respond. Shapes and movement are still visible, but without cone stimulation there is no colour information — which is why everything looks grey at night.

04Exam drill

D1

State what is meant by a reflex action. (2)

Show answer

A rapid / automatic response to a stimulus 1; that does not involve conscious thought / the brain 1.

D2

List, in order, the parts of a reflex arc from stimulus to response. (3)

Show answer

Stimulus → receptor 1; sensory neurone → relay neurone → motor neurone 1; effector → response 1.

D3

Describe how a nerve impulse passes across a synapse. (3)

Show answer

The impulse causes a neurotransmitter to be released into the gap 1; the neurotransmitter diffuses across the synapse 1; it binds to receptors on the next neurone, starting a new impulse 1.

D4

Describe the changes in the iris when a person moves into bright light. (3)

Show answer

The circular muscles contract 1; the radial muscles relax 1; so the pupil constricts / gets smaller, letting in less light and protecting the retina 1.

D5

Describe how the eye focuses on a near object. (3)

Show answer

The ciliary muscles contract 1; the suspensory ligaments slacken 1; the lens becomes fatter / more curved, so light is refracted more 1.

D6

Name the part of the eye that carries out most of the refraction, and the part that contains the receptor cells. (2)

Show answer

Cornea 1; retina 1.

D7

Give one difference between rods and cones. (2)

Show answer

Rods work in dim light but do not detect colour 1; cones detect colour but need bright light 1.

D8

Explain the advantage of a reflex action not involving the brain. (2)

Show answer

The pathway is shorter so the response is faster 1; which reduces the damage / injury caused by the harmful stimulus 1.
