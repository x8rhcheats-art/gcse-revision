01How it actually works

Air travels trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli. The alveoli are where gas exchange happens: oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses out. Everything above the alveoli is plumbing.

Ventilation — the two movements

Spec 2.47

This is a pressure story, and it needs all four steps in order. Volume changes first, pressure follows, air moves last.

The diaphragm flattens when it contracts. It is a domed muscle at rest, so contracting pulls it down into a flatter shape — students routinely write that it "moves up" when breathing in, which is exactly backwards.

How the alveolus is adapted — the four-mark answer

Spec 2.48

Each adaptation must be paired with why it speeds diffusion. A bare list of features scores half marks at best.

AdaptationWhy it helps
Millions of alveoliLarge surface area for diffusion
Wall one cell thickShort diffusion distance between air and blood
Surrounded by a dense capillary networkGood blood supply maintains the concentration gradient
Ventilated by breathingFresh air maintains the concentration gradient on the air side
Moist liningGases dissolve before diffusing across

"Maintains the concentration gradient" is the phrase that separates a 2-mark answer from a 4-mark one. Blood flow keeps oxygen low on the blood side; breathing keeps it high on the air side; the difference is what drives diffusion.

Exhaled air still contains plenty of oxygen — about 16% — which is why mouth-to-mouth resuscitation works. "All the oxygen is used up" is wrong.

The practical: exercise and breathing rate

Spec 2.50

Measure breathing rate (breaths per minute) at rest, exercise for a fixed time, then measure again immediately and at intervals until it returns to resting.

The explanation: exercise means muscles respire faster, needing more oxygen and producing more carbon dioxide. Breathing rate and depth rise to supply oxygen and remove carbon dioxide faster. Afterwards it stays high to repay the oxygen debt from any anaerobic respiration.

Controls worth naming: same person, same exercise and duration, same rest before starting, and counting over the same time interval.

02Cheat sheet

The ventilation chain, in order

Muscles contract → volume increases → pressure decreases → air moves in. Reverse every step for breathing out. Never skip the pressure step — it is its own mark.

03Understanding it

U1

Explain how air is drawn into the lungs, from muscle contraction to air movement.

Show answer

The intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribcage up and out. At the same time the diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving down.

Together these increase the volume of the thorax. Increasing the volume decreases the pressure inside, so it falls below atmospheric pressure. Air therefore moves in, down the pressure gradient.

The lungs are passive throughout — they do not pull. Answers phrased as "the lungs suck air in" miss the pressure mark, which is the one the question is really testing.

U2

A patient with emphysema has fewer, larger alveoli. Explain why they become breathless during mild exercise.

Show answer

Fewer, larger alveoli means a much smaller total surface area for gas exchange. Less oxygen can diffuse into the blood per breath.

During exercise the muscles respire faster and need more oxygen, but the damaged lungs cannot supply it. The patient breathes faster and deeper to compensate, and still feels short of breath. More anaerobic respiration occurs, producing lactic acid and fatigue.

U3

Why does blood flowing past the alveoli keep gas exchange going, rather than diffusion simply stopping once the gases equalise?

Show answer

Because the blood is constantly replaced. Deoxygenated blood arriving has a low oxygen concentration, and oxygenated blood carrying oxygen away is removed. This maintains the concentration gradient.

Breathing does the same job on the other side: fresh air keeps the alveolar oxygen concentration high and carbon dioxide low.

If both stopped, the concentrations would equalise and net diffusion would stop — which is exactly what happens if you hold your breath.

U4

A smoker gets repeated chest infections. Explain the link, naming the component of smoke responsible.

Show answer

Tar paralyses and destroys the cilia lining the airways.

Normally the cilia sweep mucus — which traps dust, bacteria and other pathogens — up and out of the lungs. With the cilia damaged, that mucus stays in the airways, along with the bacteria trapped in it.

The trapped bacteria multiply, causing repeated infections such as bronchitis. The build-up of mucus also triggers the persistent "smoker's cough".

U5

A student's breathing rate is 14 at rest, 32 immediately after exercise, and still 20 two minutes later. Explain the third figure.

Show answer

Breathing rate stays above resting because of the oxygen debt. During exercise some muscle respiration was anaerobic, producing lactic acid.

Extra oxygen is needed to break down that lactic acid, so breathing remains elevated until it has been cleared. Only then does the rate return to 14.

A common wrong answer is "the body is still recovering" — true but not creditworthy. The mark is for naming lactic acid and the oxygen needed to remove it.

04Exam drill

D1

Name the structures air passes through, in order, from the throat to the site of gas exchange. (2)

Show answer

Trachea, bronchi, bronchioles 1; alveoli 1.

D2

Describe the changes in the thorax that cause air to leave the lungs. (4)

Show answer

Intercostal muscles relax so the ribcage moves down and in 1; the diaphragm relaxes and moves up into a domed shape 1; the volume of the thorax decreases so the pressure increases 1; air moves out down the pressure gradient 1.

D3

Explain how the alveoli are adapted for efficient gas exchange. (4)

Show answer

There are millions of alveoli giving a large surface area for diffusion 1; the walls are one cell thick giving a short diffusion distance 1; a dense capillary network maintains the concentration gradient 1; the lining is moist so gases dissolve before diffusing 1.

D4

Explain why carbon monoxide in cigarette smoke is harmful. (2)

Show answer

It binds to haemoglobin in the red blood cells in place of oxygen 1; so it reduces the capacity of the blood to carry oxygen 1.

D5

Give two differences in composition between inhaled and exhaled air. (2)

Show answer

Exhaled air contains less oxygen, about 16% compared with 21% 1; exhaled air contains more carbon dioxide, about 4% compared with 0.04% 1. More water vapour is also accepted.

D6

A student measures breathing rate before and after exercise. State the independent and dependent variables, and give two control variables. (4)

Show answer

Independent: whether the person is at rest or exercising / the amount of exercise 1; dependent: breathing rate in breaths per minute 1; one control from same person, same type of exercise, same duration, same counting interval 1; a second control from that list 1.

D7

Explain why breathing rate increases during exercise. (3)

Show answer

Muscles respire faster during exercise 1; so more oxygen is needed and more carbon dioxide is produced 1; breathing rate increases to supply the oxygen and remove the carbon dioxide more quickly 1.

D8

Explain the role of the pleural membranes. (2)

Show answer

They line the lungs and the chest cavity 1; the fluid between them reduces friction as the lungs move during ventilation 1.
