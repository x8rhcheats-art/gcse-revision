02How it actually works

Air travels trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli. The alveoli are where gas exchange happens: oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses out. Everything above the alveoli is plumbing, and the muscles around it are the pump.

The thorax — the parts

Spec 2.46 · 2023 Y10 Q4(a) · 2024 Y10 Q3(a) · 2024 Y11 mock Q7(a)

Air passes down a branching system of tubes to millions of tiny air sacs, the alveoli. Label questions on this diagram come up almost every year.

StructureWhat it does
TracheaCarries air to the lungs; held open by rings of cartilage
BronchiOne to each lung, branching from the trachea — also held open by cartilage
BronchiolesNarrow branches ending at the alveoli
AlveoliTiny air sacs — the site of gas exchange
RibsProtect the lungs and move during ventilation
Intercostal musclesBetween the ribs; move the ribcage up and out, or down and in
DiaphragmDome-shaped sheet of muscle below the lungs; flattens when it contracts

Bronchus is one; bronchi is two. The labelling questions give you letters on a diagram: the thick tube with rings is the trachea, the two tubes it splits into are bronchi, and the fine branches are bronchioles.

Keeping the airways clean

Spec 2.46, 2.49

Mucus traps dirt and bacteria, and cilia sweep it up and out of the lungs.

  1. Cells in the lining of the trachea and bronchi make mucus.
  2. Dust and bacteria in the air stick to it.
  3. Cilia — tiny hairs on the lining cells — beat and sweep the mucus up to the throat.
  4. It is swallowed, and stomach acid kills the bacteria.

This matters because smoking breaks it. It is the link between tar and chest infections.

Breathing in

Spec 2.47 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1B Q1(b) · 2023 Y10 Q4(a)(ii)

Muscles contract, the chest gets bigger, the pressure inside drops, and air is pushed in from outside. Volume changes first, pressure follows, air moves last.

  1. The intercostal muscles contract, moving the ribcage up and out.
  2. The diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving down.
  3. The volume of the thorax increases.
  4. The pressure inside decreases, below atmospheric pressure.
  5. Air moves in, down the pressure gradient.

The breathing-in answer — four marks

1. Diaphragm contracts and flattens / moves down 1

2. Intercostal muscles contract, ribcage moves up and out 1

3. Volume of the thorax increases 1

4. Pressure decreases, so air is drawn in 1

✗ "The diaphragm moves up." ✗ "The diaphragm collapses." ✗ "The lungs expand and suck air in."
✓ "The diaphragm contracts and flattens, so the volume increases and the pressure decreases."

The lungs cannot pull. They are passive — they follow the chest wall.

Breathing out

Spec 2.47

Breathing out at rest is every step of breathing in, reversed.

FeatureBreathing inBreathing out

RibcageMoves up and outMoves down and in
DiaphragmContracts and flattensRelaxes and domes upwards
Volume of thoraxIncreasesDecreases
Pressure in thoraxDecreasesIncreases
AirMoves inMoves out

The diaphragm is domed at rest. Contracting pulls it down into a flatter shape. Relaxing lets it spring back up.

Gas exchange is diffusion

Spec 2.48

Oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli into the blood, and carbon dioxide diffuses the other way, each down its concentration gradient.

  • Air in the alveolus has a high oxygen concentration. Blood arriving from the body has a low one. So oxygen diffuses into the blood.
  • Blood arriving has a high carbon dioxide concentration. The alveolus air has a low one. So carbon dioxide diffuses into the alveolus and is breathed out.

Diffusion is passive — no energy is used. Anything that makes it faster is an adaptation.

How the alveoli are adapted — the four-mark answer

Spec 2.48 · 2022 Y10 Q4(a)

Every adaptation either increases the surface area, shortens the distance, or keeps the concentration gradient steep. Pair each feature with its reason. A bare list scores half marks at best.

AdaptationWhy it helps
Millions of alveoliLarge surface area for diffusion
Wall one cell thickShort diffusion distance between air and blood
Capillary wall one cell thick, pressed against the alveolusShort diffusion distance
Dense network of capillariesBlood flow carries oxygen away and brings carbon dioxide, which maintains the concentration gradient

"Maintains the concentration gradient" is the phrase that separates a two-mark answer from a four-mark one.

Inhaled versus exhaled air

2024 Y10 Q3(b) · 2024 Y11 mock Q7(b) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1B Q1(a)

Exhaled air has less oxygen and more carbon dioxide, because of gas exchange in the lungs and respiration in the cells.

GasInhaledExhaled
Oxygenabout 21%about 16%
Carbon dioxideabout 0.04%about 4%
Nitrogenabout 78%about 78% — not used
Water vapourVariesMore — saturated

The full answer has three parts: oxygen diffused into the blood and carbon dioxide out; the oxygen is used and the carbon dioxide made by respiration in cells; nitrogen is not used, so it does not change.

Exhaled air still has about 16% oxygen. It is also not the same as alveolar air: it is a mix of air from the alveoli and air that never got past the trachea and bronchi. Gas exchange only happens at the alveoli, so exhaled air has more oxygen than the air deep in the lungs.

The limewater practical: breathe in through one flask and out through the other. The flask you breathe out through turns milky faster, showing exhaled air has more carbon dioxide. The tube you breathe out through must dip into the limewater; the one you breathe in through must not, or you would suck up limewater.

Smoking — the heart and blood

Spec 2.49 · 2024 Edexcel 2B Q4(c) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1B Q4

Carbon monoxide reduces how much oxygen the blood can carry; nicotine and smoke damage the heart and blood vessels.

CauseEffect

Emphysema — why exercise is hard

2022 Y10 Q4

Less surface area means less oxygen gets into the blood, so the muscles release less energy.

  1. The alveoli have a smaller surface area.
  2. So less oxygen diffuses into the blood and reaches the muscles.
  3. So less aerobic respiration happens in the muscles.
  4. So less energy is released for muscle contraction.

The mark scheme's note: "Energy is produced / made" is not allowed. Use released.

Practical — exercise and breathing rate

Spec 2.50 · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q3(b)

Exercise makes muscles respire faster, so breathing gets faster and deeper to supply oxygen and remove carbon dioxide.

  1. Count breaths per minute at rest.
  2. Do a set exercise for a set time — for example step-ups for two minutes.
  3. Count breaths per minute straight after, then every minute until it is back to the resting rate.
  4. Repeat, or use several people, and calculate a mean.

Control: same person or people of the same age, sex and fitness; same type and length of exercise; same counting time.

The explanation: muscles contract more, so they respire faster, needing more oxygen and making more carbon dioxide. Breathing stays high afterwards to repay the oxygen debt — the oxygen needed to break down lactic acid.

Reading breathing data

2023 Y10 Q4(b) · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q3(a)

Ventilation rate is breaths per minute multiplied by the volume of each breath.

Ventilation rateventilation rate = breathing rate × volume per breathdm³ per minute · 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³

In the 2023 Year 10 cycling data, ventilation rate rose at low speeds only because each breath got deeper — the breathing rate stayed the same. Only at higher speeds did the rate rise. The teacher's comment: most students just repeated the table instead of commenting on it. Say what changes, where, and which part — rate or depth — is changing.

Vital capacity is the most air you can force out in one breath. It rises until about 20–25 years as the chest and lungs grow, levels off, then falls as the intercostal muscles and diaphragm weaken and the lungs become less elastic. A median is used when a few extreme values would distort the mean.

04Understanding it

U1

Explain how air is drawn into the lungs, from muscle contraction to air movement.

Show answer

The intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribcage up and out. At the same time the diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving down.

Together these increase the volume of the thorax. Increasing the volume decreases the pressure inside, so it falls below atmospheric pressure. Air therefore moves in, down the pressure gradient.

The lungs are passive throughout. "The lungs suck air in" misses the pressure mark, which is the one the question is really testing.

U2

A patient with emphysema has fewer, larger alveoli. Explain why they become breathless during mild exercise.

Show answer

Fewer, larger alveoli means a much smaller total surface area for gas exchange. Less oxygen can diffuse into the blood per breath.

During exercise the muscles respire faster and need more oxygen, but the damaged lungs cannot supply it. The patient breathes faster and deeper to make up for it, and still feels short of breath. More anaerobic respiration occurs, producing lactic acid and fatigue.

U3

Why does blood flowing past the alveoli keep gas exchange going, rather than diffusion simply stopping once the gases even out?

Show answer

Because the blood is constantly replaced. Blood arriving from the body has a low oxygen concentration, and blood that has picked up oxygen is carried away. This maintains the concentration gradient.

Breathing does the same job on the other side: fresh air keeps the alveolar oxygen concentration high and carbon dioxide low.

If both stopped, the concentrations would even out and net diffusion would stop — which is what happens if you hold your breath.

U4

A smoker gets repeated chest infections. Explain the link, naming the part of the smoke responsible.

Show answer

Tar paralyses and destroys the cilia lining the airways.

Normally the cilia sweep mucus — which traps dust and bacteria — up and out of the lungs. With the cilia damaged, the mucus stays in the airways, along with the bacteria trapped in it.

The bacteria multiply, causing repeated infections such as bronchitis. The build-up of mucus also causes the persistent "smoker's cough".

U5

A student's breathing rate is 14 at rest, 32 straight after exercise, and still 20 two minutes later. Explain the third figure.

Show answer

Breathing stays above resting because of the oxygen debt. During exercise some respiration in the muscles was anaerobic, producing lactic acid.

Extra oxygen is needed to break down that lactic acid, so breathing stays high until it has been cleared. Only then does the rate return to 14.

"The body is still recovering" is true but scores nothing. The mark is for naming lactic acid and the oxygen needed to remove it.

U6

Air deep in the alveoli has about 14% oxygen, but the air you breathe out has about 16%. How can exhaled air have more oxygen than the air it came from?

Show answer

Because exhaled air is a mixture. Some of it comes from the alveoli, where oxygen has diffused into the blood. The rest was sitting in the trachea, bronchi and bronchioles, which have no gas exchange, so it still has close to 21% oxygen.

Mix the two together and you get something in between. Gas exchange only happens at the alveoli — that is the mark.

U7

A study shows vital capacity rises until about age 22, stays level, then falls with age. Explain the shape, and why the scientists plotted the median for each age.

Show answer

Rising: the body is still growing, so the chest and lungs get bigger and hold more air.

Level: growth has stopped, so there is little change.

Falling: the intercostal muscles and diaphragm get weaker and the lungs become less elastic, so less air can be forced out.

Median: each age group had a wide range, with some extreme values — perhaps athletes, or people with lung disease. The median is the middle value, so it is less affected by extreme values than the mean.

U8

A young smoker with healthy alveoli still gets out of breath faster than a non-smoker. Explain why, without mentioning emphysema.

Show answer

Carbon monoxide in the smoke binds to haemoglobin in the red blood cells, in the place where oxygen would go.

So the blood carries less oxygen to the muscles, even though the lungs are fine. The muscles respire aerobically less, release less energy, and switch to anaerobic respiration sooner.

The smoker breathes harder to make up for it. Nicotine adds to the problem by raising the heart rate and narrowing blood vessels.
