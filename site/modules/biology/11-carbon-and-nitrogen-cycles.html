Carbon and Nitrogen Cycles

Year 10 content. Two cycles, and nearly every mark is for naming the right process or the right bacteria. Four kinds of microorganism live in the nitrogen cycle, and swapping any two of them loses the mark. Real Edexcel papers ask it as a labelled diagram, or hide it inside a passage about rivers, rainforests or fish farms.

02How it actually works

Atoms are never used up. Carbon and nitrogen move between the air, living things and the soil, and the same atoms go round again and again. Microorganisms do most of the returning. Every question in this module is really asking: which process moves the atom from here to there?

The carbon cycle — the processes

Spec 4.10 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1B Q3

Carbon moves between the air and living things by photosynthesis, respiration, feeding, decomposition and combustion. The spec names four of these — respiration, photosynthesis, decomposition and combustion — so learn those first.

ProcessWhat happens to carbon
PhotosynthesisPlants remove CO₂ from the air and make glucose
RespirationPlants, animals and decomposers return CO₂ to the air
FeedingCarbon compounds pass along the food chain as animals eat
DecompositionBacteria and fungi break down dead organisms and waste — and respire, releasing CO₂
CombustionBurning wood and fossil fuels returns CO₂ to the air
FossilisationDead matter that does not decay, under pressure over millions of years, becomes coal, oil and gas

In the air, carbon is in carbon dioxide. Inside living things it is in carbon compounds — carbohydrates, proteins and fats. Keep those two words apart and the arrows make sense.

Photosynthesis — the only way out of the air

Spec 4.10, 2.18 · links to module 2

Photosynthesis is the only process in the cycle that takes carbon dioxide out of the air.

Photosynthesis — carbon incarbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygenlight energy absorbed by chlorophyll · the carbon ends up in glucose, then starch, cellulose and proteins

Every other arrow puts carbon dioxide back. That one fact answers most "why does CO₂ rise" questions: less photosynthesis (deforestation) or more combustion.

✗ "Trees absorb carbon dioxide by respiration."
✗ "Plants only take carbon in."
✓ "Plants remove CO₂ by photosynthesis, but they also release CO₂ by respiration."

Respiration — everyone does it

Spec 4.10 · links to module 3 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1B Q3(b)(ii)

Every living organism respires, all the time, and every one of them returns carbon dioxide to the air.

Aerobic respiration — carbon outglucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + waterplants, animals, bacteria and fungi · day and night

On a cycle diagram there is a respiration arrow from plants, from animals and from the decomposers. The one from the decomposers is the one students forget.

Feeding — carbon along the food chain

Spec 4.8, 4.10 · links to module 9

When an animal eats, carbon passes to it as carbon compounds, not as carbon dioxide.

  1. A plant makes glucose, then starch, cellulose, proteins and fats.
  2. A herbivore eats the plant and digests these into small molecules.
  3. It absorbs them and builds its own carbon compounds — or respires them, releasing CO₂.
  4. A carnivore eats the herbivore, and the same happens again.

Carbon that is not respired or eaten ends up in dead bodies, faeces and urine — which is where the decomposers come in.

Decomposition — how decomposers return carbon

Spec 4.10 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1B Q3(c) · 2023 Edexcel 2B Q1(b)(ii)

Decomposers — bacteria and fungi — release carbon dioxide because they respire the products of the dead matter they digest.

  1. Decomposers secrete enzymes onto dead organisms and waste.
  2. The enzymes digest large carbon compounds into small soluble molecules.
  3. The decomposers absorb these molecules.
  4. They use the glucose in respiration, which releases carbon dioxide into the air.

Decomposition is fastest when it is warm, moist and has plenty of oxygen, because the decomposers' enzymes work best and they can respire aerobically. pH matters too — enzymes have an optimum pH.

Planning a decomposition investigation — the six marks (2022 Jan 1B Q3(d))

C — change the pH: add acid, alkali or buffers to give different pH values 1

O — same type, age and mass of organic material, e.g. leaves 1

R — repeat at each pH and calculate a mean 1

M1 — measure the loss in mass, or the carbon dioxide released (limewater or hydrogencarbonate indicator) 1

M2 — after a stated time, e.g. two weeks 1

S — keep the temperature, oxygen and water the same, and add the same decomposers 1

Combustion, fossil fuels and locked-up carbon

Spec 4.10 · 2022 Jan Edexcel 1B Q3(b)(i) · 2022 Edexcel 2B Q1(b)

When dead matter does not decompose, its carbon is locked away — until it is burned.

Waterlogged, acidic or oxygen-poor conditions stop decomposers. Over millions of years, under heat and pressure, the undecayed matter becomes coal, oil and natural gas. Combustion of these fossil fuels releases, in a few years, carbon that was stored for millions — which is why CO₂ in the air is rising (module 12).

The 2022 paper turned this round: plants that store carbon in a substance that decays slowly keep it out of the air for longer. The marks were:

  1. Plants take in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis.
  2. The carbon is stored in a compound that is decomposed slowly.
  3. So less carbon dioxide is released by the respiration of decomposers.

The nitrogen cycle — why plants need help

Spec 4.11B, 2.22

Air is 78% nitrogen, but plants cannot use nitrogen gas — they must absorb nitrate ions from the soil.

N₂ is very unreactive. Plants need nitrogen to make amino acids, and so proteins (and DNA and chlorophyll). They take nitrate in through their root hair cells by active transport, because the concentration in the soil is usually lower than in the root. Animals get their nitrogen by eating proteins.

Nitrogen fixation and root nodules

Spec 4.11B

Nitrogen-fixing bacteria are the only living route from nitrogen gas into the food chain.

Some live free in the soil. Others live in root nodules — swellings on the roots of legumes such as peas, beans and clover. It is a partnership: the bacteria give the plant nitrogen compounds for amino acids, and the plant gives the bacteria sugars from photosynthesis.

That is why legumes grow well in poor soil, and why a field is richer in nitrogen after a crop of clover.

From dead matter to nitrate — decomposers and nitrifying bacteria

Spec 4.11B · 2023 Jan Edexcel 2B Q5(b)(i) · 2023 Edexcel 2B Q1(c) · 2022 Jan Edexcel 2B Q4(b)

Nitrogen in dead organisms reaches plants in two bacterial steps: decomposers make ammonium, then nitrifying bacteria turn ammonium into nitrate.

  1. A plant or animal dies, or an animal excretes urea or produces faeces.
  2. Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down the proteins and urea into ammonium compounds.
  3. Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium into nitrite, then nitrite into nitrate. This is nitrification, and it needs oxygen.
  4. Plant roots absorb the nitrate by active transport and use it to make amino acids and proteins.

The three-mark chain (2023 Jan 2B Q5(b)(i))

1. Decomposition by bacteria / fungi 1

2. Organic waste → ammonium (ammonification) 1

3. Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate 1

"Ammonium to nitrate" with no bacteria named was ignored. Name the nitrifying bacteria, and mention nitrite if you can.

Fish farms use the same bacteria. Fish excrete ammonium, which is toxic to them; a filter full of nitrifying bacteria turns it into nitrite and then the much less harmful nitrate.

Denitrifying bacteria and waterlogged soil

Spec 4.11B

Nitrifying bacteria need oxygen; denitrifying bacteria work without it — so waterlogged soil loses its nitrate.

  1. Water fills the air spaces in the soil, so there is little oxygen.
  2. Nitrifying bacteria cannot respire aerobically, so they stop making nitrate.
  3. Denitrifying bacteria are anaerobic, so they thrive and turn nitrate into nitrogen gas, which escapes to the air.
  4. Less nitrate for the crop → fewer amino acids and proteins → stunted growth and yellow leaves.

✗ "Waterlogged soil drowns the bacteria."
✗ "Nitrifying bacteria turn nitrate into nitrogen."
✓ "No oxygen, so nitrifying bacteria stop, and denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate into nitrogen gas."

Say both halves. One bacterium stopping and the other speeding up are two separate marks.

04Understanding it

U1

A leaf falls to the forest floor. Trace one carbon atom in it back into the atmosphere.

Show answer

The carbon is in organic compounds such as cellulose. Decomposers — bacteria and fungi — secrete enzymes that break these down, and absorb the products.

The decomposers use the glucose in respiration, which releases the carbon as carbon dioxide into the air. Decomposition itself does not release CO₂ — the decomposers' respiration does.

U2

A field floods for several weeks. Afterwards the crop grows poorly. Explain, using the nitrogen cycle.

Show answer

Waterlogged soil has no air spaces, so it lacks oxygen.

Nitrifying bacteria need oxygen, so they stop turning ammonium into nitrates. Denitrifying bacteria are anaerobic and thrive, converting the nitrates that remain into nitrogen gas.

Both changes mean less nitrate in the soil. The crop cannot make enough amino acids and proteins, so its growth is stunted.

U3

Why might a farmer plant clover in a field one year and wheat the next?

Show answer

Clover is a legume with nitrogen-fixing bacteria in its root nodules. They convert nitrogen gas into nitrogen compounds, enriching the soil.

When the clover is ploughed in, decomposers and nitrifying bacteria turn its proteins into nitrates. The wheat then has plenty of nitrate for amino acids and proteins, so less fertiliser is needed.

U4

Name the only process in the carbon cycle that removes CO₂ from the air, and explain why cutting down rainforest raises CO₂ in two ways.

Show answer

Photosynthesis is the only process that removes CO₂.

Deforestation raises CO₂ because fewer trees means less photosynthesis, so less is removed; and the trees are usually burned or left to decompose, which releases the carbon stored in them.

U5

Leaves decompose within months on a warm, damp forest floor, but can last thousands of years in a peat bog. Explain the difference.

Show answer

Decomposers need oxygen to respire aerobically, water, and a suitable temperature and pH for their enzymes.

A bog is waterlogged, so there is almost no oxygen, and it is acidic. Few decomposers survive and their enzymes work slowly, so the dead matter is not broken down. Its carbon stays locked away instead of returning to the air as CO₂. Over millions of years, that is how fossil fuels formed.

U6

A fish tank filter is full of bacteria. Why do new tanks often kill fish in the first few weeks?

Show answer

Fish excrete ammonium, which is toxic. In an established tank, nitrifying bacteria in the filter convert ammonium into nitrite and then into nitrate, which is far less harmful.

In a new tank the population of nitrifying bacteria is still small, so ammonium builds up faster than they can convert it. The filter also needs oxygen flowing through it, because nitrifying bacteria are aerobic.

U7

Why do plants take in nitrate by active transport rather than diffusion, and what would happen to a root with no oxygen?

Show answer

The concentration of nitrate in the soil water is usually lower than inside the root hair cells. Diffusion only moves particles down a gradient, so it would carry nitrate out. Active transport moves it against the concentration gradient, using energy from respiration.

Without oxygen the root cells cannot respire aerobically, so there is too little energy for active transport. Nitrate uptake falls — a second reason waterlogged plants struggle.

U8

Follow one nitrogen atom from the air, into a pea plant, into a rabbit, and back to the air.

Show answer

Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the pea's root nodules turn N₂ into nitrogen compounds. The pea uses them to make amino acids and proteins.

The rabbit eats the pea, digests the proteins to amino acids and builds its own proteins. Excess is excreted as urea. When the rabbit dies, decomposers turn its proteins into ammonium, and nitrifying bacteria turn that into nitrate.

In waterlogged soil, denitrifying bacteria turn the nitrate into nitrogen gas, which returns to the air.
