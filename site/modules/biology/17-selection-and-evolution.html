From the school's January 2023 mock scheme, on the woodlouse question:

"'Passes on the mutation / behaviour' alone is not enough — say alleles or genes."

The last of the four marks was for passing on the alleles. A mutation happens once, in one individual; what spreads through the population is the allele it created. Behaviour is not inherited directly at all — the allele that causes it is.

02How it actually works

Individuals in a species are not identical. Some of those differences are inherited, and some of the inherited ones help an organism survive. Over many generations, the helpful alleles become more common. That is natural selection — and when humans do the choosing instead of the environment, it is selective breeding.

Variation — genes, environment or both

Spec 3.33 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 2B Q3(c)(i)

Variation within a species can be genetic, environmental, or a combination of both.

CauseWhat it meansExamples
GeneticDecided by the alleles inheritedBlood group, eye colour, cystic fibrosis
EnvironmentalDecided by conditions during lifeScars, language spoken, a plant growing tall in shade
BothGenes set a range; the environment decides where in it you end upHeight, body mass, crop yield

Identical twins have identical genes, so any difference between them must be environmental. Only genetic variation can be passed on to offspring — and so only genetic variation matters for natural selection.

Where genetic variation comes from

Spec 3.30, 3.31, 3.34

Genetic variation comes from mutation, which makes new alleles, and from sexual reproduction, which shuffles existing ones.

  1. Mutation — a rare, random change in DNA. The only source of brand-new alleles (module 15).
  2. Meiosis — each gamete gets a different mix of chromosomes, so gametes are genetically different.
  3. Random fertilisation — any sperm can fuse with any egg, giving new combinations of alleles.

Asexual reproduction uses mitosis only, so the offspring are clones. Apart from rare mutations, there is no variation between them.

Why some features vary more than others

Spec 3.22, 3.33 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 2B Q3(c)(i)

A feature controlled by one gene falls into a few clear groups; one controlled by many genes and the environment varies smoothly.

Hair colour in cattle: one gene, two alleles, three colours. Height in cattle: polygenic — many genes each add a little — and nutrition adds more. So a herd has three hair colours but a whole range of heights. The 2023 scheme gave one mark for each idea, and ignored "multiple alleles".

Darwin's theory — natural selection in five steps

Spec 3.38 · 2023 Y11 mock Q2(c)(iii)

Individuals with inherited features that suit their environment are more likely to survive and reproduce, so they pass on their alleles and those alleles become more common.

  1. A mutation produces a new allele, so there is variation in the population.
  2. Some individuals have a characteristic that gives a survival advantage in their environment — for example avoiding predators, finding food, resisting disease.
  3. Those individuals are more likely to survive; others die, often through competition or predation.
  4. The survivors reproduce.
  5. They pass on the advantageous allele to their offspring, so over many generations it becomes more common in the population.

Every step is a mark. The one lost most is the last: say alleles or genes are passed on.

✗ "The woodlice learned to roll up to survive."
✗ "The woodlice adapted to roll up because of the predators."
✓ "A mutation gave some woodlice an allele for rolling up. They were more likely to survive predators and reproduce, passing on the allele."

Individuals do not change to suit their environment. The environment decides which of the already-different individuals leave offspring.

Writing it for an unfamiliar organism

Spec 3.38 · 2023 Y11 mock Q2(c)(iii)

Every natural selection question is the same five steps with the organism's own advantage dropped in at step 2.

First find the advantage in the question. Rolling up protects the soft underside from predators. Pale fur is camouflage in snow. A long neck reaches higher leaves. Then write the chain.

The woodlouse answer — four marks

1. A mutation produced an allele for rolling up 1

2. There is variation — woodlice that roll up are protected from predators and survive 1

3. They reproduce 1

4. Passing on the alleles to their offspring 1

"Survival of the fittest" on its own scores nothing. Write the steps.

Competition and extinction

Spec 3.38 · 2024 Edexcel 2B Q1(b)

When two species compete for the same food, the better-adapted one survives and reproduces more, and the other can be driven to extinction.

The 2024 passage: scientists think the giant shark megalodon was replaced by giant, predatory toothed whales. The marks went to:

  • competition between megalodon and the whales for food
  • the whales were better adapted — faster, or better at hunting — so they ate more of the food
  • the whales survived and reproduced more, having more offspring

Most species that have ever lived are now extinct. A species goes extinct when its environment changes — a new competitor, a new predator, a new disease, a change in climate — and none of its individuals have the alleles to survive it.

From natural selection to evolution

Spec 3.38

Evolution is the change in the inherited characteristics of a population over many generations, and natural selection is how it happens.

One generation changes the proportion of an allele a little. Thousands of generations change a population a lot. If two groups of the same species are separated — by a sea, a mountain range — and face different conditions, different alleles are selected in each. Given enough time, they can become so different that they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring: they have become separate species.

Darwin's evidence came from observing variation in the wild, from fossils showing gradual change, and from the results of selective breeding.

Antibiotic resistance

Spec 3.39

Antibiotic resistance spreads by natural selection: the antibiotic kills the non-resistant bacteria, and the resistant ones survive and reproduce.

  1. A random mutation gives one bacterium an allele for resistance.
  2. The antibiotic is used. It kills the non-resistant bacteria.
  3. The resistant bacterium survives, with less competition.
  4. It reproduces — bacteria divide every 20 minutes or so in good conditions.
  5. Its offspring inherit the resistance allele. Soon most of the population is resistant.

It is fast because bacteria reproduce so quickly: many generations pass in a single day.

✗ "The antibiotic caused the bacteria to become resistant."
✓ "A random mutation had already made some bacteria resistant. The antibiotic selected them by killing the rest."

Why resistant infections are hard to control

Spec 3.39

Once bacteria are resistant, the usual antibiotic no longer kills them, so the infection spreads and is difficult to treat.

MRSA is a strain of Staphylococcus aureus resistant to many antibiotics; it spreads in hospitals, where many people are ill and many antibiotics are used. Doctors then have to find a different antibiotic, and bacteria may become resistant to that one too.

To slow the spread of resistance:

  • Do not use antibiotics for viral infections — they do not work on viruses, and every use selects for resistance.
  • Finish the course, so the more resistant bacteria are killed too, rather than surviving to multiply.
  • Avoid overuse — for example, routine use in farm animals.
  • Good hygiene in hospitals, so resistant bacteria are not passed between patients.

Pesticide resistance

Spec 3.39 · 2024 Y11 mock Q2(d)

Pesticide resistance works exactly like antibiotic resistance, with insects in place of bacteria.

In the fire ant question, numbers fell sharply after the pesticide, then rose again after about six months. Two explanations scored: the pesticide wore off, or a few ants had an allele for resistance, survived, reproduced and passed it on. Phorid flies — a biological control that breeds and keeps working — held numbers down for longer. That comparison is module 10.

Selective breeding in plants

Spec 5.10 · 2022 Edexcel 1B Q9 · 2023 Edexcel 2B Q6(a)(i)

In selective breeding, humans choose the parents with the characteristic they want, breed them, and repeat over many generations.

  1. Choose parent plants with the desired characteristic — for example the highest grain yield.
  2. Cross-pollinate them, or let them self-pollinate.
  3. Grow the seeds and select the offspring that show the characteristic best.
  4. Breed those, and repeat over many generations.

Examples: high-yield wheat, disease-resistant crops, brightly coloured flowers. The 2022 paper added dwarf wheat — a shorter, thicker stem means less energy used for growing tall and so more for grain, and the plants are less likely to be blown over by wind and rain and are easier to harvest.

Describe selective breeding — three marks

1. Select and breed parents with the desired characteristic, e.g. highest yield 1

2. Select the offspring with that characteristic and breed them 1

3. Repeat over many generations 1

Selective breeding in animals

Spec 5.11 · 2022 Edexcel Jan 2B Q4(a) · 2023 Edexcel Jan 2B Q3(c)(ii)

The same method works for animals: mate the best, keep the best offspring, repeat — so the alleles for the characteristic are passed on and become more common.

Examples: cows that give more milk, beef cattle that grow faster, sheep with better wool, fish that grow rapidly, docile dogs. For the fish question, the extra mark was the reason: genes or alleles for fast growth are passed on.

It takes a long time, because each round must wait for the animals to grow up and breed.

Problems with selective breeding — and how it differs from natural selection

Spec 5.10, 5.11, 3.38

Selective breeding narrows the range of alleles, so the population loses variation.

  • Inbreeding — the best animals are often related, so harmful recessive alleles are more likely to meet, causing genetic disorders.
  • Less variation — if a new disease appears, there may be no individuals with an allele to resist it, so the whole crop or herd can be lost.
  • Features chosen for people's benefit can harm the animal — for example breathing problems in some dog breeds.
FeatureNatural selectionSelective breeding
Who "chooses"The environment — who survivesHumans — who breeds
Characteristics favouredThose that help survivalThose useful to humans
SpeedUsually very slowFaster — only chosen parents breed
Effect on variationVariation kept by mutation and sexual reproductionVariation reduced

04Understanding it

U1

Some woodlice roll into a ball when touched. Explain how this behaviour could have become common through natural selection.

Show answer

A mutation produced an allele causing rolling-up behaviour, giving variation.

Woodlice that roll up expose only their hard shell, so they are less likely to be eaten and more likely to survive. They reproduce and pass on the allele. Over many generations the allele becomes more common, so more of the population rolls up.

U2

Why do doctors tell patients to finish a course of antibiotics?

Show answer

Stopping early kills only the most susceptible bacteria, leaving the more resistant ones alive. They reproduce and pass on the alleles for resistance.

Finishing the course kills as many bacteria as possible, so fewer resistant ones survive to multiply.

U3

Why does antibiotic resistance spread through bacteria in months, while a new feature in elephants takes thousands of years?

Show answer

Natural selection works one generation at a time. Bacteria can divide every 20 minutes or so, so there are many generations in a single day. Elephants take over ten years to breed.

Bacteria also exist in huge numbers, so a rare resistance mutation is likely to be present somewhere. And the antibiotic is a very strong selection pressure — it kills almost all the non-resistant bacteria at once.

U4

A student writes: "Giraffes stretched their necks to reach high leaves, and their young were born with longer necks." What is wrong with this explanation?

Show answer

Stretching during life does not change the alleles in the gametes, so it cannot be inherited. Only genetic variation is passed on.

Darwin's version: giraffes already varied in neck length because of different alleles. Those with longer necks reached more food, so were more likely to survive and reproduce, passing on the alleles for longer necks. Over many generations, the average neck got longer.

U5

Fire ant numbers fall after a pesticide is sprayed, then rise again six months later even though spraying continues. Explain, using natural selection.

Show answer

By chance, a few ants had an allele — from a mutation — that made them resistant to the pesticide. The pesticide killed the non-resistant ants, so the resistant ones survived with less competition.

They reproduced and passed on the resistance allele, so each generation had a larger proportion of resistant ants. The pesticide no longer controls the population.

U6

Identical twins are separated at birth. As adults, one is 8 cm taller than the other. What does this tell you about the cause of the difference?

Show answer

Identical twins have identical alleles, so the difference cannot be genetic. It must be environmental — for example diet, illness or exercise during childhood.

Height is affected by both genes and environment: the genes set a range, and the environment decides where in that range each twin ends up.

U7

A whole field of a selectively bred, high-yield wheat variety is destroyed by a new fungal disease, while a field of old-fashioned wheat nearby survives. Explain why.

Show answer

Selective breeding for yield used only a few parent plants, so the new variety has a small range of alleles — very little variation. If none of the plants has an allele for resistance to the new fungus, all of them are killed.

The old wheat is more varied, so some plants are likely to carry an allele that lets them resist the disease and survive.

U8

How is selective breeding like natural selection, and how is it different?

Show answer

Alike: both need variation; in both, only some individuals breed, so their alleles become more common over many generations.

Different: in natural selection the environment decides who survives, favouring features that help survival. In selective breeding humans decide who breeds, favouring features useful to people — which may not help the organism at all. Selective breeding is usually faster and reduces variation.
