01How it actually works

Variation

Spec 3.31 – 3.33

Differences between individuals of a species come from three sources:

  • Genetic — from alleles inherited: blood group, eye colour
  • Environmental — from conditions: scars, language, a plant in shade
  • Both — genes set a potential, environment decides how much is reached: height, body mass

Genetic variation arises from mutation (new alleles), meiosis (shuffled chromosomes) and random fertilisation.

Natural selection — five steps

Spec 3.38

  1. A mutation produces a new allele, so there is variation in the population.
  2. Some individuals have a characteristic that gives a survival advantage in their environment.
  3. Those individuals are more likely to survive.
  4. They reproduce.
  5. They pass on the advantageous allele to their offspring, so over many generations it becomes more common.

Every step is a mark. The one lost most is the last: say alleles or genes are passed on. "Passes on the mutation" or "passes on the behaviour" was not accepted in the Year 11 mock.

Over a very long time, populations can change so much they can no longer interbreed — a new species. That is evolution.

Antibiotic resistance

Spec 3.39

The same five steps, fast, because bacteria reproduce every few minutes. A random mutation makes one bacterium resistant. When an antibiotic is used, the non-resistant bacteria die; the resistant one survives and reproduces, passing on the resistance allele. Soon the whole population is resistant — MRSA is the example.

Pesticide resistance works identically. In the fire ant question, numbers dropped after pesticide then rose again, because resistant ants survived and bred.

Selective breeding

Spec 5.10, 5.11

Here humans do the selecting:

  1. Choose parents with the desired characteristic.
  2. Breed them together.
  3. Select the offspring that show it best.
  4. Breed those, and repeat over many generations.

Examples: high-yield crops, disease-resistant wheat, cows giving more milk. The risk is inbreeding — a smaller range of alleles, so less variation, more genetic disorders, and the whole population is vulnerable to a new disease.

03Understanding it

U1

Some woodlice roll into a ball when touched. Explain how this behaviour could have become common through natural selection.

Show answer

A mutation produced an allele causing rolling-up behaviour, giving variation.

Woodlice that roll up expose only their hard shell, so they are less likely to be eaten and more likely to survive. They reproduce and pass on the allele. Over many generations the allele becomes more common, so more of the population rolls up.

U2

Why do doctors tell patients to finish a course of antibiotics?

Show answer

Stopping early kills only the most susceptible bacteria, leaving the more resistant ones alive. They reproduce and pass on the alleles for resistance.

Finishing the course kills as many bacteria as possible, so fewer resistant ones survive to multiply.

04Exam drill

D1

Explain how a population of bacteria becomes resistant to an antibiotic. (4)

Show answer

A mutation makes some bacteria resistant 1; the antibiotic kills the non-resistant bacteria 1; the resistant bacteria survive and reproduce 1; passing on the allele for resistance 1.

D2

Describe how a farmer could use selective breeding to produce cows that give more milk. (3)

Show answer

Choose cows that give the most milk and breed them 1; select the offspring that give the most milk 1; breed those and repeat over many generations 1.

D3

Give one disadvantage of selective breeding. (2)

Show answer

Inbreeding reduces the variety of alleles 1; so the population is more vulnerable to disease or genetic disorders 1.

D4

Name the three sources of genetic variation between individuals. (3)

Show answer

Mutation 1; meiosis 1; random fertilisation 1.
