02How it actually works

A small topic that pairs up with Forces at the back of the paper. Two things in it are marked far more strictly than anything else in the course — the wording of the efficiency definition, and Sankey diagrams — and both are flagged below. This paper uses g = 10 N/kg. Every heading says where it was asked.

Stores and transfers — two different lists

Spec 4.2 · 2024 Q1(a) · 2025 Q10(a) · Mock 2 Q1(a)(b)

Energy is not "types" any more. It sits in stores, and it moves between them by transfers. Questions ask for one or the other and the two lists are not interchangeable.

The eight storesThe four transfers
chemical · kinetic · gravitational · elastic · thermal · magnetic · electrostatic · nuclearmechanically (by a force doing work) · electrically · by heating · by radiation (light and sound)

So a battery-powered drill: the chemical store in the battery decreases, energy is transferred electrically to the motor, and then mechanically from the drill bit to the wood.

The giveaway in the question wording: "state the energy store" wants a word from the left column; "state the method by which energy is transferred" wants one from the right. Answering "heat energy" to either is wrong twice over — the store is thermal, and the transfer is by heating.

Note that stretching something fills the elastic store, and the transfer into it is mechanical, done by whoever pulled it. 2025 asked exactly that about a stretched rubber band, including who supplied the energy — the answer being the person.

Conservation of energy

Spec 4.3 · 2023 Q6(a)

Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one store to another.

Which raises the obvious question: if energy is always conserved, what does "wasted energy" mean? It means energy transferred to a store where it is not useful — almost always the thermal store of the surroundings, spread out so thinly that it cannot be gathered up and used again.

So nothing is ever lost in the sense of ceasing to exist. It is dissipated — scattered into the surroundings. That word is worth using, because it is precise about what actually happened.

Work done, and why it equals energy transferred

Spec 4.11, 4.12 · 2023 Q6(c)

W = F × d
W = work done in joules (J) · F = force in newtons (N) · d = distance moved in the direction of the force, in metres (m)

Work done is equal to the energy transferred. They are the same quantity in joules — "work done" simply describes energy being moved by a force.

The phrase that matters is "in the direction of the force". Carrying a suitcase along a level corridor does no work on the suitcase, however tiring it feels: the force you apply is upwards and the movement is horizontal, so the distance in the direction of the force is zero.

2023 ran this backwards — given the energy transferred and the distance, find the force: F = W ÷ d = 45 ÷ 7.5 = 6.0 N.

Gravitational potential and kinetic energy

Spec 4.13, 4.14

GPE = m × g × h
GPE = gravitational potential energy in joules (J) · m = mass in kilograms (kg) · g = gravitational field strength in N/kg (use 10) · h = height in metres (m)

KE = ½ × m × v²
KE = kinetic energy in joules (J) · m = mass in kilograms (kg) · v = velocity in metres/second (m/s)

The velocity is squared, and the ½ applies to everything. On a calculator, work out v² first, then multiply by the mass, then halve — doing it left to right without brackets is a standard way to get it wrong.

The squaring has a real consequence worth knowing: doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy. That is why braking distance grows so sharply with speed — the brakes must remove four times as much energy, over four times the distance.

Converting between GPE and KE

Spec 4.15 · Mock 1 Q10(d)

Conservation of energy links the two. For something falling with no air resistance, the GPE lost equals the KE gained:

m × g × h = ½ × m × v²

The mass appears on both sides and cancels — which is the physics behind the famous result that, without air resistance, everything falls at the same rate regardless of mass. A feather and a hammer land together on the Moon.

Worked example

A 0.20 kg ball is dropped from 5.0 m. Find its speed on landing, ignoring air resistance.

1. GPE lost: 0.20 × 10 × 5.0 = 10 J

2. This becomes KE: 10 = ½ × 0.20 × v², so v² = 10 ÷ 0.10 = 100

3. Speed: v = 10 m/s

Don't forget the final square root — stopping at v² = 100 and writing 100 m/s is a common slip.

When air resistance is not ignored, some of the GPE is dissipated to the thermal store of the air instead, so the KE gained is less and the ball lands more slowly. In the extreme case of terminal velocity, the speed is constant so the KE does not change at all, and every joule of GPE lost is dissipated to the surrounding air.

Efficiency — and two words that are worth a mark

Spec 4.4 · 2023 Q6(b) · Mock 2 Q1(c)

efficiency = (useful energy output ÷ total energy input) × 100%

Both mark schemes refuse the shortened version. 2023: "useful energy / total energy without reference to output not allowed." Mock 2: "'useful energy ÷ total energy' without the words output and input was explicitly refused by the department."

Write useful energy output and total energy input. It is one mark, every time it is asked, for four extra words.

Efficiency is always less than 100% because some energy is always dissipated, usually as thermal energy through friction. Two useful rearrangements:

  • Useful output = efficiency × total input — 2023 gave 15% and 165 J, so useful = 0.15 × 165 = 25 J
  • Wasted energy = total input − useful output — so 165 − 25 = 140 J

Watch whether the answer is wanted as a decimal or a percentage. 1200 ÷ 4000 = 0.30, which is 30%.

Sankey diagrams — marked strictly

Spec 4.5 · 2023 Q6(b)(iii) · Mock 2 Q1(c)(iii) — the most detailed examiner complaint in the pack

A Sankey diagram shows energy flowing through a device, with the width of each arrow proportional to the amount of energy. Input enters from the left, useful output continues straight on, and waste branches away.

The five things being marked

  1. An input arrow. No input arrow means no mark at all — the single most common failure.
  2. Joined up. One connected diagram, not separate floating arrows.
  3. Recognisably arrows, with arrowheads.
  4. Every arrow labelled with its store and its energy value — "chemical, 4000 J", "kinetic, 1200 J", "thermal, 2800 J".
  5. Widths to scale, and state the scale. If waste is 2800 J and useful is 1200 J, the waste arrow must be visibly wider — a bit over twice.

The 2023 report on this one question:

"Needed to have joined up diagram (eg no mark if no input line), needs to be easily identifiable as arrows… 'transferred to surroundings' not accepted, needed reference to thermal/heat. Lots of students only marked output arrows and not input arrow so lost marks… scale marked strictly. Many students used same width for input energy and wasted energy."

Three specific things to do: draw the input arrow first; label waste as thermal or heat, never "transferred to the surroundings"; and check the widths against each other before moving on. The input arrow must be the widest — it equals the two outputs added together.

Power

Spec 4.16, 4.17 · 2024 Q1(b)

P = W ÷ t
P = power in watts (W) · W = work done or energy transferred in joules (J) · t = time in seconds (s)

Power is the rate of transfer of energy, or the rate of doing work. One watt is one joule per second. It is not how much energy — it is how fast.

So two cranes lifting the same load to the same height do the same work, but the faster one has the greater power.

2024 gave three marks purely for unit handling: recognising that power is a rate, that 2.2 kW = 2200 W, and that 1 hour = 3600 s. Free marks, and only free if the conversions are automatic.

Generating electricity

Spec 4.18P, 4.19P

Each method is a chain of stores, and describing that chain is what earns the marks:

MethodEnergy transfer chain
Fossil fuelsChemical → thermal (burning) → kinetic (steam turns a turbine) → electrical
NuclearNuclear → thermal (fission) → kinetic → electrical
WindKinetic of the air → kinetic of the turbine → electrical
Water (hydroelectric)Gravitational → kinetic → electrical
GeothermalThermal in the rocks → kinetic (steam) → electrical
Solar cellsRadiation (light) → electrical, directly
Solar heatingRadiation (light) → thermal in water, directly

Notice that most of them are the same middle: something makes steam or moving air or falling water, which spins a turbine, which drives a generator. Only solar cells skip that and go straight to electrical.

Advantages and disadvantages, which is the other half of the spec point:

Renewables (wind, water, solar, geothermal)Non-renewables (fossil fuels, nuclear)
ForWill not run out · no fuel cost · no carbon dioxide produced while runningReliable, available on demand · fossil fuels have low set-up cost · nuclear produces no carbon dioxide and a large output from little fuel
AgainstUnreliable — depends on wind, sunshine or rainfall · high set-up cost · visual impact and land useFossil fuels release carbon dioxide, causing climate change, and sulfur dioxide, causing acid rain · nuclear produces radioactive waste that stays dangerous for a very long time · both will eventually run out

The most useful single distinction: renewables will not run out but are unreliable; non-renewables are reliable but will. Nuclear sits awkwardly across the two — non-renewable and producing dangerous waste, but generating no carbon dioxide.

The eight stores and four transfers

StoresTransfers
chemical, kinetic, gravitational, elastic, thermal, magnetic, electrostatic, nuclearmechanically (by a force), electrically, by heating, by radiation (light and sound)

Questions ask "which store increases" and "which transfer". Keep the two lists separate. A store is where energy sits; a transfer is how it moves.

Worked example — a stretched rubber band: the elastic store increases, the transfer is mechanical, and the source is the chemical store in the person's muscles.

Conservation of energy

Energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only be transferred from one store to another.

Energy is never lost. It is dissipated — usually to the thermal store of the surroundings, where it is spread out and no longer useful. The 2023 examiner tolerated "lost" but the safe word is dissipated or transferred to the surroundings.

Efficiency

Write the equation with all four words: useful energy output over total energy input. Dropping "output" was explicitly refused.

Efficiency is always less than 100%, and has no units. If your answer exceeds 100% you have inverted the fraction. Multiply by 100 only if the question wants a percentage — read the answer line.

Sankey diagrams — the rules that were marked strictly

  1. Draw arrows, not boxes. They must be identifiable as arrows.
  2. Include the input arrow on the left. Omitting it lost a mark on its own.
  3. Join everything up. The output arrows must physically branch from the input arrow, not float nearby.
  4. Width is proportional to energy. Choose a scale, e.g. 1 cm = 100 J, and use it for every arrow. The examiner measured widths.
  5. Useful output goes horizontally; wasted energy branches away, usually downwards.
  6. Label every arrow with the energy type and the value in joules. Wasted energy must be labelled thermal or heat — "transferred to surroundings" alone was rejected.
  7. Total width in = total width out. That's conservation of energy, drawn.

The most common failure was drawing the input arrow and the wasted arrow the same width. If 500 J goes in and 100 J is wasted, the wasted arrow must be one fifth the width of the input.

Power

Power is the rate of energy transfer, or the rate of doing work. One watt is one joule per second.

Note the multiple choice in 2024: power is the rate of energy transferred, not the amount. That single word is the whole question.

GPE, KE and the standard conversion

When an object falls, GPE converts to KE. If air resistance is negligible, the KE gained equals the GPE lost:

m × g × h = ½ × m × v²m = mass (kg) · g = gravitational field strength (N/kg) · h = height fallen (m) · v = speed at the bottom (m/s) — the mass cancels, so the speed does not depend on it

Mass cancels, which is why all objects fall at the same rate in a vacuum. Rearranged: v = √(2gh).

If air resistance is not negligible, KE at the bottom is less than GPE at the top, and the difference has been dissipated to the thermal store of the surroundings by friction.

04Understanding it

U1

A car brakes to a stop. Its kinetic energy was 200 kJ. Where did it go, and does this violate conservation of energy?

Show answer

Nothing is violated. The 200 kJ was transferred to the thermal store of the brakes, tyres, road and surrounding air, by friction — a mechanical transfer that ends up heating things. The brake discs get genuinely hot; on a long descent they can glow.

The energy still exists. What's changed is that it is now dissipated — spread thinly across a huge number of particles in the surroundings, at a temperature barely above ambient. You cannot practically gather it back up and use it to move the car again.

Why this matters for the exam: "energy is lost" invites the follow-up "lost where?". Say dissipated to the thermal store of the surroundings and you've answered both.

Regenerative braking in electric cars exists precisely to intercept some of this energy before it dissipates, transferring it to a chemical store in the battery instead.

U2

Doubling a car's speed doubles its momentum but quadruples its kinetic energy. Using the KE equation, explain why — and connect it to braking distance.

Show answer

KE = ½mv². Velocity is squared, so doubling v multiplies KE by 2² = 4. At 20 m/s a car has four times the kinetic energy it had at 10 m/s.

The braking connection: to stop, all that kinetic energy must be transferred away by the braking force doing work. Work done = force × distance. If the braking force is roughly constant (limited by tyre grip), then four times the energy requires four times the distance.

This is the same result you get from v² = u² + 2as in the forces module — arrived at from energy instead. Both routes give braking distance ∝ speed².

Being able to reach the same answer two ways is a good check under exam pressure.

U3

A car engine is about 25% efficient. A kettle is about 95%. Why can't we simply build a better engine and reach 95% too?

Show answer

Because the two are asking for different things.

A kettle's useful output is thermal energy — heating water. Nearly all the waste is also heat, and much of that ends up in the water anyway. It's easy to be efficient when the thing you want is the thing everything degrades into.

A car engine's useful output is kinetic energy, and it gets there by burning fuel to make heat first. Converting heat into motion is fundamentally limited: a large fraction must be rejected as waste heat through the exhaust and radiator. No amount of engineering removes that.

The general principle: energy tends to spread out into thermal stores. Devices whose useful output is thermal can be highly efficient. Devices trying to convert thermal energy into anything else are always poor.

An electric motor sidesteps this — around 90% efficient — because it goes from electrical to kinetic without a heat stage. That's a large part of why electric cars are more efficient than petrol ones.

U4

You carry a heavy suitcase 100 m along a level corridor at constant speed. In physics terms, how much work have you done on the suitcase? Does that match how it feels?

Show answer

Zero. Work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force. The force you exert on the suitcase is upwards, supporting its weight. The motion is horizontal. There is no movement in the direction of the force, so no work is done on the suitcase.

Consistent with energy: the suitcase's stores are unchanged. Same height, so same GPE. Constant speed, so same KE.

Why it feels exhausting: your muscles do work internally maintaining tension, which is a real biological cost, and you do work against friction and air resistance moving your own body. But none of that is work done on the suitcase.

Exam relevance: W = F × d always means the distance moved in the direction of the force. If a question gives a force at an angle to the motion, that qualifier is doing work.

U5

Two mark schemes explicitly refuse "efficiency = useful energy ÷ total energy". The formula is arithmetically identical to the accepted one. Why is it rejected?

Show answer

Because without the words output and input, the statement is ambiguous about which energies are being compared.

A device has energy going in and energy coming out. "Useful energy" and "total energy" do not say which side of the device each one is on — and the whole idea of efficiency is the comparison across the device: what you got out that was useful, against what you put in altogether.

What scores: efficiency = (useful energy output ÷ total energy input) × 100

2023: "useful energy / total energy without reference to output not allowed." Mock 2: "without the words output and input was explicitly refused by the department."

This is worth treating as a free mark you must not drop. It is asked almost every year, it is one line, and the only difference between full marks and zero is four words.

U6

If energy cannot be created or destroyed, what does it mean to say a device "wastes" energy? And why can't the wasted energy simply be collected and reused?

Show answer

Wasted energy is energy transferred to a store where it is not useful — almost always the thermal store of the surroundings. Not a single joule has been destroyed; it has just ended up somewhere useless.

Why it cannot be recovered: it has been dissipated — spread out thinly across a huge amount of surrounding air, machinery and ground. A car engine's wasted energy raises the temperature of the whole street by an unmeasurably small amount. There is no way to gather it back up, because it is no longer concentrated anywhere.

That is the real distinction: not between energy that exists and energy that doesn't, but between energy that is concentrated and useful, and energy that is spread out and useless.

Use the word "dissipated" rather than "lost". Mark schemes accept it and it describes what actually happened. Saying energy is "lost" invites the follow-up question about conservation.

U7

A skydiver at terminal velocity falls 40 m at a constant speed. Her gravitational potential energy is clearly decreasing. Where is it going, given that her kinetic energy is not changing?

Show answer

All of it is dissipated to the thermal store of the surrounding air, by the work done against air resistance.

The reasoning in three steps:

  1. She is losing height, so her GPE decreases.
  2. Her speed is constant, so her KE is unchanged — this is the step people miss, because the usual falling-object story has GPE turning into KE.
  3. Energy is conserved, so the GPE lost must have gone somewhere else. The only thing acting is air resistance, so it has been transferred to the thermal store of the air (and slightly to her suit).

Why terminal velocity is the clean case: during the earlier part of the fall she was speeding up, so the GPE lost was split — some into KE, some dissipated. Once at terminal velocity the split stops and every joule of GPE lost goes to the air.

Note this is an energy question. The companion question about how she reaches terminal velocity is a forces question, and answers to one score nothing on the other.

U8

In the equation for gravitational potential energy turning into kinetic energy, the mass cancels from both sides. What does that tell you physically, and why does the real world not seem to agree?

Show answer

Setting GPE lost equal to KE gained gives m × g × h = ½ × m × v². The mass appears on both sides and cancels, leaving g × h = ½v².

Physically: the speed an object reaches after falling a given height does not depend on its mass at all. A cannonball and a marble dropped from the same window would land at the same instant, at the same speed.

Why the world seems to disagree: because of air resistance, which the equation ignores. A feather has a large surface area for its tiny weight, so air resistance balances its weight almost immediately and it reaches a very low terminal velocity. A cannonball's weight is enormous compared with the drag on it, so it barely notices.

The difference we observe is therefore not about mass affecting gravity — it is about how quickly air resistance becomes significant compared with the object's weight.

The confirming experiment: the same objects dropped in a vacuum tube, or on the Moon, land together. Apollo 15's commander did exactly that with a hammer and a feather.

(a) Calculate the gravitational potential energy lost. (2)
(b) Calculate the speed of the ball as it lands. (4)
(c) In practice the ball lands more slowly than this. Explain why. (2)

Show answer

(a) GPE = m × g × h = 0.40 × 10 × 8.0 1 = 32 J 1

(b) All the GPE is transferred to the kinetic store, so KE = 32 J 1

KE = ½ × m × v², so 32 = ½ × 0.40 × v² 1

v² = 32 ÷ 0.20 = 160 1

v = 13 m/s (2 s.f.) 1

(c) Some of the gravitational potential energy is transferred to the thermal store of the surrounding air by air resistance 1

so less is available to the kinetic store, and the ball's landing speed is lower 1

Do not stop at v² = 160. The square root is the last step, and forgetting it gives an answer eight times too large.

D12

A car of mass 1200 kg is travelling at 15 m/s.

(a) Calculate its kinetic energy. (3)
(b) The car's speed doubles to 30 m/s. Calculate its new kinetic energy, and state the factor by which it has increased. (3)
(c) Explain what this means for the braking distance of the car. (2)

Show answer

(a) KE = ½ × m × v² 1

= ½ × 1200 × 15² = ½ × 1200 × 225 1 = 135 000 J 1

(b) = ½ × 1200 × 30² = ½ × 1200 × 900 1 = 540 000 J 1

The kinetic energy has increased by a factor of 4 1

(c) The brakes must transfer away four times as much energy to bring the car to rest 1

Since the braking force is roughly the same and work done = force × distance, the braking distance is about four times greater 1

This is why speed limits matter so much more than they appear to: the energy to be removed grows with the square of the speed, not in proportion to it.

D13

Describe the energy transfers involved in generating electricity using (a) a coal-fired power station and (b) a wind turbine. (5)

Show answer

(a) The chemical store in the coal decreases as it is burned, transferring energy to the thermal store 1

This heats water to make steam, which turns a turbine — a transfer to the kinetic store 1

The turbine drives a generator, transferring energy electrically 1

(b) The kinetic store of the moving air is transferred to the kinetic store of the turbine blades 1

which drive a generator, transferring energy electrically 1

Most methods share the same middle: something spins a turbine, which drives a generator. Only solar cells skip it, going from radiation straight to electrical.

D14

Give two advantages and two disadvantages of generating electricity from wind rather than from fossil fuels. (4)

Show answer

Advantages, any two 11: wind is renewable and will not run out · no carbon dioxide is produced while generating, so it does not contribute to climate change · no sulfur dioxide, so no acid rain · no fuel cost

Disadvantages, any two 11: unreliable, since it only generates when the wind blows · high initial set-up cost · visual impact on the landscape and noise · a large number of turbines is needed to match one fossil-fuel station

The distinction to carry into any question of this type: renewables will not run out but are unreliable; non-renewables are reliable but will run out and cause pollution.

D15

A student carries a 12 kg suitcase 60 m along a level corridor at a constant speed, then lifts it 1.5 m onto a rack. Take g = 10 N/kg.

(a) Calculate the work done on the suitcase while carrying it along the corridor. (2)
(b) Calculate the work done lifting it onto the rack. (3)

Show answer

(a) Zero 1

Work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force. The supporting force is vertical while the motion is horizontal, so the distance in the direction of the force is zero 1

(b) The force needed equals the weight: W = m × g = 12 × 10 = 120 N 1

Work done = F × d = 120 × 1.5 1 = 180 J 1

Part (a) feels wrong because carrying a suitcase is tiring — but that energy goes into your muscles holding themselves tense, not into the suitcase. The physics definition is about the object.

Note that (b) is the same calculation as GPE = mgh: 12 × 10 × 1.5 = 180 J. Work done against gravity and gain in gravitational potential energy are the same quantity.
