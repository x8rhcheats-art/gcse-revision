01What actually comes up

This topic appeared in five of the ten real Edexcel papers checked, for 31 marks in total. It comes as one self-contained question of 5 to 8 marks, and the same three things keep returning: measure an angle with a protractor, state and use n = sin i ÷ sin r, and explain a path using refraction or total internal reflection.

PaperWhat was asked
2024 Edexcel 1P Q8 (6)Two rays hit an air/water boundary (n = 1.33). Explain both paths, with measured angles of incidence and calculated angles of refraction, and why there is no total internal reflection
2023 Edexcel 2P Q7(a) (6)Light entering a glass prism: name the protractor, measure i and r, state n = sin i ÷ sin r, calculate the refractive index (1.5)
2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q6 (6)Optical fibres: what waves transfer, draw the incident ray in air, name refraction, explain the path along the fibre (total internal reflection, denser core, angle above the critical angle)
2022 Edexcel Jan 1P Q4 (5)Plane mirror practical: name the protractor, label and measure the angle of incidence (79°), draw the reflected ray
2022 Edexcel Jan 1P Q10 (8)Violet light entering a prism: find r from an angle measured to the surface, state the formula, refractive index to 2 s.f., then deduce how refractive index depends on wavelength from red and violet rays

Two patterns are worth noticing. "State the formula linking refractive index, angle of incidence and angle of refraction" has been a free mark twice. And every one of these questions needs the normal: the angles are measured from it, and a diagram angle measured from the surface has to be taken away from 90° first.

On the formula sheet: the 2022–2024 June papers printed both refraction equations on their formula page. The current specification (Issue 4, 2024) lists them among the relationships that will not be provided. For 2027, learn both by heart.

On 2023 2P Q7, the mark scheme says: "reject division of i and r giving approx 1.5-1.6". Dividing the angles themselves (44 ÷ 28 = 1.57) looks right and scores nothing. You must take the sine of each angle first: sin 44° ÷ sin 28° = 1.48, which rounds to 1.5.

And on 2024 1P Q8, where some candidates worked out a critical angle for rays going from air into water: "ignore calculated values of the critical angle as not relevant". Total internal reflection cannot happen when light enters the denser material, so the critical angle has nothing to do with it.

02How it actually works

Module 04 introduced reflection and refraction as things all waves do. This module does it properly for light: the angles, the refractive index, the critical angle and total internal reflection. Every heading says where it was actually asked. All angles in this topic are measured from the normal, never from the surface.

Light is a transverse wave that can be reflected and refracted

Spec 3.14 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q6(a)

Light is a transverse wave, and like every wave it can be reflected and refracted. Transverse means the vibrations are at right angles to the direction the wave travels. That was module 04; here it is the starting point.

Light is part of the electromagnetic spectrum, so it travels at 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum (and almost exactly that in air). It slows down when it enters glass, water or Perspex. That change of speed is what makes it bend.

2023 Jan 1P Q6(a) opened with a free mark: optical fibres use light to communicate, because waves transfer energy and information without transferring matter.

The normal, and how to measure an angle

Spec 3.15, 3.16 · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1P Q4(a), (b) · 2023 Edexcel 2P Q7(a)(i), (ii) · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1P Q10(a)(i)

The normal is a line drawn at 90° to the surface at the point where the ray hits it, and every angle is measured from the normal. Draw it as a dashed line with a ruler.

  • The angle of incidence, i, is between the incident ray and the normal.
  • The angle of reflection is between the reflected ray and the normal.
  • The angle of refraction, r, is between the refracted ray and the normal.

The instrument that measures angles is a protractor. It has been a 1-mark question twice (2022 Jan 1P Q4(a), 2023 2P Q7(a)(i)).

The angle-to-the-surface trap. 2022 Jan 1P Q10 marked the refracted ray at 54° to the surface of the prism. The angle of refraction is 90° − 54° = 36°. Using 54° gives the wrong refractive index.

✗ "The angle of incidence is the angle between the ray and the mirror."
✓ "The angle of incidence is the angle between the incident ray and the normal."

When you measure, put the protractor's centre exactly on the point where the ray meets the surface, and line its zero up with the normal. The 2023 mark scheme allowed about ±2° either side, so careful measuring is part of the mark.

Reflection and the plane mirror

Spec 3.15, 3.16 · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1P Q4

The law of reflection: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. Both are measured from the normal, and the reflected ray is on the other side of the normal from the incident ray.

To draw the reflected ray, as 2022 Jan 1P Q4(b)(iii) asked for 2 marks:

  1. Draw the normal at the point where the ray hits the mirror.
  2. Measure the angle of incidence (79° on that paper).
  3. On the other side of the normal, starting from the same point, draw a straight ray at the same angle to the normal. Add an arrow pointing away from the mirror.

The two marks were for a straight ray on the correct side of the normal coming from the point of incidence, and for the correct angle.

The mirror practical

Put the plane mirror on paper and draw along its back. Draw a normal. Shine a narrow ray from a ray box at the point where the normal meets the mirror. Mark the incident and reflected rays with two crosses each, then join the crosses with a ruler. Measure both angles from the normal with a protractor. Repeat for several angles of incidence. Each time, angle of incidence = angle of reflection.

Refraction: which way does the ray bend?

Spec 3.14, 3.16 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q6(b) · 2024 Edexcel 1P Q8

Refraction is the change in direction of light as it crosses from one material into another, because its speed changes.

A material in which light travels more slowly is called optically denser. Glass and water are optically denser than air. The 2024 mark scheme gave a mark for "water and air have different (optical) densities" and another for "light travels slower in water than air".

2023 Jan 1P Q6(b) showed the refracted ray inside the glass and asked for the ray in air before it hit the boundary. The mark needed the ray in air to be further from the normal than the ray in the glass, because the ray bends towards the normal as it goes in.

2023 Jan 1P Q6(b)(ii) asked for the name of the behaviour. The mark scheme accepted any recognisable spelling of refraction but said "reject if spelling implies reflection". Write it clearly.

✗ "The light bends because the glass is thicker."
✓ "The light slows down as it enters the glass, so it changes direction towards the normal."

Ray diagrams through blocks

Spec 3.16, 3.17

A ray through a rectangular glass block bends towards the normal going in, away from the normal coming out, and leaves parallel to the ray that went in.

  1. At the first face: air to glass, so the ray bends towards the normal.
  2. Inside the block: a straight line to the opposite face.
  3. At the second face: glass to air, so it bends away from the normal by the same amount.
  4. Because the two faces are parallel, the emerging ray is parallel to the incident ray, just shifted sideways.

Rules for drawing that earn the marks: use a ruler, draw a normal at every point where the ray meets a surface, put arrows on the rays, and make the bend go the right way at each face. Some light is also reflected at each surface. Examiners do not need it unless they ask; the 2024 scheme said "ignore reflected rays".

Refractive index: n = sin i ÷ sin r

Spec 3.18 · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1P Q10(a)(ii), (iii) · 2023 Edexcel 2P Q7(a)(iii), (iv) · 2024 Edexcel 1P Q8

The refractive index of a material tells you how much it bends light: n = sin i ÷ sin r.

n = sin i ÷ sin r
n = refractive index (no unit) · i = angle of incidence in air (°) · r = angle of refraction in the material (°)

Three facts about n. It has no unit, because it is one sine divided by another. It is always greater than 1 for glass, water or Perspex, because light slows down and bends towards the normal, so sin i is bigger than sin r. And a bigger n means the light bends more. Typical values: water 1.33, glass about 1.5.

Mark script: calculate the refractive index

From 2022 Jan 1P Q10, three marks: substitution, evaluation, answer to 2 s.f.

i = 61°, and the refracted ray was at 54° to the surface, so r = 90 − 54 = 36°.

n = sin 61° ÷ sin 36° = 0.875 ÷ 0.588 = 1.488… = 1.5 (2 s.f.)

Using it the other way round (2024 1P Q8): water has n = 1.33 and a ray hits it at i = 36°. Then sin r = sin 36° ÷ 1.33 = 0.442, so r = 26°. The scheme accepted 24°–28°.

✗ n = 44 ÷ 28 = 1.57  — "reject division of i and r giving approx 1.5-1.6"
✓ n = sin 44° ÷ sin 28° = 0.695 ÷ 0.469 = 1.48 = 1.5

Check your calculator is in degrees (a small D on the screen). In radians, sin 44 gives 0.018 and everything after it is wrong.

If the light is coming out of the glass, i in the equation is still the angle in air and r is still the angle in the glass. The angle in air is always the bigger one.

Practical: the refractive index of a glass block

Spec 3.17, 3.19

Trace rays through a rectangular glass block, measure i and r from the normal, and calculate n = sin i ÷ sin r for several angles.

  1. Put the block on a sheet of paper and draw around it with a sharp pencil.
  2. Draw a normal at a point on one long side.
  3. Use a ray box with a narrow slit to aim a ray at that point, at a measured angle of incidence, e.g. 20°.
  4. Mark the ray that comes out of the far side with two crosses, well apart.
  5. Remove the block. Join the crosses and extend back to the block's outline. Join the point where the ray went in to the point where it came out. That line is the refracted ray inside the glass.
  6. Measure the angle of refraction, r, from the normal with a protractor.
  7. Repeat for a range of angles of incidence, e.g. 20°, 30°, 40°, 50°, 60°.
  8. Calculate sin i ÷ sin r for each and take the mean. Or plot sin i against sin r: a straight line through the origin whose gradient is n.

Why these details matter: a narrow ray and crosses far apart make the line more accurate. A darkened room makes the ray easier to see. Several angles and a mean reduce the effect of small measuring errors. A typical result for glass is about 1.5.

Prisms, semi-circular blocks, and colours

Spec 3.17 · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1P Q10(b) · 2023 Edexcel 2P Q7(a)

A triangular prism bends light at both faces it passes through, and because the faces are not parallel the ray comes out in a new direction. The rule at each face is the same as for the block: towards the normal going in, away from the normal coming out.

A semi-circular block is used to study light leaving glass. Aim the ray at the centre of the flat face, through the curved side. The ray then enters the curved side along a radius, which is along the normal, so it does not bend going in. All the bending happens at the flat face, where you can measure it. This is how you find the critical angle (next section).

2022 Jan 1P Q10(b) shows why prisms make rainbows. White light is a mixture of colours. Glass has a slightly different refractive index for each colour, so each colour bends by a different amount and the colours spread out. The mark scheme wanted three linked points:

  1. Red refracts less than violet.
  2. So red has a lower refractive index than violet.
  3. Red has a longer wavelength, so refractive index decreases as wavelength increases.

The critical angle

Spec 3.21 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q6(c)

The critical angle is the angle of incidence, inside the denser material, at which the angle of refraction is 90°. At that angle the refracted ray skims along the boundary.

What you see with a semi-circular block as you increase the angle of incidence at the flat face:

  1. Small angles: most light refracts out into the air, bending away from the normal. A weak ray is also reflected back inside.
  2. Increasing the angle: the refracted ray bends further from the normal and gets dimmer; the reflected ray gets brighter.
  3. At the critical angle: the refracted ray travels along the boundary, at 90° to the normal.
  4. Above the critical angle: no light leaves. All of it is reflected back inside. This is total internal reflection.

Critical angles: about 42° for glass (n = 1.5) and about 49° for water (n = 1.33). A higher refractive index gives a smaller critical angle.

Total internal reflection: the two conditions

Spec 3.20, 3.21 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q6(c) · 2024 Edexcel 1P Q8

Total internal reflection happens only when both conditions are met: light is going from a denser material into a less dense one, and the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle.

  1. The light is travelling from a more optically dense material to a less dense one (higher to lower refractive index), e.g. glass to air or water to air.
  2. The angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle.

Then all of the light reflects back inside, obeying the law of reflection.

Mark script: "explain the paths of the rays" (2024 1P Q8, 6 marks)

Two rays from a lamp in air hit a water surface (n = 1.33). The six marks, any six from seven:

  • Ray A is refracted and changes direction.
  • Ray B is refracted and changes direction.
  • A correctly measured angle of incidence: about 36° for A, 60° for B.
  • A correctly calculated angle of refraction: sin 36° ÷ 1.33 gives 26°; sin 60° ÷ 1.33 gives 41°.
  • Water and air have different optical densities.
  • Light travels slower in water than in air.
  • No total internal reflection, because the light is going into the denser material.

Draw both refracted rays, bending towards the normal, on the diagram. The question said "include calculations"; do them for both rays.

✗ "The ray is totally internally reflected because the angle is big."
✓ "The ray is totally internally reflected because it is going from glass into air and the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle."

Light going from air into water or glass can never be totally internally reflected, however steep the angle. The scheme said to "ignore calculated values of the critical angle as not relevant" in that situation.

Critical angle and refractive index: sin c = 1 ÷ n

Spec 3.22

The critical angle depends only on the refractive index: sin c = 1 ÷ n.

sin c = 1 ÷ n
c = critical angle (°), measured from the normal inside the denser material · n = refractive index of that material (no unit)

Mark script: both directions

Find c from n. Glass, n = 1.50: sin c = 1 ÷ 1.50 = 0.667. c = sin⁻¹ 0.667 = 41.8° = 42°.

Find n from c. A student measures c = 43° with a semi-circular block: n = 1 ÷ sin 43° = 1 ÷ 0.682 = 1.466 = 1.47.

Show three steps: the equation, the substitution, the answer. The last step needs the sin⁻¹ (shift sin) button.

✗ sin c = 1 ÷ 1.50 = 0.667, so c = 0.667°
✓ sin c = 0.667, so c = sin⁻¹ 0.667 = 42°

The number 0.667 is the sine of the angle, not the angle. And a critical angle must be less than 90°; if you get something bigger, you have divided the wrong way round.

Optical fibres and prisms that reflect

Spec 3.20 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q6

An optical fibre carries light along its length by total internal reflection, many times over.

  1. Light enters one end of the thin glass fibre, refracting as it goes in.
  2. It hits the side of the fibre at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle.
  3. The glass core is optically denser than what surrounds it, so the light is totally internally reflected.
  4. This repeats all along the fibre, even round gentle bends, so the light reaches the far end with very little lost.

The 3-mark answer to 2023 Jan 1P Q6(c) was exactly those three words: total internal reflection; because the core has a higher refractive index than air; because the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle. Uses: sending information (telephone and internet signals) as pulses of light, and endoscopes that let doctors see inside the body.

Prisms use the same effect. In a glass prism with angles of 45°, 45° and 90°, light enters one short face along the normal, so it does not bend. It then meets the long face at 45°. That is greater than the critical angle of glass (42°), so the light is totally internally reflected. The ray turns through 90°, as in a periscope, or through 180° using two reflections, as in binoculars and bicycle reflectors. Almost all the light is reflected, which is better than a mirror.

✗ "The light bounces off the sides of the fibre."
✓ "The light is totally internally reflected at the sides of the fibre, because the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle."

"Bounces" and "reflects" earn nothing on their own. You need the full name and the reason.

Sound waves reflect and refract too

Spec 3.23

Sound waves are longitudinal, and they can also be reflected and refracted. Module 04 covers sound in detail. Only three points belong here.

  • Sound is longitudinal: the vibrations are parallel to the direction the wave travels. Light is transverse.
  • A reflected sound is an echo. Sonar and ultrasound scanning use reflected sound.
  • Sound is refracted when it changes speed as it passes into a new material. It goes the opposite way from light: sound speeds up going from air into water, so it bends away from the normal.

04Understanding it

These are the "why" questions. Try each one properly before opening the answer.

U1

A ray of light hits a glass block at an angle and bends. A second ray hits the block along the normal and goes straight on. Both rays slow down. Why does only one of them bend?

Think about Which side of a wide beam reaches the glass first?

Show answer

Picture the ray as a wide beam with a straight front. If it arrives at an angle, one side of the front enters the glass first and slows down, while the other side is still in air, moving fast. The fast side catches up, so the whole front swings round. That swing is the change of direction.

If the beam arrives along the normal, the whole front enters the glass at the same moment. It all slows together, so nothing swings round. The speed changes; the direction does not.

The point: refraction is caused by the change of speed, but you only see a change of direction when the light meets the boundary at an angle.

U2

Why does refractive index have no unit, and why is it always more than 1 for glass or water?

Show answer

No unit: n = sin i ÷ sin r. A sine is a ratio of two lengths and has no unit, so one sine divided by another has no unit either.

More than 1: light slows down in glass or water, so it bends towards the normal. That makes r smaller than i, so sin r is smaller than sin i, and sin i ÷ sin r is bigger than 1.

Use it as a check: if you calculate a refractive index of 0.67, you have divided the wrong way round. If you give it a unit, you lose the mark.

U3

A student measures i = 44° and r = 28° and writes n = 44 ÷ 28 = 1.57. The answer looks sensible for glass. Why does the mark scheme reject it?

Show answer

Because the equation uses the sines of the angles, and sine is not proportional to the angle. Doubling an angle does not double its sine.

Correctly: n = sin 44° ÷ sin 28° = 0.695 ÷ 0.469 = 1.48, which is 1.5 to 2 s.f.

The two answers are close here only by luck. Try i = 70° and r = 39°: dividing the angles gives 1.79, but sin 70° ÷ sin 39° = 1.49. The 2023 scheme said "reject division of i and r giving approx 1.5-1.6", so the examiners were ready for exactly this mistake.

U4

Why can light going from air into water never be totally internally reflected, however steep the angle?

Think about Which way does the ray bend going into water?

Show answer

Going from air into water, light slows down and bends towards the normal. So the angle of refraction is always smaller than the angle of incidence. Even at an angle of incidence of almost 90°, the refracted ray is only at about 49° to the normal.

Total internal reflection needs the refracted ray to reach 90° and have nowhere to go. That can only happen when the ray bends away from the normal, which means going from the denser material to the less dense one.

This was the seventh mark point on 2024 1P Q8: no total internal reflection, because water is more optically dense than air.

U5

Show that sin c = 1 ÷ n follows from n = sin i ÷ sin r.

Show answer

In n = sin i ÷ sin r, i is the angle in air and r is the angle in the glass, whichever way the light travels.

At the critical angle, light leaving the glass meets the boundary at c (inside the glass) and leaves at 90° in air. So the angle in air is 90° and the angle in glass is c:

n = sin 90° ÷ sin c = 1 ÷ sin c, so sin c = 1 ÷ n.

What it tells you: the bigger the refractive index, the smaller the critical angle, and the easier it is to trap light by total internal reflection.

U6

A ray passes through a rectangular glass block and comes out parallel to the ray that went in. Why parallel? And why does a triangular prism not do the same?

Show answer

In a rectangular block the two faces are parallel, so their normals are parallel too. The ray meets the second face at the same angle it was refracted to at the first face. Coming out, it bends away from the normal by exactly as much as it bent towards the normal going in. The two bends cancel, so the ray leaves in its original direction, just shifted sideways.

In a triangular prism the two faces are at an angle to each other. Their normals point in different directions, so the two bends do not cancel. Both bends turn the ray the same way, towards the thick base of the prism, so it leaves in a new direction.

U7

White light goes into a prism and comes out as a spread of colours, with violet bent the most and red the least. What does this tell you about the refractive index of glass?

Show answer

Every colour hits the prism at the same angle of incidence. Violet is bent the most, so its angle of refraction is the smallest, and from n = sin i ÷ sin r its refractive index is the largest. Red is bent the least, so it has the smallest refractive index.

Red has a longer wavelength than violet. So for glass, refractive index decreases as wavelength increases.

That is exactly the 3-mark chain on 2022 Jan 1P Q10(b): red refracts less than violet; so red has a lower refractive index; so refractive index decreases with increasing wavelength.

U8

An optical fibre still carries light round a gentle curve. Explain why, and explain what could happen if it were bent very sharply.

Show answer

Round a gentle curve, the light still meets the side of the fibre at a large angle to the normal, greater than the critical angle. So it is still totally internally reflected, and it follows the fibre round.

At a very sharp bend, the side of the fibre turns towards the light. The light then meets the side more nearly along the normal, so its angle of incidence gets smaller. If it falls below the critical angle, the light refracts out through the side and escapes, and the signal is weakened.

The point: total internal reflection is not a property of the fibre alone. It depends on the angle every time the light meets the boundary.

D4

This question is about optical fibres.
