01What actually comes up

Across the three past papers in the pack, this topic supplied roughly 42 of 255 marks. It never appears as one big question — it's split between a short moments calculation and a long multi-part question at the end of the paper.

PaperWhat was asked
2024 Q7 (10)Spring extension graph, moment of a force on a gate, principle of moments to find an unknown force, elastic limit

Two patterns are worth noticing. Moments turns up every year, usually early, usually with the equation asked for separately as a free mark. And the paper's biggest single question is often this topic, ending in a long "explain in terms of forces" answer worth 5 or 6 marks.

On the 2025 paper, the 5-mark forces question was where the year group haemorrhaged marks. The examiner wrote: "A number of answers focussed either partly or exclusively on energy which was ignored. The danger with introducing energy concepts here is that they become muddled with forces. Unbalanced forces cause accelerations, NOT GPE/KE."

They also noted that several students "didn't see this question, despite it saying clearly on the page before that the question continued on the next page." Fifteen marks, at the back of the paper. Turn the page.

02How it actually works

This topic is 16% of the paper but it never arrives as one big question — it is a short moments calculation early on and a long multi-part question at the back. Every heading below says where it was actually asked. Note throughout that this paper uses g = 10 N/kg, which is what the mark schemes use.

Moments — the marks that appear every single year

Spec 1.30P · 2024 Q7(b) · 2025 Q3(a) · Mock 1 Q3(a) — and the equation is asked for separately, as its own mark

A moment is the turning effect of a force about a pivot.

moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot
moment in newton metres (N m) · force in newtons (N) · distance in metres (m)

The word perpendicular is doing real work. The distance you want is measured at right angles from the pivot to the line the force acts along — not along the arm if the force is at an angle to it. This is also why a longer spanner loosens a stubborn nut without you pushing harder: you have increased d, so the moment increases.

The unit conversion is the whole trap. Distances are given in centimetres and answers are demanded in newton metres. Mock 1 gave 24 cm and wanted N m: 24 cm = 0.24 m, so 85 × 0.24 = 20.4 N m. An answer of 2040 is a unit error, not a physics error, and it costs the marks anyway.

But check which unit is actually demanded. In 2025 the answer was wanted in N cm and no conversion was needed at all — converting would have been wrong. Read the demanded unit before you convert anything.

The principle of moments

Spec 1.32P, 1.33P · 2024 Q7(c) — worth 3 marks including the statement

When something balances and does not turn:

total clockwise moment = total anticlockwise moment

That single sentence is often a mark on its own, so write it down before calculating. Then the method is always the same:

  1. Find the moment you can calculate completely — force × distance.
  2. Set it equal to the moment on the other side, written as (unknown force) × (its distance).
  3. Rearrange and divide.

In 2024: the gate's weight gave 480 × 0.84 = 403 N m, and the push acted at 3.2 m, so F = 403.2 ÷ 3.2 = 130 N. Notice the answer is smaller than the weight — acting further from the pivot means less force is needed for the same turning effect.

Related and examinable: the weight of an object acts through its centre of gravity (spec 1.31P). For a uniform beam or rule that is its midpoint, which is how you handle the beam's own weight in a balancing problem.

Vectors, scalars, and adding forces up

Spec 1.13, 1.14, 1.15 · Mock 1 Q10(b)(i)

A scalar has size only — mass, speed, distance, time, energy. A vector has size and direction — force, weight, velocity, acceleration, momentum.

That distinction is why speed and velocity are different words. Speed is how fast; velocity is how fast and which way. An object going round a bend at a steady 20 m/s has constant speed but changing velocity — and because velocity is changing, it is accelerating, even though the speedometer is still.

Because force is a vector, forces along a line combine by adding those in one direction and subtracting those in the other. The answer — the resultant force — needs a direction as well as a number. In mock 1: 750 N down minus 300 N up gives 450 N, and the mark scheme wanted "450 N downwards".

Friction always opposes motion (spec 1.16), so it points backwards along the direction of travel. Air resistance is friction with the air and behaves the same way.

Speed, velocity and acceleration

Spec 1.4, 1.6 · 2024 Q8(d) · Mock 2 Q5(b), (d)

average speed = distance moved ÷ time taken
speed in metres/second (m/s) · distance in metres (m) · time in seconds (s)

a = (v − u) ÷ t
a = acceleration in m/s² · v = final velocity (m/s) · u = initial velocity (m/s) · t = time (s)

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity — how much the velocity changes each second. So m/s² reads as "metres per second, per second". A negative acceleration means slowing down, and mark schemes accept either "deceleration of 2.4 m/s²" or "acceleration of −2.4 m/s²".

2024 ran this backwards to find a reaction time: it gave a speed and a thinking distance, and wanted the time. Same equation, rearranged to t = distance ÷ speed.

Motion graphs — the three things they ask

Spec 1.3, 1.7, 1.8, 1.9 · Mock 2 Q5 · 2024 Q8(c)

GraphGradient meansArea underneath means
Distance–timeSpeed. Steeper = faster; flat = stationaryNothing useful
Velocity–timeAcceleration. Flat = constant velocityDistance travelled

The area rule is worth understanding rather than memorising. For a flat section, distance = speed × time, which is exactly the area of that rectangle. For a sloping section the speed changes, but the same reasoning applied to thin strips gives the area of the triangle. So the area is always the distance.

The method for "determine the total distance"

Chop the graph into rectangles and triangles, work out each, and add. From mock 2:

Triangle 0–10 s: ½ × 10 × 12 = 60 m  ·  rectangle 10–25 s: 15 × 12 = 180 m  ·  triangle 25–30 s: ½ × 5 × 12 = 30 m

Total = 270 m. Then average speed for the journey = 270 ÷ 30 = 9.0 m/s.

Note that the average speed is not the average of the velocities — it is the total distance over the total time. These give different answers and only one is right.

When you are asked to plot, the marks are for labelling both axes with quantity and unit, choosing a scale that uses most of the grid, plotting every point correctly, and joining them as instructed.

v² = u² + 2as — the one for "no time given"

Spec 1.10 · 2024 Q8(e) · 2025 Q10(c)(i)

v² = u² + 2as
v = final speed (m/s) · u = initial speed (m/s) · a = acceleration (m/s²) · s = distance moved (m)

Reach for this whenever a question gives you two speeds and a distance but no time. That is its entire purpose, and spotting it is most of the difficulty.

Two situations where signs matter. Something slowing to rest has v = 0, so the equation gives a negative a — report it as a deceleration. Something thrown upwards also has v = 0 at the top, with a = −10 m/s²: 2025's rubber band gave 0 = 13² + 2 × (−10) × s, so s = 169 ÷ 20 = 8.5 m.

In 2024 candidates used the stopping distance or the thinking distance where the question wanted the braking distance. That capped them at 3 of 4 marks. Read which distance is being asked for — the graph gives you all three.

2025: "A significant number gave 2 d.p. instead of 2 s.f." — 8.45 was rejected where 8.5 was wanted. Give the number of significant figures asked for.

Weight, mass and F = ma

Spec 1.17, 1.18 · Mock 1 Q10(a), (b)(ii)

W = m × g
W = weight in newtons (N) · m = mass in kilograms (kg) · g = gravitational field strength in N/kg (use 10)

F = m × a
F = resultant force in newtons (N) · m = mass in kilograms (kg) · a = acceleration in m/s²

Mass and weight are not the same thing. Mass is the quantity of matter in kilograms and does not change wherever you take it. Weight is the force of gravity on that mass, in newtons, and changes with the gravitational field — the same astronaut has the same mass on the Moon but about a sixth of the weight.

The critical word in F = ma is resultant. It is not any old force in the problem, it is the total after everything has been combined. Mock 1's sequence is the standard one and worth learning as a unit: weight = 75 × 10 = 750 N, resultant = 750 − 300 = 450 N, acceleration = 450 ÷ 75 = 6.0 m/s².

Falling objects and terminal velocity — in terms of forces

2025 Q10(c)(ii) — 5 marks · Mock 1 Q10(c) — 4 marks. "This is where the year group haemorrhaged marks."

This is the single most valuable thing in the module, and the marks are lost on how the answer is written rather than on the physics.

The sequence, which is one mark per step:

  1. At the start, weight acts downwards and there is little or no air resistance, so there is a resultant downward force and the object accelerates.
  2. As its speed increases, the air resistance increases.
  3. So the resultant force decreases, and therefore the acceleration decreases — it is still speeding up, just less quickly.
  4. Eventually air resistance equals weight, so the resultant force is zero.
  5. With no resultant force there is no acceleration, so it falls at a constant velocity — terminal velocity.

The examiner on 2025 Q10: answers "focussed either partly or exclusively on energy which was ignored… Unbalanced forces cause accelerations, NOT GPE/KE."

If the question says "in terms of forces", every mention of kinetic or gravitational potential energy scores zero, no matter how correct it is. Mock 1 makes the point sharply by asking part (c) in terms of forces and part (d) in terms of energy — the same fall, and answers that work for one score nothing for the other. Read which one you are being asked for.

Two words to get right: say weight, not "gravity" — "gravity" was ignored. And say air resistance or drag, not "wind resistance", which was not accepted.

One more thing from that paper: "several students didn't see this question — fifteen marks, at the back of the paper." Turn the page.

Stopping distance

Spec 1.19, 1.20 · 2024 Q8(a)–(c)

stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance

Two separate phases, affected by different things — which is exactly what the multiple-choice parts test:

What it isIncreased by
Thinking distanceHow far you travel while reacting, before the brakes are touchedGreater speed · longer reaction time (tiredness, alcohol, drugs, distraction)
Braking distanceHow far you travel once the brakes are appliedGreater speed · greater mass · wet or icy road · worn brakes or tyres

The distinction that earns marks: road conditions affect only the braking distance — ice does not slow your reactions. Tiredness affects only the thinking distance — it does not change the grip of the tyres. Speed increases both.

Hooke's law and elastic behaviour

Spec 1.22, 1.23, 1.24 · 2024 Q7(a), (d) · 2025 Q10(b)

Hang increasing weights on a spring, measure the extension each time, and plot force against extension. The result starts as a straight line through the origin: extension is directly proportional to force. That initial straight part is Hooke's law.

Beyond a certain point — the elastic limit — the line bends and the material stops obeying it.

Elastic behaviour is the ability of a material to return to its original shape once the forces deforming it are removed. On a graph, that shows as the line returning to the origin when it is unloaded, which is exactly what 2025 asked for.

2024 asked what happens when a spring is stretched past its elastic limit. The mark scheme wanted the elastic limit being exceeded, and permanent deformation — it does not return to its original shape. "The spring breaks" was ignored: the point is permanent deformation, not breakage.

In part (a), "force is proportional to extension" earned both marks — describing the graph as a straight line through the origin and saying the relationship is linear are what is wanted, not a vague "it goes up".

Newton's third law

Spec 1.29P

For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. The part that is actually tested: the two forces in a third-law pair act on two different objects, and they are always the same type of force.

So a book resting on a table is not a third-law pair, even though the two forces are equal and opposite: the book's weight and the table's push both act on the book, and they are different kinds of force. They are balanced forces on one object, which is a different idea. The genuine third-law partner of the book's weight is the book's gravitational pull on the Earth.

On the 2025 elastic-behaviour question: "Must state or imply that the return to original shape is after the band has been unloaded, not just after it has been stretched."

And on the same paper: "BOD given on 'force of gravity' but weight or gravitational force should really be used. Just saying gravity is not accepted as per the MS." BOD means "benefit of the doubt" — don't rely on it.

The terminal velocity script

Learn this as a three-beat sequence. Almost every version of the question wants these beats in this order.

  1. At the start, weight is greater than air resistance, so there is a resultant force downwards and the object accelerates.
  2. As speed increases, air resistance increases. The resultant force therefore gets smaller, so the acceleration decreases.
  3. When air resistance equals weight, the resultant force is zero, so the object travels at a constant velocity — terminal velocity.

The variant that catches people

The 2025 paper asked about a rubber band falling from its maximum height and explicitly said assume it does not reach terminal velocity. So the script above doesn't apply — but the reasoning does. The answer needed: weight acts downwards, air resistance acts upwards and is smaller, so there is a resultant force downwards; the band accelerates; as it speeds up air resistance grows, so the resultant force decreases and the acceleration decreases — but it never reaches zero.

Read the assumptions in the box. They are there because they change the answer.

04Understanding it

These aren't exam questions. They're the questions worth thinking about slowly, because they attack the misconceptions that make exam questions go wrong. Try each one properly before opening the answer.

U1

A book lies at rest on a table. The table pushes up on the book with a force equal in size to the book's weight.

Is this an example of Newton's third law? Explain your reasoning.

Think about Which object is each force acting on?

Show answer

No. This catches almost everyone.

Newton's third law pairs act on different objects. The book's weight is a force from the Earth on the book; its third-law partner is the force from the book on the Earth. The table's push on the book has as its partner the book's push down on the table.

The weight and the table's push are both acting on the same object — the book. They're equal here because the book is in equilibrium (resultant force zero), not because of Newton's third law. If you put the book in a lift accelerating upwards, they'd no longer be equal — but the third-law pairs would still be equal.

The test: a third-law pair always acts on two different bodies, and is always the same type of force.

U2

A skydiver reaches terminal velocity. She then opens her parachute.

Describe what happens to her speed immediately afterwards, and explain why she reaches a new, lower terminal velocity — in terms of forces.

Think about What changed when the parachute opened? Weight, or air resistance?

Show answer

Opening the parachute massively increases the surface area, so air resistance increases sharply. Her weight is unchanged.

Air resistance is now greater than weight, so there's a resultant force upwards. She therefore decelerates — note she is still moving downwards, just more slowly. Deceleration is not the same as moving backwards.

As she slows, air resistance falls again. When air resistance once more equals weight, the resultant force is zero and she falls at a new, lower constant velocity.

Why this matters: the whole question turns on the fact that air resistance depends on speed and area, weight doesn't. If you can say that sentence, you can answer any version of this question.

U3

Why does the area under a velocity–time graph give the distance travelled? Explain it without just asserting that it does.

Think about Start with a graph that's a flat horizontal line.

Show answer

Take the simplest case first: an object moving at a constant 5 m/s for 4 s. The graph is a horizontal line at 5, and the region under it is a rectangle of height 5 and width 4. Its area is 20 — and the distance travelled is speed × time = 5 × 4 = 20 m. The area is the multiplication.

Now take a sloping line. Chop the region under it into very thin vertical strips. Over each strip the velocity is almost constant, so each strip's area is a small distance. Add all the strips and you have the total distance.

This is why the units work: the vertical axis is in m/s and the horizontal in s, so area has units of m/s × s = m.

Practical payoff: when the shape is a trapezium, you can either use the trapezium formula or split it into a rectangle plus a triangle. The second is harder to get wrong under pressure.

U4

A car doubles its speed from 15 m/s to 30 m/s.

(a) What happens to its thinking distance?
(b) What happens to its braking distance?
(c) Why are the answers different?

Think about Which equation governs each one?

Show answer

(a) Thinking distance doubles. During the reaction time the car moves at constant speed, so distance = speed × time. Reaction time is unchanged, so doubling speed doubles the distance.

(b) Braking distance quadruples. Rearranging v² = u² + 2as with final speed zero gives s = u² ÷ (2a). Distance depends on the square of the initial speed, so doubling speed multiplies the distance by four.

(c) Because thinking distance is linear in speed and braking distance is quadratic. This is exactly why the graph in the 2024 paper shows the braking distance curving upwards while the thinking distance stays a straight line — and why speeding is disproportionately dangerous.

Watch for: road conditions, brake condition and vehicle mass affect braking distance only. Tiredness, alcohol, drugs and distraction affect thinking distance only. Speed affects both.

U5

A metre rule is balanced on a pivot at its centre. A 2.0 N weight hangs 40 cm to the left of the pivot. Where must a 4.0 N weight be hung to balance it?

Then: explain why the answer doesn't depend on the weight of the rule itself.

Think about Where does the rule's own weight act?

Show answer

Anticlockwise moment = 2.0 × 40 = 80 N cm. For balance, the clockwise moment must also be 80 N cm. So 4.0 × d = 80, giving d = 20 cm to the right of the pivot.

The rule's own weight acts through its centre of gravity, which for a uniform rule is at its midpoint — which is exactly where the pivot is. Its perpendicular distance from the pivot is therefore zero, so its moment is zero. It has no turning effect.

The follow-up worth knowing: if the pivot were not at the centre, the rule's weight would produce a moment and you'd have to include it. Exam questions usually say "uniform" and pivot at the centre to avoid this — but if they don't, that's the trap.

U6

A student stretches a spring past its elastic limit and then removes the force.

(a) What does the force–extension graph look like?
(b) What happens to the spring?
(c) Is this the same as the limit of proportionality?

Show answer

(a) The graph starts as a straight line through the origin. Beyond the limit of proportionality it curves, with extension increasing more rapidly for each additional newton.

(b) The spring is permanently deformed — it does not return to its original length when unloaded. It has undergone plastic rather than elastic deformation.

(c) Not quite. The limit of proportionality is where the graph stops being a straight line — Hooke's law no longer holds. The elastic limit is where permanent deformation begins. They're close together and IGCSE often treats them loosely, but if a question asks about the graph shape the answer is proportionality; if it asks about permanent damage the answer is elastic limit.

U7

Mock 1 asks about a skydiver twice: part (c) says "explain, in terms of forces, how she reaches terminal velocity" and part (d) asks what happens to her gravitational potential energy. Why can a correct answer to one score zero on the other?

Show answer

Because the two parts are testing different models of the same event, and each mark scheme ignores the other model entirely.

"In terms of forces" wants weight, air resistance, resultant force and acceleration. Writing "she loses GPE which becomes KE" is true, but it answers a question that wasn't asked, so it scores nothing.

"What happens to her GPE" wants energy stores and transfers. Writing "air resistance balances her weight" is again true and again scores nothing.

The 2025 examiner made this exact complaint about the whole year group: answers "focussed either partly or exclusively on energy which was ignored… Unbalanced forces cause accelerations, NOT GPE/KE."

What to do: before writing, find the phrase "in terms of…" in the question and underline it. It is not decoration — it defines which vocabulary earns marks.

U8

An astronaut travels to the Moon, where the gravitational field strength is about 1.6 N/kg. What happens to her mass, and what happens to her weight? Why does the distinction matter in a calculation?

Show answer

Her mass is unchanged. Mass is the quantity of matter in her body, measured in kilograms, and travelling somewhere does not add or remove any of it.

Her weight falls to roughly a sixth. Weight is the force of gravity acting on that mass, measured in newtons, and it depends on the field she is in: W = m × g, with g dropping from 10 N/kg to 1.6 N/kg.

Why it matters in a calculation: F = m × a needs the mass in kilograms, never the weight. If a question gives you a weight in newtons and asks for an acceleration, you must divide by mass — so you may have to get the mass back out of the weight first. Substituting 750 instead of 75 is a standard way to lose every mark in a part.

Everyday speech makes this worse: bathroom scales report "weight" in kilograms, which is a mass. In physics the two words are not interchangeable.

U9

A car goes round a roundabout at a steady 20 m/s. Its speed never changes. Is it accelerating?

Show answer

Yes. This is the clearest demonstration of why speed and velocity are different words.

Speed is a scalar — size only. It is 20 m/s throughout, so the speed is constant.

Velocity is a vector — size and direction. Going round the roundabout the direction changes continuously, so the velocity changes continuously.

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, not of speed. A changing direction is a changing velocity, so the car is accelerating even though the speedometer reads the same number.

The consequence worth remembering: acceleration requires a resultant force, so there must be one — provided by friction between the tyres and the road, pointing towards the centre of the roundabout. On ice that force disappears and the car carries straight on.

U10

A student cannot loosen a nut with a spanner. Explain, using the moment equation, two different ways of getting more turning effect — and why one of them is usually easier.

Show answer

Moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot, so there are exactly two things you can change.

1. Increase the force — push harder. Often not possible; if they could push harder they already would have.

2. Increase the perpendicular distance — use a longer spanner, or slide a pipe over the handle. This is usually far easier, because doubling the length doubles the moment for exactly the same effort.

This is the standard one-mark answer: "use a longer spanner, increasing the perpendicular distance from the pivot". Naming the quantity you are increasing is what earns it — "use a bigger spanner" alone is vague.

The word doing the work is perpendicular. Pulling the spanner along its own length, straight towards the nut, has a perpendicular distance of zero and produces no turning effect at all, however hard you pull.

U11

A cyclist accelerates from rest to 12 m/s over 10 s, holds 12 m/s for 15 s, then stops in 5 s. A student calculates the average speed as (0 + 12 + 12 + 0) ÷ 4 = 6 m/s. What is wrong, and what is the right answer?

Show answer

The student has averaged the velocity readings. Average speed is not the average of the speeds — it is total distance ÷ total time, and the readings are not held for equal lengths of time.

Do it properly, from the area under the velocity–time graph:

Speeding up (triangle): ½ × 10 × 12 = 60 m  ·  steady (rectangle): 15 × 12 = 180 m  ·  stopping (triangle): ½ × 5 × 12 = 30 m

Total distance = 270 m over a total time of 30 s, so average speed = 270 ÷ 30 = 9.0 m/s.

It is higher than 6 m/s because the cyclist spent most of the journey at the top speed, and the crude average gave the brief slow parts equal weight.

U12

On a distance–time graph and on a velocity–time graph, a horizontal line means two completely different things. What are they, and how do you avoid confusing them under pressure?

Show answer

Distance–time, horizontal: the distance is not changing, so the object is stationary.

Velocity–time, horizontal: the velocity is not changing, so the object is moving at a constant velocity — which is the opposite of stopped, unless the line happens to sit on zero.

How to avoid the mix-up: read the vertical axis label before reading the shape. Every time. The graphs look identical and only the axis tells you which you have.

The same discipline sorts out the other two facts: on a distance–time graph the gradient is the speed, and on a velocity–time graph the gradient is the acceleration and the area is the distance. Area under a distance–time graph means nothing at all.

05Exam drill

Now in exam register. Time yourself at roughly one minute per mark. State formulas when asked, show substitutions, give units.

D1

State the equation linking moment, force and perpendicular distance from the pivot. (1)

A force of 60 N is applied to a lever at a perpendicular distance of 45 cm from the pivot. Calculate the moment. Give your answer in N m. (3)

Show answer

moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot 1

45 cm = 0.45 m 1

moment = 60 × 0.45 1

= 27 N m 1

If you wrote 2700, you didn't convert. The unit demanded in the answer line tells you which conversion to do — check it before you calculate, not after.

D2

A cyclist accelerates uniformly from 4.0 m/s to 12 m/s over a distance of 32 m.

Calculate her acceleration. (3)

Show answer

v² = u² + 2as 1

12² = 4.0² + 2 × a × 32 → 144 = 16 + 64a 1

a = 128 ÷ 64 = 2.0 m/s² 1

Note you were given u, v and s but no time — that's the signal to reach for v² = u² + 2as rather than a = (v−u)/t.

D3

A car of mass 1400 kg brakes from 30 m/s to rest in a distance of 75 m.

(a) Calculate the deceleration. (3)
(b) Calculate the braking force. (2)

Show answer

(a) v² = u² + 2as, with v = 0 1

0 = 30² + 2 × a × 75 → 0 = 900 + 150a 1

a = −900 ÷ 150 = −6.0 m/s², i.e. a deceleration of 6.0 m/s² 1

(b) F = m × a = 1400 × 6.0 1

= 8400 N 1

The negative sign in (a) is correct and worth keeping in your working — it shows the acceleration opposes the motion. In (b) use the magnitude.

D4

A parachutist of mass 80 kg falls from a helicopter. Take g = 10 N/kg.

(a) Calculate her weight. (2)
(b) At one moment the air resistance acting on her is 500 N. Calculate her acceleration at that moment. (3)
(c) Explain, in terms of forces, what happens to her acceleration as she continues to fall. (3)

Show answer

(a) W = m × g = 80 × 10 1 = 800 N 1

(b) Resultant force = 800 − 500 = 300 N downwards 1

a = F ÷ m = 300 ÷ 80 1 = 3.75 m/s² 1

(c) As her speed increases, air resistance increases 1. The resultant force therefore decreases 1, so her acceleration decreases — until air resistance equals weight, the resultant force is zero and she falls at terminal velocity 1

Notice part (c) says "in terms of forces". Do not mention kinetic or gravitational potential energy. It won't be credited and it risks muddling the answer.

D5

A uniform plank 3.0 m long rests on a pivot 1.0 m from its left-hand end. A 200 N box sits on the extreme left-hand end.

Calculate the downward force that must be applied at the extreme right-hand end to keep the plank horizontal. Ignore the weight of the plank. (4)

Show answer

Anticlockwise moment (box) = 200 × 1.0 = 200 N m 1

Distance from pivot to right-hand end = 3.0 − 1.0 = 2.0 m 1

For equilibrium, clockwise moment = anticlockwise moment, so F × 2.0 = 200 1

F = 100 N 1

The step people miss is the second one: the distance to the right-hand end is not 3.0 m, because moments are measured from the pivot. Mark the pivot on the diagram and measure both distances from it before doing any arithmetic.

D6

The graph in a question shows a vehicle's velocity rising steadily from 0 to 20 m/s over 8 s, holding at 20 m/s for 10 s, then falling steadily to rest over 4 s.

(a) Calculate the acceleration during the first 8 s. (2)
(b) Calculate the total distance travelled. (3)
(c) Calculate the average speed for the whole journey. (2)

Show answer

(a) a = (v − u) ÷ t = (20 − 0) ÷ 8 1 = 2.5 m/s² 1

(b) Split the area into three pieces:

  • Triangle: ½ × 8 × 20 = 80 m
  • Rectangle: 10 × 20 = 200 m
  • Triangle: ½ × 4 × 20 = 40 m 1 for method, 1 for two correct areas

Total = 320 m 1

(c) Total time = 8 + 10 + 4 = 22 s. Average speed = 320 ÷ 22 1 = 14.5 m/s (3 s.f.) 1

Average speed uses total distance over total time — not the average of the speeds, and not the maximum speed.

D7

A student investigates how the extension of a spring varies with the force applied.

(a) Describe how the student should carry out this investigation, including how they will make it a fair test. (4)
(b) Sketch the shape of the graph they would obtain if they continued adding weights well past the elastic limit, and label the limit of proportionality. (2)
(c) Explain what would happen to the spring if the force applied were too large. (2)

Show answer

(a) Any four of the following 1111

  • Hang the spring vertically from a clamp and record its original length with a ruler
  • Add a known weight, and measure the new length; extension = new length − original length
  • Repeat for a range of increasing weights, recording both force and extension
  • Use the same spring throughout and keep the ruler vertical, reading at eye level to avoid parallax
  • Remove the weights between readings to check the spring returns to its original length

(b) A straight line through the origin 1, then curving upwards (extension increasing more per unit force), with the point where it stops being straight labelled as the limit of proportionality 1

(c) The spring would be stretched beyond its elastic limit 1 and would be permanently deformed — it would not return to its original length when the force was removed 1

Part (a) is a validity question in disguise. "Use the same spring" is the controlled variable that earns the fair-test mark.

D8

A ball is thrown vertically upwards and returns to the thrower's hand.

Explain the motion of the ball from the moment it leaves the hand to the moment it returns, referring to forces throughout. Assume air resistance is not negligible. (6)

Show answer

This is the 5- or 6-mark question this topic builds towards. Structure it as up, top, down.

Going up: weight acts downwards and air resistance also acts downwards, because air resistance always opposes motion 1. The resultant force is therefore downwards and larger than the weight alone, so the ball decelerates 1.

At the top: the ball is momentarily at rest, so there is no air resistance. The only force is its weight, so the resultant force is downwards and it begins to accelerate downwards 1.

Coming down: weight acts downwards, air resistance now acts upwards, opposing the downward motion 1. The resultant force is downwards but smaller than the weight, so the ball accelerates downwards at less than 10 m/s² 1. As speed increases, air resistance increases, so the resultant force and therefore the acceleration decrease 1.

The mark that separates good answers: air resistance changes direction because it opposes motion, not because it opposes weight. That single sentence is worth learning.

D9

A student uses a spanner to loosen a nut, applying a force of 92 N at a perpendicular distance of 18 cm from the centre of the nut.

(a) State the formula linking moment, force and perpendicular distance from the pivot. (1)
(b) Calculate the moment of the force. Give your answer in N m. (3)
(c) Suggest one way the student could increase the moment without applying a larger force. (1)

Show answer

(a) moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot 1

(b) 18 cm = 0.18 m 1

92 × 0.18 1 = 16.56 N m, or 17 N m to 2 s.f. 1

(c) Use a longer spanner, increasing the perpendicular distance from the pivot 1

The answer is demanded in N m and the distance is given in cm. An answer of 1656 is a unit-conversion error. Convert first, before you multiply — it is much harder to forget that way.

D10

A uniform gate weighs 360 N. Its weight acts through its centre of gravity, 0.90 m from the hinge. A person pushes vertically upwards at a point 2.4 m from the hinge to take the weight off the hinge.

(a) State the principle of moments. (1)
(b) Calculate the moment of the gate's weight about the hinge. (2)
(c) Calculate the force the person must apply. (3)

Show answer

(a) For a balanced object, the total clockwise moment equals the total anticlockwise moment about the same pivot 1

(b) moment = 360 × 0.90 1 = 324 N m 1

(c) Principle of moments: F × 2.4 = 324 1

F = 324 ÷ 2.4 1 = 135 N 1

Sanity check: the person pushes at nearly three times the distance of the weight, so needs roughly a third of the force. An answer larger than 360 N would mean you had divided the wrong way round.

Note where the gate's weight is taken to act — through its centre of gravity. For a uniform object that is its midpoint, and questions rely on you knowing it.

D11

A vehicle's velocity is recorded as: 0 s → 0 m/s; 6 s → 18 m/s; 14 s → 18 m/s; 20 s → 0 m/s.

(a) Calculate the acceleration during the first 6 seconds. (3)
(b) Determine the total distance travelled. (3)
(c) Calculate the average speed for the whole journey. (2)
(d) The vehicle has a mass of 1200 kg. Calculate the braking force during the last 6 seconds. (3)

Show answer

(a) a = (v − u) ÷ t 1 = (18 − 0) ÷ 6 1 = 3.0 m/s² 1

(b) Triangle 0–6 s: ½ × 6 × 18 = 54 m; rectangle 6–14 s: 8 × 18 = 144 m; triangle 14–20 s: ½ × 6 × 18 = 54 m 11

Total = 252 m 1

(c) average speed = 252 ÷ 20 1 = 12.6 m/s 1

(d) Deceleration = 18 ÷ 6 = 3.0 m/s² 1

F = m × a = 1200 × 3.0 1 = 3600 N 1

The distance is the area under the graph, split into rectangles and triangles. Do not average the velocities in (c) — average speed is total distance over total time.

D12

A ball is thrown vertically upwards at 16 m/s. Take g = 10 m/s².

Calculate the maximum height it reaches. Give your answer to 2 significant figures. (4)

Show answer

At the highest point the ball is momentarily at rest, so v = 0 1

v² = u² + 2as, so 0 = 16² + 2 × (−10) × s 1

s = 256 ÷ 20 1

= 13 m (2 s.f.) 1

Two things sink people here. The acceleration is negative, because gravity acts downwards while the ball moves upwards — miss the minus sign and you get a negative height. And the 2025 examiner: "A significant number gave 2 d.p. instead of 2 s.f." 12.8 is 3 s.f.; the answer wanted is 13.

Reach for v² = u² + 2as whenever you are given two speeds and a distance, or asked for a distance, with no time mentioned anywhere.

D13

A stone is dropped from a tall bridge and falls towards the water below.

Explain, in terms of the forces acting, how the motion of the stone changes from the moment it is released until it reaches terminal velocity. (5)

Show answer

At the moment of release the stone has weight acting downwards and little or no air resistance, so there is a resultant downward force and it accelerates 1

As its speed increases, the air resistance acting on it increases 1

The resultant force therefore decreases 1

So the acceleration decreases — the stone is still speeding up, but less rapidly 1

When the air resistance has grown equal to the weight, the resultant force is zero, so there is no acceleration and the stone falls at a constant velocity — its terminal velocity 1

This is the question the year group lost most marks on. The examiner: answers "focussed either partly or exclusively on energy which was ignored… Unbalanced forces cause accelerations, NOT GPE/KE."

Any sentence about kinetic or gravitational potential energy scores zero here. Say weight, not "gravity" — "gravity" was ignored. Say air resistance or drag, not "wind resistance", which was not accepted.

D14

The same stone now falls the last 30 m at a constant terminal velocity of 24 m/s.

State what happens to its gravitational potential energy during this part of the fall, and explain where that energy goes. (3)

Show answer

Its gravitational potential energy decreases, because it is losing height 1

Its kinetic energy does not change, because its speed is constant 1

So all of the gravitational potential energy lost is dissipated to the thermal energy store of the surrounding air, by the work done against air resistance 1

Compare this with D13 deliberately. Same stone, same fall — but that question said "in terms of forces" and this one is about energy. An answer about weight and air resistance balancing would score nothing here, and this answer would score nothing there. The instruction in the question decides which vocabulary earns marks.

D15

(a) State what is meant by the stopping distance of a vehicle. (1)
(b) For each of the following, state whether it affects the thinking distance, the braking distance, or both: (i) an icy road, (ii) the driver being tired, (iii) driving faster, (iv) a heavily loaded car. (4)

Show answer

(a) The stopping distance is the thinking distance plus the braking distance 1

(b) (i) Icy road — braking distance only. Ice reduces the grip of the tyres; it does not affect reactions 1

(ii) Tired driver — thinking distance only. A longer reaction time means further travelled before braking begins; the brakes work exactly as well 1

(iii) Driving faster — both. More distance is covered during the reaction time, and more distance is needed to shed the extra speed 1

(iv) Heavily loaded — braking distance only. More mass takes more distance to stop; it does not change the driver's reactions 1

The examiner's favourite trap is offering "ice on the road" as a factor affecting thinking distance. Ask yourself: does this change the driver's reactions, or the car's grip? That question sorts every case.

D16

A car travels at 24 m/s. Its thinking distance is measured as 14.4 m.

(a) State the formula linking average speed, distance and time. (1)
(b) Calculate the driver's reaction time. (3)
(c) The car's braking distance at this speed is 43 m. Calculate its deceleration while braking. (4)

Show answer

(a) average speed = distance moved ÷ time taken 1

(b) Rearranged, time = distance ÷ speed 1

= 14.4 ÷ 24 1 = 0.60 s 1

(c) Use v² = u² + 2as with the braking distance: 0 = 24² + 2 × a × 43 1

576 = −86a 1, so a = −6.7 m/s² 1

A deceleration of 6.7 m/s² (2 s.f.) 1

Part (c) must use the braking distance of 43 m, not the stopping distance of 57.4 m. In 2024, candidates who used the wrong distance were capped at 3 marks out of 4. The question tells you which one — read it twice.

D17

A student hangs increasing weights from a spring and measures the extension each time.

(a) Describe the relationship shown by the initial part of a force–extension graph, and name the law it obeys. (2)
(b) The student continues past the elastic limit. State and explain what happens to the spring when the force is removed. (2)
(c) A different sample returns exactly to its original length when unloaded. State what this shows, and how it would appear on the graph. (2)

Show answer

(a) A straight line through the origin — the extension is directly proportional to the force 1

This initial linear region obeys Hooke's law 1

(b) The spring has been stretched beyond its elastic limit 1

It is permanently deformed — it does not return to its original length when the force is removed 1

(c) It is showing elastic behaviour: the ability to recover its original shape once the deforming forces are removed 1

On the graph, the unloading line returns to the origin — to zero extension at zero force 1

In 2024, "the spring breaks" was ignored in part (b). The examinable point is permanent deformation, not breakage. And in part (a), "force is proportional to extension" earned both marks — say "proportional", not "it goes up".

D18

A skydiver of total mass 90 kg falls from an aircraft. Take g = 10 N/kg.

(a) Calculate her weight. (2)
(b) At one moment the air resistance acting on her is 360 N. Calculate the resultant force. (2)
(c) Calculate her acceleration at that moment. (3)
(d) Later, the air resistance is 900 N. State her acceleration and explain your answer. (2)

Show answer

(a) W = m × g = 90 × 10 1 = 900 N 1

(b) 900 − 360 1 = 540 N downwards 1

(c) F = m × a, so a = F ÷ m 1 = 540 ÷ 90 1 = 6.0 m/s² 1

(d) Zero 1

The air resistance now equals her weight, so the resultant force is zero, and with no resultant force there is no acceleration — she is at terminal velocity 1

Note in (b) that the mark scheme wants the direction as well as the number: force is a vector. And in (c), F = ma always takes the resultant force and the mass in kilograms — never the weight.

D19

A book rests on a table. A student says: "the book's weight and the table's upward push are a Newton's third law pair, because they are equal and opposite."

State whether the student is correct and explain your answer. (3)

Show answer

The student is incorrect 1

Both of those forces act on the same object — the book — whereas the two forces of a third-law pair always act on two different objects 1

They are also different types of force (one gravitational, one a contact push), while a third-law pair is always the same type. The true partner of the book's weight is the gravitational pull of the book on the Earth 1

Equal and opposite is not enough on its own. The test is: are they on different objects, and are they the same kind of force? Balanced forces on one object are a different idea entirely.

D20

A trolley is pulled along a bench by a force of 12 N. Friction opposes its motion with a force of 4.5 N. The trolley has a mass of 2.5 kg.

(a) Calculate the resultant force on the trolley. (2)
(b) Calculate its acceleration. (3)
(c) The pulling force is removed. Describe and explain what happens to the trolley. (3)

Show answer

(a) 12 − 4.5 1 = 7.5 N in the direction of motion 1

(b) a = F ÷ m 1 = 7.5 ÷ 2.5 1 = 3.0 m/s² 1

(c) The trolley slows down and eventually stops 1

Friction still acts, and it opposes motion, so it now acts backwards with nothing balancing it 1

There is therefore a resultant force backwards, which decelerates the trolley 1

Friction always opposes motion — that is its defining property, and it means friction reverses direction if the object does.

D21

Full exam-style question. A 0.30 kg ball is dropped from a height and bounces.

(a) Calculate its weight. (2)
(b) It hits the ground at 7.0 m/s. Calculate the height it was dropped from, to 2 significant figures. Take g = 10 m/s². (4)
(c) Explain, in terms of forces, why the ball accelerates as it falls. (2)
(d) The ball rebounds to a lower height each time. Explain why, in terms of energy. (3)

Show answer

(a) W = m × g = 0.30 × 10 1 = 3.0 N 1

(b) v² = u² + 2as, with u = 0 1

7.0² = 0 + 2 × 10 × s 1, so s = 49 ÷ 20 1

= 2.5 m 1

(c) Its weight acts downwards and air resistance is small, so there is a resultant downward force 1

An unbalanced force produces an acceleration, in the direction of that force 1

(d) On each bounce some energy is transferred away from the ball's kinetic store 1

It is dissipated to thermal energy stores in the ball and the ground, and to the surroundings by sound 1

So less kinetic energy is available to be transferred to the gravitational potential store on the way up, and the ball rises to a lower height each time 1

Parts (c) and (d) are the forces/energy pairing again. Keep them apart: no energy language in (c), no force language in (d).

D22

A uniform plank of weight 150 N and length 4.0 m rests on a pivot 1.5 m from its left-hand end. A box of weight 200 N is placed on the extreme left-hand end.

(a) State where the plank's own weight acts. (1)
(b) Calculate the moment of the box about the pivot. (2)
(c) Determine whether the plank balances, showing your reasoning. (4)

Show answer

(a) Through its centre of gravity, which for a uniform plank is at its midpoint — 2.0 m from either end 1

(b) The box is 1.5 m from the pivot, so moment = 200 × 1.5 1 = 300 N m anticlockwise 1

(c) The plank's centre of gravity is 2.0 − 1.5 = 0.5 m to the right of the pivot 1

Its moment = 150 × 0.5 = 75 N m clockwise 1

300 N m anticlockwise does not equal 75 N m clockwise 1

So the plank does not balance — it tips to the left, the box's side 1

The plank's own weight is easy to forget, and questions include it deliberately. Whenever a beam is described as "uniform" and given a weight, it contributes a moment acting at its midpoint.
