02How it actually works

A lot of this topic is straight recall — the spectrum in order, one use and one danger per band — and it is marked strictly, word by word. The rest is one equation used in three disguises. Every heading says where it was asked.

What a wave is, and what it does not carry

Spec 3.4

Waves transfer energy and information without transferring matter. That sentence is the definition, and the second half is the part that earns marks.

Watch a duck on a pond as ripples pass: it bobs up and down and stays put. The water is not travelling towards the shore — each bit of water moves about its own position and passes the disturbance on. Same with sound: the air by your ear vibrates, but no air travels from the speaker to you.

So what moves along is the disturbance, carrying energy with it. This is why a wave can be enormously powerful without anything being thrown at you.

Transverse and longitudinal

Spec 3.2 · 2025 Q4(a) · 2024 Q2(a)(iii) · Mock 2 Q6(a) — asked in some form on every paper

The difference is the direction the particles vibrate, relative to the direction the wave travels.

Particles vibrateExamples
TransversePerpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of travelAll EM waves, water ripples, waves on a rope
LongitudinalParallel to the direction of travelSound, ultrasound

A longitudinal wave is a pattern of squashed regions and stretched regions — compressions and rarefactions — travelling along. Push and pull the end of a slinky and you can watch them move.

2024's mark scheme gives one mark just for mentioning vibrations or oscillations before you say anything about direction. Start your answer with the word "vibrate" and you have banked a mark.

Then use parallel and perpendicular. "Up and down" and "side to side" are not precise enough, because they depend on which way you have drawn the wave.

Amplitude, wavelength, frequency and period

Spec 3.3, 3.24P · 2023 Q3(a) · Mock 1 Q4(a) — a 2-mark definition

  • Amplitude — the maximum displacement from the undisturbed position. Measured from the middle to a peak, not from peak to trough.
  • Wavelength (λ) — the distance for one complete wave, measured in metres. Peak to next peak works.
  • Frequency (f) — the number of complete waves passing a point per second, in hertz.
  • Period (T) — the time for one complete wave to pass, in seconds.
  • Wavefront — a line joining points on a wave that are all at the same stage, such as a line of crests.

"Explain what a frequency of 30 kHz means" — two marks

The mark scheme splits it in two, so say both halves: 30 000 complete waves 1 pass a point each second 1.

Saying only "30 000 hertz" scores nothing — that is restating the number, not explaining it. And remember the prefix: kHz means × 1000, and unit conversions like this are where the calculation marks go.

Worth knowing alongside: human hearing runs from 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz, so 30 kHz is ultrasound — above the range we can hear.

v = f λ — and the mark for quoting it

Spec 3.5, 3.7 · 2023 Q3(b) · 2025 Q4(c) · Mock 1 Q4(b)

v = f × λ
v = wave speed in metres/second (m/s) · f = frequency in hertz (Hz) · λ = wavelength in metres (m)

It works for every wave — sound, light, water, radio — which is why it appears three or four times a paper in different costumes.

2025's examiner: students "confused velocity for distance and did not quote v = fλ — quote the equation." There is a mark for writing it down before substituting, and it is regularly thrown away.

Same paper on significant figures: "a problem for a lot of students" — 5.5 × 10¹⁴ was rejected where 5.6 × 10¹⁴ was wanted. Carry the full number through your working and round only at the very end.

Watch the powers of ten. Light is 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s, wavelengths of light are around 10⁻⁷ m, and a slip of a single power costs the answer.

Echoes and sonar — the factor of two

2023 Q3(b)(iii) · Mock 1 Q4(c) — "the single most common error in echo and sonar problems"

A sonar pulse travels down to the sea bed and back again. The time you are given is for the whole round trip, so the distance you calculate from speed × time is twice the depth.

The method, with the halving made explicit

A pulse returns 0.16 s after being emitted. Sound travels at 1500 m/s in sea water. How deep is it?

1. Total distance travelled: 1500 × 0.16 = 240 m

2. That is there and back, so the depth = 240 ÷ 2 = 120 m

The factor of two is its own mark in both mark schemes. Write the words "there and back" in your working so the examiner can see you have used it.

The same applies in reverse: if you are given the depth and asked for the time, double the distance before dividing by the speed. In 2023, candidates who forgot scored 3 out of 4.

Period, frequency and the oscilloscope

Spec 3.6, 3.26P, 3.27P · Mock 2 Q6(c)

f = 1 ÷ T
f = frequency in hertz (Hz) · T = time period in seconds (s)

Frequency and period are two ways of saying the same thing. If one wave takes 0.005 s, then 200 fit into a second, so the frequency is 200 Hz.

A microphone connected to an oscilloscope turns a sound wave into a trace on a screen, letting you measure it. The horizontal axis is time, controlled by the time base setting — so many milliseconds per division.

Reading it, step by step

One complete wave spans 3 divisions; the time base is 2 ms per division.

1. Period: 3 × 2 ms = 6 ms = 0.006 s

2. Frequency: f = 1 ÷ 0.006 = 167 Hz

Convert milliseconds to seconds before taking the reciprocal. Forgetting gives an answer 1000 times out.

Note also what the trace shows: a taller wave means a larger amplitude, so a louder sound. A wave with more cycles across the screen means a higher frequency, so a higher pitch.

Sound: pitch, loudness, and measuring its speed

Spec 3.25P, 3.28P, 3.29P

Two straightforward links, and they are not interchangeable:

  • Pitch depends on the frequency of vibration of the source. Faster vibration, higher pitch.
  • Loudness depends on the amplitude of vibration of the source. Bigger vibration, louder sound.

To measure the speed of sound in air: stand a measured distance from a large flat wall, make a sharp sound, and time the delay before the echo returns. Speed = (2 × distance) ÷ time — the factor of two again, because the sound goes there and back. Repeat and average, and use a long distance so the time is big enough to measure reliably against your reaction time.

The Doppler effect

Spec 3.8 · 2025 Q4(b) · Mock 2 Q6(b) — 4 marks

When a source moves relative to you, the observed frequency and wavelength change. A siren sounds higher-pitched approaching and drops as it passes.

The reason is geometry, not physics of sound. As the source moves towards you it partly catches up with the waves it has already emitted, so they arrive bunched together — a shorter wavelength. Behind it, the waves are stretched out — a longer wavelength.

The four-mark answer, two marks for each direction:

  1. Approaching: the waves are squashed together, so the wavelength is shorter
  2. The wave speed is unchanged, so from v = fλ a shorter wavelength means a higher observed frequency — a higher pitch
  3. Receding: the waves are stretched out, so the wavelength is longer
  4. Again the speed is unchanged, so the observed frequency is lower — the pitch drops

Mock 2 is explicit: "do not write that the sound travels faster or slower. The speed is fixed by the air." The speed of sound depends on the medium, not on how fast the source is moving. What changes is the wavelength, and therefore the frequency you receive.

Note also that the source has not changed. The siren is emitting exactly the same note throughout — the driver hears no change at all.

The electromagnetic spectrum

Spec 3.10, 3.11 · 2025 Q1(a) · Mock 1 Q1(a)(c)

In order of increasing frequency and decreasing wavelength:

radio · microwave · infrared · visible · ultraviolet · X-ray · gamma

Within visible light, the same order runs red → orange → yellow → green → blue → indigo → violet: red has the longest wavelength and lowest frequency, violet the shortest and highest.

What all EM waves have in common, which is a two-mark question in its own right:

  • All are transverse
  • All transfer energy
  • All travel at the same speed in a vacuum (3.0 × 10⁸ m/s)
  • All can be reflected and refracted

Notice the pattern that makes the dangers memorable: the higher the frequency, the more energy each wave carries, and the more damaging it is. Radio waves pass through you harmlessly; gamma rays cause mutations.

Reflection and refraction

Spec 3.9

All waves can be reflected and refracted — sound as well as light.

Reflection bounces a wave off a surface; an echo is reflected sound. Refraction is the change of direction when a wave enters a different medium and changes speed.

The key to refraction questions: when a wave crosses into a new medium, its frequency stays the same — it is set by the source, and the waves cannot pile up at the boundary. So if the speed changes, from v = fλ the wavelength must change in proportion. Light entering water slows down, so its wavelength shortens.

Sound and light behave oppositely here, which is one of the standard "differences between sound and light" marks: light slows when it enters a denser medium, while sound speeds up — it travels faster in water than in air, and faster still in solids.

Sound

  • Sound is a longitudinal wave and requires a medium — it cannot travel through a vacuum.
  • Human hearing range: 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz.
  • Pitch depends on frequency. Higher frequency, higher pitch.
  • Loudness depends on amplitude. Larger amplitude, louder.
  • Sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids, slowest in gases — the opposite of light.

On an oscilloscope trace

The horizontal axis is time, set by the time base (e.g. 2 ms per division). Count the divisions for one complete wave to get the time period, then use f = 1 ÷ T.

A louder sound gives a taller trace. A higher-pitched sound gives waves squashed closer together.

The Doppler effect

When a source moves towards an observer, the waves ahead of it are squashed together: wavelength decreases and observed frequency increases. Moving away, the waves are stretched: wavelength increases, frequency decreases.

The wave speed itself does not change — it's set by the medium. Only the observed wavelength and frequency change. Saying "the waves speed up" loses the mark.

04Understanding it

U1

You see lightning several seconds before you hear the thunder, even though both happen at the same instant. Estimate how far away a storm is if the gap is 6 seconds — and explain why you can ignore the travel time of the light.

Show answer

Sound: distance = speed × time = 340 × 6 = about 2000 m, or 2 km.

Light covers that same 2 km in 2000 ÷ (3 × 10⁸) ≈ 7 × 10⁻⁶ s — about seven millionths of a second. Compared with 6 seconds, that is utterly negligible, so you can treat the flash as instantaneous.

The wider point: light is roughly 900,000 times faster than sound. Any question involving both — thunder, a starting pistol seen and heard, an echo timed with a stopwatch — you assume the light arrives instantly.

U2

A wave passes from air into water and slows down. Its frequency stays the same. What happens to its wavelength, and why must the frequency be unchanged?

Show answer

From v = fλ, if v decreases and f is fixed, then λ must decrease. The wave gets shorter.

Why frequency can't change: frequency is set by the source. If 500 waves per second arrive at the boundary, 500 waves per second must leave it — otherwise waves would be piling up at the boundary or vanishing there, and neither happens. Waves are neither created nor destroyed at a boundary.

This is also the mechanism of refraction: the change of speed at the boundary is what bends the wave.

U3

An ambulance siren sounds higher-pitched as it approaches. Does the siren itself change? Explain what is physically happening to the waves.

Show answer

The siren doesn't change at all. It emits exactly the same frequency throughout. What changes is what reaches your ear.

As the ambulance moves towards you, it partially catches up with the waves it has already emitted. Each successive wave is emitted from a position slightly closer to you than the last, so the waves bunch up. The distance between them — the wavelength — decreases.

The waves still travel at 340 m/s through the air. So with v fixed and λ smaller, v = fλ means the frequency you receive is higher, and you hear a higher pitch.

As it passes and recedes, the waves stretch out, wavelength increases and pitch drops — which is why the sound falls audibly at the moment of passing.

U4

Why is ultraviolet dangerous to skin while visible light and infrared, which reach you in far greater quantities from the Sun, are not — despite all three being electromagnetic waves?

Show answer

Because the danger depends on frequency, not on quantity.

Higher frequency means each wave carries more energy. Infrared and visible light have relatively low frequencies — they deposit energy as heat, which the body handles routinely.

Ultraviolet sits above visible light in frequency, and carries enough energy per wave to be ionising: it can knock electrons off atoms and damage DNA in skin cells. Damaged DNA can cause mutations, which can lead to skin cancer. X-rays and gamma rays sit higher still and are correspondingly more dangerous.

The pattern to remember: moving right along the spectrum, frequency rises, energy per wave rises, and the biological danger shifts from heating to ionisation.

U5

A sonar question gives you the time for a pulse to return, and you calculate a distance of 240 m. Why is the answer to "how deep is the sea" not 240 m — and why is this described as the single most common error in the topic?

Show answer

Because the pulse travelled down to the sea bed and back up again. The 240 m is the whole round trip, so the depth is 240 ÷ 2 = 120 m.

Why it catches so many people: the calculation itself is easy — speed × time — and it produces a confident-looking number. Nothing in the arithmetic reminds you that the journey was doubled. You have to spot it from the physical situation, and under time pressure that step gets skipped.

The habit that fixes it: write the words "there and back" in your working before you divide. Both mark schemes award the factor of two as its own mark, so making it visible earns it even if you then slip in the arithmetic.

It works in reverse too. If you are given the depth and asked for the time, double the distance first. In 2023, candidates who forgot scored 3 out of 4.

U6

A police car passes you with its siren on and the pitch drops. A student says the sound waves slow down as the car moves away. What is wrong with that, and what actually changes?

Show answer

The speed of sound does not change at all. It is fixed by the medium — the air — and has nothing to do with how fast the source is moving. Mock 2's mark scheme says so explicitly.

What changes is the wavelength. As the car recedes, each wave is emitted from slightly further away than the last, so the waves arrive stretched out — a longer wavelength.

Then use v = fλ. The speed v is unchanged and the wavelength λ has increased, so the frequency f you receive must have decreased — and lower frequency means lower pitch.

The detail that proves the source hasn't changed: the driver hears no change whatsoever. The siren emits exactly the same note throughout. The effect exists only for an observer the source is moving relative to.

U7

Light slows down when it enters water, but sound speeds up when it enters water. Both are waves. Why do they behave in opposite ways?

Show answer

Because they travel by completely different mechanisms.

Sound needs particles to carry it — it is passed on by particles bumping into their neighbours. The closer together and more strongly linked those particles are, the faster the disturbance is handed along. So sound is fastest in solids, slower in liquids, slowest in gases, and cannot travel through a vacuum at all.

Light is an electromagnetic wave and needs no medium. It travels fastest in a vacuum, and anything it passes through only impedes it. So a denser medium slows light down.

What they share: in both cases the frequency is unchanged when the wave crosses the boundary — it is set by the source. So from v = fλ, whichever way the speed goes, the wavelength goes the same way.

This contrast is one of the standard marks in "give three differences between sound and light", along with longitudinal versus transverse and needing a medium versus not.

U8

The 2025 examiner refused "microwaving food" as a use of microwaves and "curing cancer" as a use of gamma rays. Both sound correct. Why were they rejected, and what should be written instead?

Show answer

Because this part of the specification is assessed as recall of specific wording, and near-misses do not score.

"Microwaving food" uses the name of the wave to describe its own use — it is circular and says nothing about what is happening. The specification wording is cooking, or heating food. (The other listed use is satellite transmissions.)

"Curing cancer" overstates it and is not the specification's phrase. Gamma rays are used for sterilising food and medical equipment, and for treating cancer. "Medicine" and "medical procedures" were also rejected for being too vague to show you know anything specific.

Mock 1 adds two more: "warming things up" is not "heaters" for infrared, and "tanning" is not "fluorescent lamps" for ultraviolet.

What to do about it: this is the one part of the course where paraphrasing is actively punished. Learn the table of uses and dangers in the exact words and reproduce them. There are only about a dozen phrases and they are worth several marks a year.

U9

Amplitude is measured from the middle of the wave to a peak, not from peak to trough. Why does it matter which one you use, and what does amplitude correspond to for a sound?

Show answer

Because measuring peak to trough gives you double the amplitude. Amplitude is defined as the maximum displacement from the undisturbed position — from the rest line to the top, not the full height of the wave.

On an oscilloscope trace this is an easy mark to throw away: the eye is drawn to the full vertical height of the wave, which is twice what is being asked for.

For a sound, amplitude means loudness. A larger amplitude of vibration at the source produces a louder sound. Frequency is separate and controls the pitch.

The two are independent, which is worth checking you believe: you can play the same note quietly or loudly (same frequency, different amplitude), and you can play a high note and a low note at the same volume (different frequency, same amplitude). On a screen, taller means louder and more-cycles-across means higher-pitched.

05Exam drill

D1

State the equation linking wave speed, frequency and wavelength. (1)

A radio station broadcasts at 98.4 MHz. Radio waves travel at 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s. Calculate the wavelength. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. (4)

Show answer

v = f × λ 1

98.4 MHz = 98.4 × 10⁶ Hz = 9.84 × 10⁷ Hz 1

λ = v ÷ f = (3.0 × 10⁸) ÷ (9.84 × 10⁷) 1

= 3.0487… 1 = 3.05 m to 3 s.f. 1

Mega means × 10⁶. Kilo means × 10³. Both appear constantly in wave questions and both are the difference between full marks and none.

D7

D13

Give one use and one danger of excessive exposure for each of: microwaves, infrared, ultraviolet, and gamma rays. (8)

Show answer

Microwaves — use: cooking or heating food, or satellite transmissions 1 · danger: internal heating of body tissue 1

Infrared — use: heaters, or night vision equipment 1 · danger: skin burns 1

Ultraviolet — use: fluorescent lamps 1 · danger: damage to surface cells, blindness 1

Gamma — use: sterilising food and medical equipment, or treating cancer 1 · danger: cancer, mutation 1

This is the strictest marking on the paper. 2025 rejected "microwaving food", "curing cancer", "medicine" and "medical procedures". Mock 1 rejected "warming things up" for infrared and "tanning" for ultraviolet. Reproduce the specification's phrases rather than your own.

D14

Describe the difference between the motion of the particles in a transverse wave and in a longitudinal wave, and give one example of each. (4)

Show answer

In both, the particles vibrate or oscillate about a fixed position 1

In a transverse wave the vibrations are perpendicular to the direction of travel — for example light, or any electromagnetic wave 11

In a longitudinal wave the vibrations are parallel to the direction of travel — for example sound 1

2024's mark scheme gives a mark simply for mentioning vibrations or oscillations before any statement about direction. Open with that word. And use parallel and perpendicular, not "up and down" and "side to side", which depend on how the wave happens to be drawn.

D15

A student stands 85 m from a large wall, claps once, and hears the echo 0.50 s later.

(a) Calculate the speed of sound in air from these figures. (3)
(b) Suggest two ways the student could improve the accuracy of this measurement. (2)

Show answer

(a) The sound travels there and back: distance = 2 × 85 = 170 m 1

speed = distance ÷ time = 170 ÷ 0.50 1

= 340 m/s 1

(b) Any two 11: repeat the measurement several times and take a mean · stand further from the wall so the time interval is longer and reaction-time error matters less · time several claps in succession and divide · use an electronic timing method rather than a stopwatch to remove reaction time

The factor of two appears here as a doubling rather than a halving, because you are given the one-way distance and the round-trip time. Always ask which of the two the question has handed you.

D16

Give three differences between a sound wave and a light wave, other than differences in their frequency and wavelength. (3)

Show answer

Any three 111:

  • Sound is longitudinal; light is transverse
  • Sound vibrations are along the direction of travel; light's are perpendicular to it
  • Light is part of the electromagnetic spectrum; sound is not
  • Light can travel through a vacuum; sound needs a medium and cannot
  • Light travels very much faster than sound
  • Light slows down in a denser medium; sound speeds up

The last one is the least well known and the most likely to separate answers. Sound is passed on by particles colliding, so closer-packed particles carry it faster; light is impeded by matter, so it is fastest in a vacuum.

D17

A wave travels from air into glass. Its speed falls from 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s to 2.0 × 10⁸ m/s. In air its wavelength is 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ m.

(a) State what happens to the frequency of the wave, and why. (2)
(b) Calculate the wavelength of the wave in the glass. (4)

Show answer

(a) The frequency is unchanged 1

It is determined by the source, and the waves cannot pile up or disappear at the boundary — the same number arrive each second as leave 1

(b) Find the frequency in air: f = v ÷ λ = 3.0 × 10⁸ ÷ 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ 1 = 5.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz 1

In the glass the frequency is the same, so λ = v ÷ f = 2.0 × 10⁸ ÷ 5.0 × 10¹⁴ 1

= 4.0 × 10⁻⁷ m 1

Shortcut worth spotting: the speed fell to two thirds, and the frequency is fixed, so the wavelength must also fall to two thirds — 6.0 × ⅔ = 4.0. Useful as a check on the long method.

D18

Full exam-style question. An ultrasound scanner emits pulses at 2.5 MHz. Ultrasound travels at 1540 m/s in body tissue.

(a) State the frequency range of human hearing, and explain why 2.5 MHz is called ultrasound. (2)
(b) Calculate the wavelength of the pulse in body tissue. (4)
(c) A pulse reflects from a boundary 7.7 cm below the skin. Calculate the time before it returns. (4)
(d) State one reason ultrasound is preferred to X-rays for scanning an unborn baby. (1)

Show answer

(a) Human hearing runs from 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz 1

2.5 MHz is 2 500 000 Hz, far above 20 000 Hz, so it is above the range of human hearing 1

(b) v = f × λ 1, so λ = v ÷ f

2.5 MHz = 2.5 × 10⁶ Hz 1

λ = 1540 ÷ 2.5 × 10⁶ 1 = 6.2 × 10⁻⁴ m 1

(c) 7.7 cm = 0.077 m 1

The pulse travels there and back: distance = 2 × 0.077 = 0.154 m 1

time = distance ÷ speed = 0.154 ÷ 1540 1 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ s 1

(d) X-rays can cause cancer or cell mutation, while ultrasound does not damage cells 1

Three separate conversions in one question — MHz to Hz, cm to m, and the there-and-back doubling. This is exactly how the real papers build a long question.
