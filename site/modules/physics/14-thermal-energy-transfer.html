Thermal Energy Transfer

How energy gets from hot things to cold things: by conduction, convection and radiation, and how we slow it down. The physics is simple; the marks go to people who name the mechanism and explain it with particles, density or infrared. This is a Year 11 topic, taught in Year 11 for the GCSE in summer 2027.

01What actually comes up

This topic turned up in six of the ten real Edexcel papers from 2022 to 2024, for about 29 marks in total. It is never a calculation. It is always explain: why snow melts slowly, why a black bar heats up faster, why a lid changes a result, or a 6-mark method for the infrared practical.

Two patterns. Surfaces and infrared come up almost every year: black absorbs and emits well, white and shiny do not. And the examiners want the mechanism named: conduction through the metal, convection in the water or air, radiation as infrared.

On the snow question, the mark for energy flow from the drain cover to the snow came with this note: "must be correct direction of energy transfer". Energy goes from the warmer metal into the colder snow. "The cold goes into the metal" scores nothing.

On the black and silver bottles graph: "reject whole response if both lines show a decrease". The bottles are being heated, so both lines rise, and the black one is always above.

And from the school's marking of the hot-water-in-a-cup question: "“Heat rises” scores nothing — name the transfer mechanism."

02How it actually works

Three mechanisms, one rule. Energy is transferred from hotter places to colder places, and it does it by conduction, convection or radiation. Every question in this topic is asking you which one, and why. This module builds on the cooling-curve work in Module 2 (Solids, Liquids and Gases) and the energy stores in Module 8 (Energy).

The one rule: hot to cold

Spec 4.6 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q10(b)

Thermal energy is always transferred from a hotter object to a colder one, and it keeps going until they reach the same temperature. The bigger the temperature difference, the faster the transfer.

When the two temperatures are equal the objects are in thermal equilibrium and there is no more net transfer. That is why a cooling curve levels off: as the temperature difference shrinks, the rate of transfer shrinks with it.

MechanismNeeds particles?Main place it happens
ConductionYesSolids, especially metals
ConvectionYes, and they must be free to moveLiquids and gases (fluids)
RadiationNoAnywhere, including a vacuum

✗ "The cold spreads into the drink."

✓ "Energy is transferred from the warmer drink to the colder ice."

Cold is not a thing that moves. Only energy moves, and only from hot to cold. The 2023 snow scheme insisted on the correct direction of energy transfer.

Conduction: particles passing on vibrations

Spec 4.6 · 2023 Edexcel 1P Q8(b) · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q10(b)

In conduction, energy passes through a material from particle to particle, without the material itself moving.

Heat one end of a solid rod and this happens:

  1. The particles at the hot end gain energy and vibrate more.
  2. They are closely packed, so they bump into their neighbours and pass on some of that energy.
  3. Those neighbours vibrate more and pass energy on in turn.
  4. Energy spreads along the rod from the hot end to the cold end.

Conduction is best in solids, where particles are packed closely together. It is poor in liquids and very poor in gases, where the particles are further apart.

Why metals are the best conductors

Spec 4.6 · 2023 Edexcel 1P Q8(b)

Metals conduct well because they contain free electrons, which move through the metal and carry energy quickly from the hot part to the cold part.

Every solid conducts by vibrating particles. Metals have this and a sea of free (delocalised) electrons. These electrons gain energy at the hot end, travel through the metal and give energy to particles further along. That is much faster than vibrations alone.

Materials that conduct poorly are insulators: wood, plastic, glass, wool, and above all air. That is why a pan has a plastic handle and why so much insulation works by trapping air.

The drain-cover mark script (2023 Edexcel 1P Q8(b))

Snow melts faster on a metal drain cover than on the ground. The two marks were for any two of:

the drain cover is warmer than the snow · metal is a good conductor · energy is transferred from the drain cover to the snow.

Convection: warm fluid rises, cool fluid sinks

Spec 4.6 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q10(b)

In convection, energy is carried by the fluid itself moving: warmer fluid is less dense and rises, and cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place.

A fluid is a liquid or a gas. Its particles can move from place to place, so the hot part can travel. The chain is always the same:

  1. The fluid near the heat source gains energy. Its particles move faster and spread further apart.
  2. So that part of the fluid expands and becomes less dense.
  3. The less dense fluid rises.
  4. Cooler, denser fluid sinks to replace it, and is then heated in turn.
  5. This circulation is a convection current.

Convection cannot happen in a solid, because its particles are fixed in place. It cannot happen in a vacuum, because there is nothing to move.

✗ "Heat rises."

✓ "The warmer air rises because it is less dense."

✗ "The particles expand."

✓ "The particles move further apart, so the air expands."

Particles do not get bigger when they are heated. The fluid expands because the gaps between the particles grow.

Convection in everyday life

Spec 4.7 · 2023 Edexcel 1P Q8(c)

Heat from below and convection spreads it upwards; cool from above and convection spreads it downwards. That idea explains where heaters, kettle elements and freezer compartments are put.

SituationWhat convection does
Room heater (often called a radiator)Air next to it warms, becomes less dense and rises. Cooler air sinks and flows across the floor towards the heater. A convection current heats the whole room. Most of its energy is transferred by convection, despite the name.
Kettle element at the bottomWater at the bottom warms and rises. Cooler water sinks to the element, so all the water gets heated.
Freezer compartment at the top of a fridgeAir cooled at the top becomes denser and sinks. Warmer air rises to be cooled. The whole fridge is cooled.
Sea breeze on a sunny dayThe land warms faster than the sea. Air above the land warms and rises; cooler air from over the sea moves in to replace it.
Snow on a still dayWarmer air rises away from the snow. The coldest, densest air stays next to the snow, so less energy is transferred to it and it melts more slowly.

The snow mark script (2023 Edexcel 1P Q8(c))

"Explain how a convection current above the snow increases the time taken for the snow to melt." Two marks from:

warmer air rises · colder air is nearest the snow · the rate of energy transfer from the air to the snow decreases.

The scheme said to ignore "nearest the ground". It is the snow the cold air sits on that matters.

Radiation: infrared needs no medium

Spec 4.6 · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1P Q8(d) · 2024 Edexcel 1P Q3

Thermal radiation is energy carried by infrared waves, which are electromagnetic waves, so it needs no particles and can travel through a vacuum.

This makes radiation different from the other two. Conduction and convection both need particles. Radiation does not. It is the only way energy can cross empty space, which is how energy from the Sun reaches the Earth.

All objects emit infrared radiation, all the time. They also absorb it. An object that emits more than it absorbs cools down. An object that absorbs more than it emits warms up.

2022's satellite question gave a mark for saying that conduction and convection play no part outside the spacecraft, and another for the reason: there are no particles in space. Say both halves.

✗ "Radiation needs air to travel through."

✓ "Infrared is an electromagnetic wave, so it can travel through a vacuum."

Surfaces: absorbers and reflectors

Spec 4.8 · 2023 Edexcel 1P Q8(a) · 2024 Edexcel 1P Q3(d)

Dark, matt surfaces are good absorbers of infrared radiation; light, shiny surfaces are poor absorbers and good reflectors.

SurfaceAbsorbing infraredEmitting infrared
Dark and matt (dull black)Best absorberBest emitter
Light and shiny (white, silver)Poor absorber, good reflectorPoor emitter

So in the same sunshine, a black car roof gets hotter than a white one. White snow reflects most of the Sun's infrared, so it absorbs little energy and melts slowly even in direct sunlight.

The two-mark pattern (2023 Edexcel 1P Q8(a), 2024 Edexcel 1P Q3(d))

Mark 1: name the process, absorption or reflection of infrared.

Mark 2: link it to the colour, e.g. white is a poor absorber / good reflector, or black is a better absorber, so more energy is transferred to it in the same time.

2024's scheme noted that "black car absorbs more energy/heat" alone scores both marks. The words absorbs and more energy do the work.

Emission depends on surface and temperature

Spec 4.8 · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1P Q8(d)

The hotter an object is, the more infrared radiation it emits; and at the same temperature, a dark matt surface emits more than a light shiny one.

A good absorber is also a good emitter. That is why a black object heats up fastest in the Sun and cools down fastest in the shade.

The spinning satellite (2022 Edexcel Jan 1P Q8(d), 4 marks)

A dull black bar and a shiny white bar turn towards and away from the Sun. The marks were for any four of:

  1. Both bars warm up facing the Sun and cool down facing away.
  2. Facing the Sun, the black bar's temperature rises faster, because black is a better absorber of radiation.
  3. So the black bar reaches a higher temperature.
  4. Facing away, the black bar's temperature falls faster, because black is a better emitter of radiation.
  5. Conduction and convection play no part outside the spacecraft, because there are no particles in space.

Practical: infrared absorbed by different surfaces

Spec 4.9 · 2024 Edexcel 2P Q3 — 8 marks, 6 of them for the method

Heat two identical containers of water, one black and one silver, with the same infrared heater, and compare how fast the water temperature rises. The water that warms faster is in the better absorber.

  1. Put an equal volume of water in the black bottle and the silver bottle.
  2. Check the water in both starts at the same temperature.
  3. Place both bottles the same distance from the heater, with the same heater setting for both.
  4. Switch on and measure the temperature of both after a set time (or every minute).
  5. Repeat the whole investigation and calculate a mean.
  6. Safety: do not touch the hot heater (burns) and keep water away from the mains (electrocution).

The result: the black bottle's water warms faster. On a temperature–time graph both lines rise and the black line is always above the silver line.

2024's graph mark: "reject whole response if both lines show a decrease". The bottles are being heated. If you draw cooling curves, you get nothing for that part.

The same apparatus run the other way round, with hot water left to cool, tests emission: the water in the black bottle cools faster. Read which one the question describes.

Practicals: conduction and convection

Spec 4.9

For conduction, compare how quickly energy travels along rods of different materials; for convection, make the moving water visible with a coloured crystal.

Conduction. Take rods of different metals of the same length and thickness. Stick a drawing pin to the far end of each with a small blob of wax (or petroleum jelly). Heat the other ends equally, for example by standing them in the same hot water. Time how long each pin takes to fall. The shortest time means the best conductor.

Convection. Drop a small crystal of potassium manganate(VII) (potassium permanganate) at the bottom of a beaker of water, at one side. Heat gently under the crystal. The purple colour rises above the heat, spreads across the top and sinks on the far side: you can see the convection current.

Radiation (emission). A metal cube with faces of different surfaces (matt black, white, shiny silver) is filled with hot water. An infrared detector is held the same distance from each face in turn. The matt black face gives the highest reading and the shiny silver face the lowest.

Reducing unwanted transfer: insulating a house

Spec 4.10

Most insulation works by trapping air: air is a poor conductor, and when it is trapped in small pockets it cannot form convection currents.

MethodHow it worksTransfer reduced
Loft insulation (fibreglass or mineral wool)Traps air between the fibresConduction and convection through the roof
Cavity wall insulation (foam or fibre)Fills the gap between the two walls and traps air in small pockets, so no convection currents form in the cavityConvection (and conduction) across the cavity
Double glazingA layer of air (or a vacuum) between two panes of glassConduction (and convection)
Draught excludersBlock gaps round doors and windowsConvection: warm air escaping
Shiny foil behind a radiatorReflects infrared back into the roomRadiation into the wall

The same trapped-air idea explains why wool jumpers, duvets, feathers and fur keep things warm.

✗ "Air is a good insulator, so the cavity can be left empty."

✓ An empty cavity lets the air circulate in convection currents. Foam traps the air so it cannot move. Air is only a good insulator when it is trapped.

The vacuum flask

Spec 4.10

A vacuum flask reduces all three kinds of transfer: the vacuum stops conduction and convection, and the silvered surfaces reduce radiation.

FeatureTransfer it reducesWhy
Vacuum between two glass wallsConduction and convectionBoth need particles, and a vacuum has none
Silvered (shiny) wallsRadiationShiny surfaces are poor emitters and reflect infrared back
Stopper (plastic or cork)Convection and evaporation from the top; conductionStops warm air or vapour escaping, and is a poor conductor
Insulated supports and outer caseConductionPoor conductors hold the inner bottle

It works in both directions. It keeps hot drinks hot and cold drinks cold, because it slows energy transfer whichever way it is going.

✗ "The vacuum stops radiation."

✓ Radiation can cross a vacuum. The vacuum stops conduction and convection; the silvering reduces radiation.

Lids and insulation in experiments

Spec 4.10 · 2022 Edexcel 2P Q7(b) · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q10(c)

In any heating experiment, energy lost to the surroundings makes the result less accurate, so insulate the container and put a lid on it.

An open cup of hot water loses energy from its surface by convection and evaporation, and from its sides by conduction and radiation. A lid cuts the losses from the top; lagging or a polystyrene cup cuts them from the sides.

The lid mark script (2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q10(c), 3 marks)

Putting a lid on the cup:

  1. reduces the energy transfer by convection and radiation (evaporation also accepted);
  2. so the equilibrium temperature is higher;
  3. and it takes longer to cool to room temperature.

2022's improvement question (Q7(b)) accepted adding insulation, a lid, or a container with low thermal conductivity, such as a polystyrene cup.

03Cheat sheet

The sentences behind most of the marks

Direction: energy is transferred from the hotter object to the colder one until they reach the same temperature.

Conduction: particles vibrate more and pass energy to neighbouring particles. Metals are best because of free electrons.

Convection: warmer fluid is less dense and rises; cooler, denser fluid sinks; this sets up a convection current. Only in liquids and gases.

Radiation: infrared electromagnetic waves. Needs no medium, so it is the only way across a vacuum.

Surfaces: dark matt = best absorber and best emitter. Light shiny = poor absorber, poor emitter, good reflector.

Temperature: the hotter an object, the more infrared it emits.

Insulation: trapped air is a poor conductor and cannot form convection currents.

Vacuum flask: vacuum stops conduction and convection; silvering reduces radiation; stopper reduces convection and evaporation.

04Understanding it

These are not exam questions. They are the "why" questions that make the exam questions easy. Try each one properly before opening the answer.

U1

A metal spoon and a wooden spoon have been in the same drawer all night, so they are at the same temperature. Why does the metal one feel colder when you pick it up?

Think about What your hand actually senses: temperature, or energy leaving it?

Show answer

Both spoons are colder than your hand, so energy is transferred from your hand to the spoon.

Metal is a good conductor, thanks to its free electrons. It carries energy away from your skin quickly, so your hand loses energy fast and feels cold.

Wood is an insulator. It takes energy from your skin slowly, so your hand barely cools.

The point: your skin senses the rate of energy transfer, not the object's temperature. Same temperature, different conductors, different feel.

U2

Why can convection happen in water and air but never in a solid block of iron?

Show answer

Convection means the warm material itself moves: it rises because it is less dense, and cooler material sinks to replace it.

In a liquid or a gas the particles are free to move from place to place, so a whole region of warm fluid can flow upwards.

In a solid the particles are fixed in position. They can only vibrate. Nothing can flow, so energy can only pass through the iron by conduction.

U3

"Heat rises" sounds right. Why does it score nothing?

Show answer

Because heat is energy, and energy does not rise. It goes from hot to cold in any direction: sideways along a rod, downwards into a cold floor.

What rises is warm fluid, and it rises for a reason: its particles have moved further apart, so it is less dense than the cooler fluid around it. The cooler, denser fluid sinks and pushes it up.

The fix: always say what rises (the warmer air or water) and why (less dense). The school's marking of this exact question said the mechanism must be named.

U4

A black car gets hotter than a white car in the sun. At night, the black car also cools down faster. How can the same colour make it both heat up and cool down faster?

Show answer

A dark matt surface is a good absorber of infrared and a good emitter of infrared. The two go together.

In the sun, more infrared arrives than the car emits. The black car absorbs more of it, so it warms faster.

At night, there is little infrared arriving. The car is now emitting more than it absorbs, and the black car emits more, so it cools faster.

This is exactly the 2022 satellite question: the black bar warmed faster facing the Sun and cooled faster facing away.

U5

A vacuum flask is advertised for hot coffee. Will it keep an iced drink cold too? Explain.

Show answer

Yes. A vacuum flask does not "keep heat in". It reduces energy transfer, in either direction.

With an iced drink, energy would flow in from the warmer room. The vacuum still blocks conduction and convection, because there are no particles to carry the energy. The silvered walls still reduce radiation, now by reflecting infrared from outside back out. The stopper still stops warm air getting in.

So the drink warms up very slowly. Whatever the direction, the same three features slow it down.

U6

Air can carry energy very well by convection: that is how a heater warms a room. So how can air also be the key ingredient in almost every insulator?

Show answer

Air is a very poor conductor: its particles are far apart, so they rarely pass on vibrations. Its only way of carrying much energy is convection, which needs the air to move.

Insulators like wool, foam, fibreglass and feathers trap the air in tiny pockets. The air cannot flow, so no convection currents can form. Now it is left with conduction only, and it is bad at that.

That is why an empty wall cavity is poor (the air circulates) but a cavity filled with foam is good (the same air, trapped).

U7

On a still, clear day, why does a convection current in the air slow down the melting of snow? Surely moving air should warm it up?

Think about Which air ends up touching the snow?

Show answer

Air touching the snow is cooled by it. Cooler air is denser, so it stays low. Warmer air rises away.

So the convection current sorts the air: the coldest air sits nearest the snow. The temperature difference between the snow and the air touching it is small.

A small temperature difference means a slow rate of energy transfer to the snow, so it takes longer to melt. These were exactly the three marking points in 2023 Edexcel 1P Q8(c).

A wind would be different: it would keep bringing warmer air to the snow. Convection on a still day does the opposite.

U8

Energy reaches us from the Sun across 150 million km of space. Why can it only arrive by radiation, and not by conduction or convection?

Show answer

Space is (almost) a vacuum: there are no particles in it.

Conduction needs particles to vibrate and pass energy on. Convection needs a fluid that can move. With no particles, neither can happen.

Radiation is carried by infrared (electromagnetic) waves, which need no medium at all. So it is the only way across.

2022's satellite question gave a mark for exactly this: convection and conduction play no part outside the spacecraft, because there are no particles there.

D11

A student drops a small purple crystal of potassium manganate(VII) to the bottom of a beaker of cold water, at one side. He heats the beaker gently, directly under the crystal.
