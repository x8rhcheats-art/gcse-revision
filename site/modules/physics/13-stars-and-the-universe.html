01What actually comes up

02How it actually works

Module 9 covered the scale of things (billions of stars in a galaxy, billions of galaxies in the universe) and the basic Doppler effect. This module does two jobs. First, the life story of a star, which is decided almost entirely by its mass. Second, the life story of the universe, which is read from the light that reaches us.

Colour tells you surface temperature

Spec 8.7, 8.8 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q8(a)

A star's colour depends on its surface temperature: blue stars are the hottest and red stars are the coolest.

Think of heating a metal poker. First it glows dull red, then orange, then yellow-white. The hotter it gets, the more of its light is at the blue end of the spectrum. Stars work the same way. So astronomers can classify stars by colour, and the colour sorts them by temperature.

  1. Red: coolest surface (Betelgeuse)
  2. Orange
  3. Yellow: the Sun
  4. White and blue-white (Sirius)
  5. Blue: hottest surface (Rigel)

2023 Jan gave a table with each star's colour and its mass, then asked which star has the highest surface temperature. The mark scheme said "ignore links between mass and temperature".

✗ "Rigel, because it has the biggest mass."
✓ "Rigel, because it is blue, and a star's colour depends on its surface temperature."

Birth: from nebula to main sequence star

Spec 8.9, 7.25 · 2024 Edexcel 1P Q4(a) · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q8(b)

A star forms when a cloud of gas and dust, a nebula, collapses under gravity and gets hot enough for fusion to start.

  1. A nebula is a huge cloud of gas (mostly hydrogen) and dust.
  2. Gravity pulls part of it together, so it collapses.
  3. As it collapses, gravitational potential energy is transferred to the thermal store. The centre gets hotter and denser. This hot ball is a protostar.
  4. When the core is hot enough, nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium begins. The star is now a main sequence star.

2024 asked which energy stores change during the collapse, before the star forms. Work it out: the gas falls inwards, so its gravitational potential store decreases; it heats up, so its thermal store increases. Fusion has not started yet and nothing reacts chemically, so the nuclear and chemical stores stay the same.

"Protostar" is not one of the four stages the spec lists, but the 2023 mark scheme gave a mark for it. Use it.

The main sequence: the long, stable part

Spec 8.9, 7.25 · 2023 Edexcel 2P Q5(d)(i)

A main sequence star releases energy by fusing hydrogen into helium in its core, and it stays stable for most of its life.

Fusion is the energy source for stars (spec 7.25). Not fission, not burning. 2023 offered alpha decay, fission, fusion and gamma decay, and only fusion scored.

Why is it stable? Two effects are balanced. Gravity pulls the star inwards. The energy from fusion heats the core and makes an outward pressure. While the two balance, the star stays the same size. The Sun has been like this for about 4.6 billion years.

The main sequence ends when the hydrogen in the core runs out. What happens next depends on the star's mass.

A star like the Sun: red giant, then white dwarf

Spec 8.9 · 2024 Edexcel 1P Q4(b), (c) · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q8(b)

A star of similar mass to the Sun goes: nebula → main sequence star → red giant → white dwarf.

  1. The hydrogen in the core runs out.
  2. The star expands enormously. Its surface cools, so it glows red. It is now a red giant.
  3. Later the red giant throws off its outer layers as a glowing shell of gas, called a planetary nebula (nothing to do with planets).
  4. The core left behind is a white dwarf: very small, very hot and very dense. No fusion happens in it, so it slowly cools and fades.

Mark script: "discuss the evolution of a star like the Sun"

Began as a nebula (a cloud of gas and dust) · collapsed under gravity to form a protostar · fusion started, making a main sequence star · hydrogen in the core ran out, so it expanded into a red giant · lost its outer layers (planetary nebula), leaving a white dwarf.

2024 asked for the property that decides whether a star becomes a red giant or a red supergiant. The answer was mass, and the scheme said "ignore size, weight".

✗ "Its size." ✗ "Its weight."
✓ "Its mass."

Stars more massive than the Sun

Spec 8.10 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q8(b) · 2024 Edexcel 1P Q4(b)

A star much more massive than the Sun goes: nebula → main sequence star → red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole.

  1. Same start: nebula, protostar, main sequence. A massive main sequence star is hotter and bluer, and uses its hydrogen faster.
  2. When the hydrogen in the core runs out, it swells into a red supergiant.
  3. It then collapses and explodes as a supernova, which for a short time can outshine a whole galaxy.
  4. What is left depends on the mass: a neutron star (extremely dense), or, for the most massive stars, a black hole (gravity so strong that not even light can escape).

Mark script: 2023 Jan, Rigel (21 solar masses) and Sirius (2 solar masses)

Both: began in a nebula · were protostars · are main sequence stars.

Rigel: a high-mass star · becomes a red supergiant · then a supernova · then a neutron star (black hole was condoned).

Sirius: a low-mass star, similar to the Sun · becomes a red giant · eventually a white dwarf.

Six of these ten scored 6 marks. Notice the three "both" marks: say what the stars have in common before you split them.

Absolute magnitude: brightness at a standard distance

Spec 8.11P (Physics-only) · 2024 Edexcel 2P Q1(b)

Absolute magnitude is a measure of how bright a star would be if it were at a standard distance from us.

How bright a star looks depends on two things: how much light it gives out, and how far away it is. A dim star nearby can look brighter than a powerful star far away. To compare the stars themselves, imagine moving them all to the same distance. Their brightness at that distance is the absolute magnitude.

The scale runs backwards: the lower (more negative) the number, the brighter the star. A star of absolute magnitude −5 is far brighter than one of +10.

The 2024 definition was worth 2 marks: brightness (1) at a standard distance (1). "Fixed", "same" and "set" distance were all fine. The standard distance is 10 parsecs (about 32.6 light years), and the scheme said "ignore incorrect distance", so you do not need the number.

✗ "How bright a star is." Not enough: that is only 1 mark.
✓ "The brightness of a star at a standard distance."

The Hertzsprung-Russell (HR) diagram

Spec 8.12P (Physics-only) · 2024 Edexcel 2P Q1(a)

The HR diagram plots absolute magnitude (brightness) against colour or surface temperature, and stars fall into clear groups on it.

How to draw it

Vertical axis: absolute magnitude. Brighter stars at the top (so the numbers get more negative as you go up).

Horizontal axis: colour or surface temperature. Hot blue stars on the left, cool red stars on the right. Temperature decreases from left to right.

Main sequence: a band from top left (hot, bright) to bottom right (cool, dim). The Sun sits in the middle of it.

Red giants and red supergiants: top right. Cool but very bright, because they are huge. Supergiants are highest.

White dwarfs: bottom left. Hot but dim, because they are tiny.

In 2024 the diagram had three labelled regions to match to objects. The list also included black hole, nebula, neutron star and supernova. None of those sit on an HR diagram as a region. The three regions are main sequence, red giants and white dwarfs.

The temperature axis runs the "wrong" way: hot on the left. ✗ Putting blue stars on the right. ✓ Blue and hot on the left, red and cool on the right.

And the scheme "reject mark if more than one line drawn from region". One line per region.

The Big Bang: how the universe began and changed

Spec 8.13P (Physics-only) · 2022 Edexcel 2P Q6(a)

The Big Bang theory says the universe began from a single point that was extremely small, hot and dense, and it has been expanding and cooling ever since.

  1. About 13.8 billion years ago, everything in the universe was in one tiny, extremely hot, dense point.
  2. It began to expand. As it expanded, it cooled.
  3. Over time, matter clumped under gravity to form stars and galaxies.
  4. It is still expanding today.

2022 asked how the size and temperature have changed. Two marks, one word each: expanding (getting larger) and cooling.

There are two main pieces of evidence: red-shift of galaxies and the cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation. The rest of this section is about them.

Red-shift: the Doppler effect for light

Spec 8.15P (Physics-only) · 2023 Edexcel Jan 2P Q8(b)

When a light source moves away from us, the light we receive has a longer wavelength and a lower frequency: it is shifted towards the red end of the spectrum.

This is the Doppler effect from Module 9, applied to light.

  1. The galaxy moves away between sending out one wave crest and the next.
  2. So the crests arrive further apart: the observed wavelength increases.
  3. The speed of light does not change, so by v = f × λ the observed frequency decreases.
  4. Longer wavelength means the light is shifted towards the red end of the spectrum: red-shift.

A source moving towards us gives the opposite: shorter wavelength, higher frequency, a blue-shift.

How do astronomers know the wavelength changed? Elements such as hydrogen absorb or emit light at exact wavelengths. They know those wavelengths from the lab. That is the reference wavelength, λ₀. In light from a galaxy, the same pattern appears, but moved to longer wavelengths.

✗ "The light turns red." It doesn't. It is shifted towards the red end: every wavelength gets longer.
✗ "The light slows down." The speed of light is constant; the wavelength and frequency change.

The red-shift equation

Spec 8.16P (Physics-only) · 2022 Edexcel Jan 2P Q8(a) · 2023 Edexcel 2P Q7(b)(i), (ii)

The fractional change in wavelength equals the galaxy's speed divided by the speed of light.

Δλ ÷ λ₀ = v ÷ c (on the formula sheet)
Δλ = change in wavelength (observed − reference) · λ₀ = reference wavelength · v = velocity of the galaxy · c = speed of light = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s

Δλ and λ₀ just need the same unit as each other (both nm, or both m). v and c need the same unit as each other too (both m/s, or both km/s). You do not need to convert nm to m, because the units cancel in the ratio.

Worked: 2023 Edexcel 2P Q7(b)

Reference wavelength 630 nm, observed 645 nm.

(i) Δλ = 645 − 630 = 15 nm

(ii) 15 ÷ 630 = v ÷ (3.0 × 10⁸) → v = (15 ÷ 630) × 3.0 × 10⁸ = 7.1 × 10⁶ m/s

Worked: 2022 Edexcel Jan 2P Q8(a)

Galaxy moves away at 3.9 × 10⁴ km/s; c = 3.0 × 10⁵ km/s; emitted wavelength 6.2 × 10⁻⁷ m.

(i) Δλ = 6.2 × 10⁻⁷ × (3.9 × 10⁴ ÷ 3.0 × 10⁵) = 8.1 × 10⁻⁸ m

(ii) Received wavelength = 6.2 × 10⁻⁷ + 8.1 × 10⁻⁸ = 7.0 × 10⁻⁷ m. It is longer, because the galaxy is moving away.

✗ Dividing by the observed wavelength (645 nm). That gave 7.0 × 10⁶ m/s and 1 mark at most.
✓ Divide by the reference wavelength, λ₀ = 630 nm.

✗ Giving Δλ as the answer to "calculate the wavelength received". ✓ Add Δλ back on to λ₀.

Red-shift and distance: why the universe is expanding

Spec 8.17P, 8.18P (Physics-only) · 2022 Edexcel Jan 2P Q8(b) · 2023 Edexcel 2P Q7(b)(iii) · 2023 Edexcel Jan 2P Q8(b) · 2022 Edexcel 2P Q6(b)

Light from almost all distant galaxies is red-shifted, and the further away a galaxy is, the greater its red-shift, so the further away, the faster it is moving away: the universe is expanding.

  1. Light from distant galaxies is red-shifted, so they are moving away from us.
  2. The further away the galaxy, the greater the red-shift.
  3. A greater red-shift means the galaxy is moving away faster.
  4. So the further away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away. Speed is directly proportional to distance.
  5. This means the galaxies are all moving apart: the universe is expanding.
  6. Run the expansion backwards and everything was once at a single point. That is the Big Bang.

Mark script: 2023 2P Q7(b)(iii), two marks

"Galaxy B has twice the red-shift of galaxy A and is twice as far away."

Twice the red-shift means twice the speed (1) · so the further away a galaxy is, the faster it moves away, meaning galaxies are moving apart (1).

The scheme said to "ignore references to single point/singularity, universe expansion, Big Bang itself" here. On this part, the marks were only for linking red-shift, speed and distance.

The 2022 scheme accepted "star" for "galaxy", but don't rely on it. Say galaxy.

✗ "The galaxies are red-shifted, so the Big Bang happened." That skips the steps that carry the marks.
✓ "The further away the galaxy, the greater the red-shift, so the faster it is moving away. So the universe is expanding, and must have started from a single point."

The cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation

Spec 8.14P (Physics-only) · 2022 Edexcel 2P Q6(b)

The CMB is microwave radiation that reaches us from every direction in space; it is left over from the hot early universe, stretched to longer wavelengths as the universe expanded.

  1. Just after the Big Bang the universe was extremely hot and gave out high-energy, short-wavelength radiation.
  2. As the universe expanded, that radiation was stretched: its wavelength increased.
  3. Today it has reached the microwave part of the spectrum.
  4. It is almost the same in all directions. That suggests all parts of the universe were once in contact, at a single hot point.

Mark script: 2022 2P Q6(b), "discuss two pieces of evidence" (6 marks)

Up to four marks on each piece of evidence.

CMB: name the cosmic microwave background radiation · it is the same in all directions / everywhere · so all parts of the universe were in contact long ago · its wavelength has increased as the universe expanded · the universe was much hotter long ago.

Red-shift: name the red-shift of galaxies · the further away the galaxy, the greater the red-shift · greater red-shift means faster · so the further away, the faster it moves away · galaxies moving apart means expansion from a single point.

Write at least three points on each. A six-mark answer on only one piece of evidence can score at most four.

2023 Jan asked why a red-shifted galaxy is evidence for the Big Bang, and the scheme said "ignore CMBR". Answer the evidence you were given.

04Understanding it

These are "why" questions. Try each one properly before opening the answer.

U1

Betelgeuse is red and Rigel is blue. Both are in the constellation Orion. Which has the hotter surface, and why can you tell just by looking?

Show answer

Rigel. A star's colour depends on its surface temperature.

Anything hot glows. As it gets hotter, more of its light is at shorter wavelengths, towards the blue end. A cooler surface gives off more red light. So a blue star has a hotter surface than a red one.

That is why colour can be used to classify stars: it sorts them by surface temperature without anyone needing to visit them.

U2

A main sequence star stays the same size for billions of years, even though gravity is pulling all its gas inwards. Why doesn't it collapse?

Show answer

Fusion in the core releases a huge amount of energy. That keeps the core very hot and produces an outward pressure.

Gravity pulls inwards. While the outward pressure balances gravity, the star stays stable.

When the hydrogen in the core runs out, the balance changes, and the star leaves the main sequence. That is why "the hydrogen runs out" is the trigger for the red giant or red supergiant stage.

U3

Two stars form in the same nebula at the same time. One has the mass of the Sun, the other has 20 times as much. Explain why they end up so differently.

Show answer

Their early lives are the same: nebula, protostar, main sequence. The difference is mass.

The Sun-like star becomes a red giant, sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula, and leaves a white dwarf.

The massive star becomes a red supergiant, explodes as a supernova, and leaves a neutron star or, if it is massive enough, a black hole.

This is why 2024 asked for "the property that determines" the path: the one-word answer is mass. Size and weight were ignored.

U4

On the HR diagram, white dwarfs are hotter than the Sun but much dimmer. Red giants are cooler than the Sun but much brighter. How can both be true?

Show answer

How bright a star is depends on its temperature and on its size.

A white dwarf is very hot, so each bit of its surface is bright. But it is tiny, about the size of the Earth, so there is very little surface. Total: dim. That puts it bottom left.

A red giant is cooler, so each bit of surface gives out less. But it is enormous, so it has a vast surface. Total: very bright. That puts it top right.

So on the HR diagram, stars above the main sequence are bigger than main sequence stars of the same colour, and stars below it are smaller.

U5

Why do astronomers bother with absolute magnitude? Why not just measure how bright each star looks?

Show answer

How bright a star looks from Earth depends on how far away it is, not just on how much light it gives out. A modest star close by can look brighter than a giant far away.

Absolute magnitude removes the distance problem. It is the brightness the star would have at a standard distance. Now every star is compared fairly, like measuring everyone's height standing on the same floor.

That is why the HR diagram uses absolute magnitude. Using how bright stars look would scatter them randomly, and the main sequence would vanish.

U6

Almost every galaxy is moving away from us. Does that mean the Earth is at the centre of the universe?

Show answer

No. Think of a loaf of raisin bread rising in the oven. Every raisin moves away from every other raisin. A raisin twice as far away moves away twice as fast, because there is twice as much dough between them expanding.

From any raisin, all the others seem to rush away. There is nothing special about the one you are on.

It is the same with galaxies. From any galaxy, the others seem to move away, faster the further away they are. That pattern (speed proportional to distance) is exactly what you expect if the whole universe is expanding. That is why it supports the Big Bang.

U7

The early universe was extremely hot, so the radiation it gave out had very short wavelengths. So why is the leftover radiation now in the microwave region?

Show answer

Because the universe has been expanding ever since, and the radiation has been stretched with it.

As space expands, the wavelength of the radiation travelling through it increases. Over billions of years it has been stretched from very short wavelengths all the way to microwaves.

So the CMB is evidence for two things at once: that the universe was once very hot, and that it has expanded. The 2022 scheme gave marks for both ideas.

U8

In Δλ ÷ λ₀ = v ÷ c, why must λ₀ be the reference wavelength and not the observed one? And why can you leave both wavelengths in nanometres?

Show answer

λ₀ is the wavelength the light had when it was emitted. The equation compares the change with that starting value. The observed wavelength already contains the change, so dividing by it gives the wrong fraction. In 2023 that cost all but 1 mark.

The left side is a ratio of two wavelengths. If both are in nm, the nm cancel, and you get a plain number. The same goes for v ÷ c: if c is in km/s, v comes out in km/s.

Example: a hydrogen line with λ₀ = 656 nm is observed at 663 nm. Δλ = 7 nm, so v = (7 ÷ 656) × 3.0 × 10⁸ ≈ 3.2 × 10⁶ m/s, moving away.

D9

A galaxy moves away from the Earth at a speed of 2.4 × 10⁴ km/s. The speed of light is 3.0 × 10⁵ km/s. Light from the galaxy is emitted with a wavelength of 5.0 × 10⁻⁷ m.

(b) Calculate the wavelength of the light received on Earth. (1)

Show answer

= 4.0 × 10⁻⁸ m 1

Show answer

Cosmic microwave background radiation:

  • There is cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation 1
  • It is the same in all directions, which suggests all parts of the universe were once in contact 1

Red-shift:
