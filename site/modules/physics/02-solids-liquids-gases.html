02How it actually works

The biggest block on the paper — about 48 of 255 marks — and it arrives in pieces rather than one question, which makes it easy to underestimate. More marks are lost here to unit conversion and to imprecise wording than to not understanding the physics. Every heading says where it was asked.

Particles in solids, liquids and gases

Spec 5.10P · 2023 Q5(a)(b) · 2025 Q3(b) · Mock 1 Q3(b) — asked in some form every year

Two things get asked separately and score separately: the arrangement and the motion. Answer both, in those terms.

ArrangementMotion
SolidClosely packed, regular patternVibrate about fixed positions
LiquidClosely packed, but random / irregularSlide or flow past one another
GasFar apart, randomMove quickly and randomly in all directions

Two specific phrases cost the year group marks.

Liquids are still closely packed. 2023's examiner noted students "regularly lost the third mark by stating the opposite". Going from solid to liquid changes the order, not really the spacing — that is why melting barely changes the volume, while boiling changes it enormously.

For liquid motion, "move freely" was ignored. The marks needed slide, flow, or move past each other. "The particles move" without "past each other" scores nothing.

Changes of state, and the flat bit on the graph

Spec 5.8P, 5.9P, 5.11P · Mock 2 Q9(c)

Heat a solid steadily and plot temperature against time. The graph rises, then goes flat while it melts, then rises again, then goes flat again while it boils.

Energy is being supplied throughout, so why does the temperature stop rising? Because the energy is doing a different job:

  • On the slopes: the energy increases the kinetic energy of the particles — they move faster — and temperature is a measure of that. So the temperature rises.
  • On the flat sections: the energy is used to break the bonds, or overcome the forces of attraction, between the particles. It is not increasing their kinetic energy, so the temperature stays constant while the state changes.

That is the three-mark answer, and all three marks are in that second bullet: energy breaks the bonds 1, rather than increasing kinetic energy 1, so the temperature stays constant during the change of state 1.

Density, and the displacement method

Spec 5.3, 5.4 · 2024 Q2(b)(c) · Mock 2 Q2(a)(b)

ρ = m ÷ V
ρ = density in kg/m³ (or g/cm³) · m = mass in kg (or g) · V = volume in m³ (or cm³)

Finding the volume of a regular shape is just multiplying its dimensions. The exam is far more interested in an irregular object, where you use displacement:

  1. Part-fill a measuring cylinder with water and record the volume.
  2. Lower the object in fully submerged, on a thread if needed so it doesn't splash.
  3. Record the new volume and subtract. The difference is the object's volume.

Mass comes from a balance, zeroed first. Accuracy marks come from details: fully submerged, no splashing, read at eye level to avoid parallax.

Watch the units the answer is demanded in. g/cm³ and kg/m³ are both legitimate but differ by a factor of 1000. If a question gives grams and cubic centimetres and asks for g/cm³, do not convert anything.

2024's final mark was awarded specifically for giving the answer to 2 significant figures. As the examiner put it: 2 s.f. is not 2 decimal places.

Pressure, and pressure in a liquid

Spec 5.5, 5.6, 5.7 · Mock 2 Q2(c)(d) · 2025 Q6(c)

P = F ÷ A
P = pressure in pascals (Pa) · F = force in newtons (N) · A = area in m²

One pascal is one newton per square metre. The same force spread over a smaller area gives a greater pressure — which is why a drawing pin has a sharp point and a snowshoe is wide.

Pressure at a point in a liquid or gas at rest acts equally in all directions (spec 5.6), not just downwards. That is why a submerged object is squeezed from every side, and why the collapsing-bottle question works.

ΔP = h × g × ρ
ΔP = pressure difference in pascals (Pa) · h = height (depth) in metres (m) · g = gravitational field strength in N/kg (use 10) · ρ = density in kg/m³

Pressure increases with depth simply because there is a greater height of liquid above pressing down. Note this equation needs kg/m³ and metres — it is the one place where converting from g/cm³ genuinely matters.

How a gas exerts pressure

Spec 5.15 · 2025 Q6(c) · Mock 1 Q6(b) — and the examiner refused a whole year group's wording

Learn this as four beats, in order. Each is a mark.

  1. The molecules (or particles) move quickly and randomly in all directions
  2. They collide with the walls of the container
  3. Each collision exerts a force on the wall
  4. Pressure = force ÷ area, so those collisions produce a pressure

The 2025 examiner: the collision mark was only given if molecules or particles were named — "they hit the walls" scored nothing. The word "they" is not good enough; name what is hitting.

And "the pressure = force/area point had to be linked into the answer, not just written down". Write the fourth beat as a sentence that connects the collisions to the pressure, not as a formula floating at the end.

Temperature, kelvin and absolute zero

Spec 5.16, 5.17, 5.18, 5.19 · 2025 Q7(a)(b)(i) · Mock 1 Q6(a)

K = °C + 273

Raising the temperature increases the average kinetic energy of the molecules, so they move faster. Cool a gas down and they move more slowly — and at −273 °C they would have essentially no kinetic energy left. You cannot remove energy that isn't there, so no lower temperature is possible. That is absolute zero, and it is where the Kelvin scale starts.

The reason the Kelvin scale matters: the kelvin temperature of a gas is proportional to the average kinetic energy of its molecules. Double the kelvin temperature and you double the average kinetic energy. Celsius has no such property — its zero is an arbitrary point about water, so doubling 10 °C to 20 °C does not double anything physical.

Say proportional to the kinetic energy. 2025's examiner made the point that temperature is not kinetic energy — it is a measure that is proportional to the average.

The two gas laws

Spec 5.20, 5.21, 5.22 · Mock 1 Q6(c)(d) · Mock 2 Q9(a)(b)

Both apply to a fixed mass of gas, and each holds one thing constant.

Held constantEquationParticle explanation for the change
Volume (a sealed rigid container)P₁ ÷ T₁ = P₂ ÷ T₂  (T in kelvin)Higher temperature → greater average kinetic energy → particles hit the walls more often and harder
Temperature (squeezing a syringe)P₁V₁ = P₂V₂The same number of particles in a smaller volume → they hit the walls more often, but not harder

The two explanations are different and the difference is examined. In the constant-temperature case the particles do not speed up — the temperature has not changed, so the average kinetic energy has not changed. Only the frequency of collisions increases, because the walls are closer. Mock 2's mark scheme calls saying they move faster "a common and costly error".

Kelvin is compulsory for the pressure–temperature law. Using Celsius gives a badly wrong answer, because the equation is a proportionality and only the Kelvin scale has a true zero. The pressure–volume law has no temperature in it, so no conversion arises.

Specific heat capacity

Spec 5.12P, 5.13P · 2023 Q5(c) · 2024 Q6(b) · 2025 Q7(b)(iii) · Mock 1 Q7 · Mock 2 Q7 — the most-asked calculation in the module

ΔQ = m × c × ΔT
ΔQ = change in thermal energy in joules (J) · m = mass in kilograms (kg) · c = specific heat capacity in J/kg °C · ΔT = change in temperature in °C

Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to raise the temperature of one kilogram of a substance by one degree Celsius. Water's is unusually large at 4200 J/kg °C, which is why the sea takes so long to warm up and why water is used in heating systems.

A large c means a substance needs a lot of energy per degree, so it heats up slowly. A small c heats up quickly — which is the answer to "the same energy is supplied to oil instead of water; what happens?" The oil, with the smaller c, reaches the higher temperature.

Three conversions account for most lost marks here, and each has been specifically flagged:

Grams to kilograms. 2023's examiner: "most marks lost were due to power-of-ten errors — students failing to convert from g to kg". 16 g is 0.016 kg.

Minutes to seconds, when energy comes from power × time. Mock 2: 5.0 minutes is 300 s — "using 5 gives an answer 60 times too small".

ΔT is a difference, not a temperature. From 16 °C to 65 °C is ΔT = 49, not 65. And because it is a difference, a change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K — so you never convert temperature here, unlike in the gas laws.

2025 also asked it rearranged for mass. Practise making every symbol the subject: m = ΔQ ÷ (c × ΔT) and c = ΔQ ÷ (m × ΔT).

Measuring specific heat capacity

Spec 5.14P · 2024 Q6(a) — 5 marks · Mock 2 Q7(a) — 5 marks

A five-mark "describe the investigation" question in two separate papers, so it is worth having a rehearsed answer. For a metal block with an immersion heater:

  1. Measure the mass of the block on a balance
  2. Insert the heater and thermometer into the holes in the block, and record the starting temperature
  3. Switch on the heater and start the stopwatch at the same moment
  4. After a measured time, switch off and record the highest temperature reached
  5. Calculate the energy supplied from E = P × t, then use c = E ÷ (m × ΔT)
  6. Insulate the block with lagging to reduce energy transfer to the surroundings

Point 4 has a reason worth knowing: the temperature keeps rising for a while after switching off, because energy is still spreading through the block. Taking the reading too early makes ΔT too small.

Why the answer usually comes out too high: some energy is transferred to the surroundings instead of into the block. The calculation assumes all of the electrical energy went into the block, so it credits the block with more energy than it actually received — and c = E ÷ (mΔT) with an overstated E gives an overstated c. That is a two-mark explanation and it comes up.

Thermal energy transfer and cooling curves

Spec 5.8P · 2023 Q8 — 11 marks

Put hot water and cold water in contact and thermal energy is transferred from the hotter to the colder, until both reach the same temperature — thermal equilibrium. On a temperature–time graph the hot line falls, the cold line rises, both curve, and they level off towards a common temperature.

The curves flatten because the rate of transfer depends on the temperature difference: as the two get closer, the transfer slows.

2023 wanted the final temperature closer to the cold water's starting temperature, because there was much more cold water. The amount of each matters, not just the temperatures.

And "'heat rises' scores nothing — name the transfer mechanism." Say conduction (through the metal), convection, radiation, or evaporation. Better insulation reduces the transfer to the surroundings, so the equilibrium temperature is higher and the cooling takes longer.

Temperature

Absolute zero is −273 °C, or 0 K. It is the temperature at which particles have minimum kinetic energy and stop moving. In both gas law equations the temperature must be in kelvin — this is the most common way to lose all the marks on an otherwise correct calculation.

Particles — the exact wording

StateArrangementMotion
SolidClose together / touching, in a regular pattern or latticeVibrate about fixed positions
LiquidClose together / touching, but randomly arrangedSlide, flow, or move past one another
GasFar apart, randomly arrangedMove quickly in all directions, at random

The liquid row is where marks die. The examiner on 2025 Q3b: "For motion, really needed to say slide / flow / move past / move around — something to suggest a degree of contact between particles."

"The particles move" scores nothing. "The particles move past each other" scores. One word.

Also from 2023: correct candidates on the solid-versus-liquid comparison "usually referred to liquids being incompressible" — a detail worth having.

How a gas exerts pressure — learn this as four beats

  1. Gas molecules move rapidly and randomly in all directions.
  2. They collide with the walls of the container.
  3. Each collision changes the molecule's momentum, so the wall exerts a force on the molecule and the molecule exerts a force on the wall.
  4. Pressure is force per unit area, so the total force from many collisions over the wall's area gives the pressure.

Say "molecules" or "particles", never "they". Reference P = F ÷ A and use it in the sentence rather than writing it in the margin — the examiner refused the mark for the equation floating unattached.

One more line the specification asks for: because the molecules move randomly in every direction, the pressure at a point in a gas or a liquid at rest acts equally in all directions — not just downwards. That is why a bottle is crushed inwards from every side, and why depth alone fixes the pressure in a liquid.

The two gas laws at particle level

From 2025: "Temperature is directly proportional to kinetic energy in Kelvin (temperature is not kinetic energy)." The kelvin temperature of a gas is proportional to the average kinetic energy of its molecules. It is not the same thing as it.

Specific heat capacity

Definition: the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C.

Water's is 4200 J/kg °C — unusually high, which is why water heats slowly, cools slowly, and is used in central heating and cooling systems. A substance with a low SHC changes temperature by a lot for the same energy input. That is the whole answer to the 2024 "why does the air heat more than the water" question.

Two practicals you must be able to describe

Density of an irregular solid

  • Measure mass on a balance, in grams or kilograms
  • Part-fill a measuring cylinder with water and record the volume
  • Lower the object in fully; record the new volume
  • Volume of object = final volume − initial volume (displacement)
  • ρ = m ÷ V

If the object floats, hold it under with a thin pin or use a sinker. If it's soluble, use a liquid it doesn't dissolve in.

Specific heat capacity of water

  • Measure the mass of water using a balance
  • Record the starting temperature with a thermometer
  • Switch the heater on and start a stopwatch simultaneously
  • After a measured time, record the final temperature
  • Energy supplied = power × time; then c = ΔQ ÷ (m × ΔT)
  • Insulate the beaker with a lid and lagging to reduce energy transfer to the surroundings

The insulation point is almost always worth a mark, as is stating that some energy is lost to the surroundings so the measured value is higher than the true value.

04Understanding it

U1

A sealed rigid steel can of gas is heated. The volume cannot change. Explain, at particle level, exactly why the pressure rises — and identify two separate reasons.

Show answer

Heating increases the average kinetic energy of the molecules, so they move faster. That produces two distinct effects, and good answers name both:

One: faster molecules cross the container more quickly, so they hit the walls more often — more collisions per second.

Two: each molecule now arrives with more momentum, so each collision exerts a larger force.

More frequent and harder collisions mean a greater total force on the same area, so by P = F ÷ A the pressure rises.

Most students give only the first reason. The second is where the extra mark lives.

U2

Ice, water and steam are all H₂O. When ice melts at 0 °C, energy is supplied but the temperature doesn't change. Where is the energy going?

Show answer

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles. During melting, the energy supplied is not increasing that — it is going into breaking the bonds between particles, overcoming the forces of attraction that hold them in a fixed lattice.

So the energy is being transferred to the potential energy of the particles, not their kinetic energy. The particles gain freedom to move past each other, but not extra speed.

On a temperature–time graph this shows as a horizontal plateau. There is one at the melting point and a longer one at the boiling point — longer because boiling requires breaking all the remaining bonds rather than just loosening them.

U3

Two identical beakers each receive 10,000 J of energy. One contains 1 kg of water (c = 4200), the other 1 kg of copper (c = 385).

Which gets hotter, and by how much? Then explain why saucepans are made of metal but filled with water.

Show answer

Rearranging: ΔT = ΔQ ÷ (m × c).

Water: 10 000 ÷ (1 × 4200) = 2.4 °C

Copper: 10 000 ÷ (1 × 385) = 26 °C

The copper gets hotter by roughly eleven times as much, because its specific heat capacity is about eleven times smaller.

The saucepan: the metal has a low SHC so it heats up rapidly for little energy, transferring heat to the food quickly. The water has a high SHC so it holds a lot of energy and its temperature stays stable, cooking food evenly rather than scorching it.

This is the same reasoning as the 2024 question about air heating more than water. Low SHC means big temperature change.

U4

A sealed plastic bottle is closed at high altitude where the air pressure is low. On landing, it is crushed. Why? And why would a rigid glass bottle not be?

Show answer

When sealed, the pressure inside equals the outside pressure at that altitude — low. Descending, the outside pressure rises, but the trapped air inside stays at the low pressure.

There is now a pressure difference, with greater pressure outside than inside. Pressure acting on the bottle's surface area produces a force pushing inwards, larger than the outward force from the air inside. The resultant inward force crushes the bottle.

A rigid glass bottle doesn't collapse because glass is strong enough to withstand that resultant force without deforming — but the pressure difference is still there.

The 2025 examiner: "If missed MP2 its because they didn't specify that it was force causing it to collapse — instead kept saying pressure difference. Some didn't specify where the greater pressure was."

So: name the pressure difference, say which side is greater, and say that it produces a force.

U5

Why do you need to convert to kelvin for the gas laws, but not for the specific heat capacity equation?

Show answer

The gas laws involve ratios of temperature. Doubling the kelvin temperature genuinely doubles the average kinetic energy — because the kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, where kinetic energy is zero. Going from 10 °C to 20 °C is not doubling anything physically; it's 283 K to 293 K, a rise of about 3.5%.

The SHC equation uses ΔT, a change in temperature. A rise of 20 °C is a rise of 20 K — the two scales have identical interval sizes, so the difference is the same number either way.

Rule of thumb: absolute temperature needs kelvin, temperature change doesn't care. The 2023 examiner noted students converting ΔT to kelvin, which was harmless but unnecessary.

U6

A liquid flows and takes the shape of its container, while a solid holds its shape. Yet the mark scheme insists that a liquid's particles are still closely packed. How can both be true?

Show answer

Because flowing is about order, not about spacing.

In a solid the particles are closely packed and in a regular, fixed pattern, so each one vibrates about a fixed position and the shape is held.

In a liquid the particles are still touching — essentially as close together as before — but the regular pattern has broken down. They are randomly arranged, so they can slide past one another. Close together, but not locked in place.

The evidence is in the volumes. Melting ice changes its volume by only a few per cent, because the spacing barely changes. Boiling water expands it about 1600 times, because that is when the particles genuinely move far apart. If liquids were not closely packed, melting would cause a huge expansion too.

2023's examiner noted students "regularly lost the third mark by stating the opposite". Say closely packed and randomly arranged.

U7

A syringe of gas is compressed to half its volume at constant temperature, and the pressure doubles. A student explains: "the particles are squashed together so they move faster and hit the walls harder." Two parts of that are wrong. Which, and what is the correct explanation?

Show answer

Wrong 1 — they do not move faster. The temperature is constant, and temperature is a measure of average kinetic energy. If the particles sped up, the temperature would have risen. It hasn't, so they haven't.

Wrong 2 — they do not hit harder. The force of each individual collision depends on how fast the particle is moving, which is unchanged.

What actually changes is how often they hit. The same number of particles now occupies half the space, so the walls are closer and each particle reaches a wall more frequently. More collisions per second on each square metre means a greater total force per unit area — which is a greater pressure.

Contrast with heating at constant volume: there the particles genuinely do speed up, so they hit both more often and harder. Two different laws, two different explanations, and the exam tests whether you know which is which.

Mock 2 calls "they move faster" here "a common and costly error".

U8

The pressure at the bottom of a swimming pool depends on the depth, but not on how wide the pool is. Why not — surely a wider pool holds far more water pressing down?

Show answer

It does hold more water — but that extra weight is spread over a proportionally larger area of floor, so the force per unit area is unchanged.

Pressure is force ÷ area. Doubling the width of the pool doubles the weight of water and doubles the floor area at the same time, and the two cancel exactly.

You can see it in the equation: ΔP = h × g × ρ contains the depth, the gravitational field strength and the density — and no term for width, area or total volume at all. The only things that matter are how deep you are and what the liquid is made of.

The practical consequence: a dam's thickness is determined by the depth of the reservoir, not by how many kilometres of lake stretch out behind it.

U9

A student measuring the specific heat capacity of a metal block gets a value higher than the accepted one. Their arithmetic is correct. Explain why the method pushes the answer the wrong way — and why it is always too high rather than sometimes too low.

Show answer

The calculation assumes all the electrical energy supplied went into the block. In reality some is transferred to the surroundings — through the air, the bench and the wires.

So the energy actually used to raise the block's temperature was less than the figure used in the sum. Since c = E ÷ (m × ΔT), an overstated E gives an overstated c.

Why always too high: because the error is one-directional. Energy always leaks out of a block that is hotter than the room — it never leaks in. So the measured temperature rise is always smaller than the supplied energy would suggest, and the calculated c is always too large.

How to reduce it: insulate the block with lagging, and keep the temperature rise modest so there is less time and less temperature difference for losses to occur.

This "suggest why" question is worth two marks and appears in both mocks. Naming the direction of the error and linking it to the equation is what earns them.

U10

You must convert to kelvin for the pressure–temperature gas law, but you must not convert for the specific heat capacity equation. Why the difference?

Show answer

Because one uses a temperature and the other uses a temperature difference.

The gas law is a proportionality. P₁ ÷ T₁ = P₂ ÷ T₂ says pressure is proportional to temperature, and a proportionality only makes sense measured from a true zero. Celsius zero is arbitrary — it is about water freezing, not about particles stopping — so "twice 10 °C" means nothing physical. Kelvin zero is the point where molecular kinetic energy is essentially zero, so doubling the kelvin temperature really does double the average kinetic energy.

The SHC equation uses ΔT, a change. A rise of 20 degrees is the same size on both scales, because the two scales have identical-sized divisions and only differ in where they start. 20 °C of change is 20 K of change, so converting achieves nothing — and converting the two temperatures separately before subtracting also works, since the 273s cancel.

The rule: a single temperature in a proportionality needs kelvin. A temperature difference never does.

U11

Water has a specific heat capacity of 4200 J/kg °C; copper's is 385 J/kg °C. Explain what this means physically, and give two everyday consequences.

Show answer

It means one kilogram of water needs 4200 joules to warm by one degree, while one kilogram of copper needs only 385 — about eleven times less. Water is unusually resistant to changing its temperature.

Consequence 1 — coastal climates are milder. The sea absorbs enormous amounts of energy in summer while warming only slightly, then releases it slowly in winter. Places near large bodies of water have smaller temperature swings than places inland.

Consequence 2 — water is used as a coolant and in heating systems. Central heating pipes and car radiators carry water because it can pick up a great deal of energy from the boiler or engine without its own temperature shooting up, then release it elsewhere.

A third, if you want it: a copper saucepan heats up almost instantly on the hob while the water in it takes minutes — same energy going in, wildly different temperature rises.

The exam version of this is "the same energy is supplied to oil, which has a lower specific heat capacity — what happens?" The oil gets hotter, because less energy is needed per kilogram per degree.

05Exam drill

D1

A metal block has a mass of 1.35 kg and measures 5.0 cm × 6.0 cm × 10.0 cm.

Calculate its density in kg/m³. (4)

Show answer

Volume = 5.0 × 6.0 × 10.0 = 300 cm³ 1

Convert: 300 cm³ = 300 ÷ 1 000 000 = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³ 1

ρ = m ÷ V = 1.35 ÷ 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ 1

= 4500 kg/m³ 1

1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³, not 100. Three dimensions, so the conversion factor is cubed. This is the single nastiest unit conversion in the syllabus.

D2

Calculate the energy needed to raise the temperature of 250 g of water from 18 °C to 88 °C. (3)
(c for water = 4200 J/kg °C)

Show answer

250 g = 0.25 kg, and ΔT = 88 − 18 = 70 °C 1

ΔQ = m × c × ΔT = 0.25 × 4200 × 70 1

= 73 500 J, or 7.35 × 10⁴ J 1

Two conversions hidden in one line: grams to kilograms, and the temperature change rather than the final temperature. Both are on the examiner's list of repeat offences.

D3

A gas occupies 250 cm³ at a pressure of 100 kPa. It is compressed at constant temperature to 80 cm³.

Calculate the new pressure. (3)

Show answer

p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ 1

100 × 250 = p₂ × 80 1

p₂ = 25 000 ÷ 80 = 312.5 kPa, or 313 kPa to 3 s.f. 1

Units of volume and pressure don't need converting here because they appear on both sides and cancel — as long as you're consistent. That is only true for Boyle's law. It is never true for temperature.

D4

A fixed mass of gas at constant volume is at 27 °C and a pressure of 150 kPa. It is heated to 127 °C.

Calculate the new pressure. (4)

Show answer

Convert both temperatures: T₁ = 27 + 273 = 300 K, T₂ = 127 + 273 = 400 K 1

p₁ ÷ T₁ = p₂ ÷ T₂ 1

150 ÷ 300 = p₂ ÷ 400 1

p₂ = 0.5 × 400 = 200 kPa 1

Had you used Celsius you'd have got 150 × 127 ÷ 27 = 706 kPa. Wrong by a factor of three and a half.

D5

Describe the arrangement and motion of the particles in a gas, and explain how this causes the gas to exert pressure on the walls of its container. (5)

Show answer

Particles are far apart and randomly arranged 1

They move quickly, in all directions, at random 1

The particles collide with the walls of the container 1

Each collision exerts a force on the wall 1

Pressure = force ÷ area, so many collisions over the wall area produce a pressure 1

Write "particles" or "molecules" every time. Not "they", not "it".

D6

A swimming pool is 2.4 m deep. The density of water is 1000 kg/m³ and g = 10 N/kg.

Calculate the pressure due to the water at the bottom of the pool. (3)

Show answer

ΔP = h × g × ρ 1

= 2.4 × 10 × 1000 1

= 24 000 Pa, or 24 kPa 1

Note this is the pressure due to the water alone. The total pressure at the bottom also includes atmospheric pressure pushing down on the surface — read carefully which the question wants.

D7

A student pours hot water into a metal container, and stands it in a plastic cup of cold water.

(a) Sketch how the temperature of each body of water varies with time. (3)
(b) Explain why the temperatures change, referring to types of thermal energy transfer. (4)

Show answer

(a) Hot water curve falls, steeply at first then levelling 1. Cold water curve rises, steeply at first then levelling 1. The two curves approach and meet at a common final temperature, between the two starting values 1

(b)

  • Energy is transferred from the hotter water to the cooler water, because energy always flows from higher to lower temperature 1
  • Conduction through the metal container wall — particles vibrate more and pass energy to neighbouring particles; metals conduct well because of free electrons 1
  • Convection within each body of water — warmer water is less dense, rises, cooler water sinks to replace it, setting up a current 1
  • The rate of transfer decreases as the temperature difference decreases, which is why both curves level off 1

The 2023 examiner noted answers where "the rationale behind their response is in the wrong order". Energy flows because of a temperature difference — say that first, then name the mechanisms.

D8

A student determines the specific heat capacity of a metal block using an immersion heater of power 45 W.

Describe the investigation the student should carry out. (5)

Show answer

Any five 11111

  • Measure the mass of the block using a balance
  • Insert the immersion heater and a thermometer into the holes in the block
  • Record the starting temperature
  • Switch on the heater and start the stopwatch at the same moment
  • After a measured time, switch off and record the highest temperature reached
  • Calculate the energy supplied using E = P × t
  • Calculate the specific heat capacity using c = E ÷ (m × ΔT)
  • Insulate the block with lagging to reduce energy transfer to the surroundings

Recording the highest temperature matters: the block keeps warming for a while after the heater is switched off, as energy spreads through it. Reading too early makes ΔT too small.

D9

The block in D8 has a mass of 0.80 kg. The heater runs for 4.0 minutes and the temperature rises by 28 °C.

(a) Calculate the energy supplied by the heater. (3)
(b) Calculate the specific heat capacity of the metal. (3)

Show answer

(a) E = P × t 1

t = 4.0 × 60 = 240 s 1

E = 45 × 240 = 10 800 J 1

(b) ΔQ = m × c × ΔT, so c = ΔQ ÷ (m × ΔT) 1

c = 10 800 ÷ (0.80 × 28) 1

= 480 J/kg °C (2 s.f.) 1

The minutes-to-seconds conversion is the whole of the first mark in (a). Mock 2's mark scheme puts it bluntly: using 4 instead of 240 gives an answer 60 times too small.

D10

A student investigates a small irregular stone.

(a) Describe how the student could find the volume of the stone using a measuring cylinder. (3)
(b) The stone has a mass of 94 g and a volume of 36 cm³. Calculate its density in g/cm³, giving your answer to 2 significant figures. (3)

Show answer

(a) Part-fill the measuring cylinder with water and record the volume 1

Lower the stone in until it is fully submerged, using a thread if necessary 1

Record the new volume and subtract the first reading to find the volume of the stone 1

(b) ρ = m ÷ V 1

= 94 ÷ 36 = 2.611… 1

= 2.6 g/cm³ (2 s.f.) 1

The answer is demanded in g/cm³ and the data is in grams and cubic centimetres — so no conversion at all. Converting to kg/m³ here would be wrong.

In 2024 the final mark was awarded specifically for 2 significant figures. As the examiner put it, 2 s.f. is not 2 decimal places: 2.61 is three significant figures and does not score.

D11

(a) Describe the arrangement of the particles in a solid and in a liquid. (3)
(b) Describe the motion of the particles in a solid and in a liquid. (2)

Show answer

(a) Particles in a solid have a regular arrangement 1

Particles in a liquid have an irregular or random arrangement 1

Particles in both are closely packed 1

(b) In a solid, particles vibrate about fixed positions 1

In a liquid, particles slide or flow past one another 1

The third mark in (a) is the one the year group kept losing, by saying liquids are not closely packed. They are — what changes on melting is the regularity, not the spacing.

In (b), "move freely" was ignored. The examiners wanted slide, flow, or move past each other.

D12

A solid is heated steadily until it melts. Its temperature–time graph shows a horizontal section.

Explain what is happening to the particles during this horizontal section. (3)

Show answer

The energy supplied is used to break the bonds, or overcome the forces of attraction, between the particles 1

rather than to increase their kinetic energy 1

so the temperature stays constant while the substance changes state 1

The trap is answering "the energy is being used to melt it", which restates the question rather than explaining it. All three marks are in what the energy does to the bonds and to the kinetic energy.

D13

A gas in a sealed rigid container is at 12 °C.

(a) Convert this temperature to kelvin. (1)
(b) The gas is heated. Explain what happens to the energy of its molecules. (2)
(c) Explain why a temperature below −273 °C is not possible. (2)

Show answer

(a) 12 + 273 = 285 K 1

(b) The energy of the molecules increases 1

Specifically their kinetic energy increases, so they move faster on average 1

(c) At −273 °C the molecules would have essentially no kinetic energy left 1

You cannot remove energy that is not there, so no lower temperature is possible — this is absolute zero 1

2025's examiner made the point that temperature is proportional to the average kinetic energy — temperature is not itself kinetic energy. The kelvin temperature is what is proportional to it, which is exactly why the scale exists.

D14

A sealed syringe contains 80 cm³ of air at a pressure of 100 kPa. The plunger is pushed in until the volume is 32 cm³. The temperature does not change.

(a) Calculate the new pressure. (3)
(b) Explain, in terms of the particles, why the pressure changed. (2)

Show answer

(a) P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ 1

100 × 80 = P₂ × 32 1

P₂ = 8000 ÷ 32 = 250 kPa 1

(b) The same number of particles now occupies a smaller volume 1

so they collide with the walls more frequently, increasing the total force per unit area and therefore the pressure 1

The particles do not speed up and do not hit harder — the temperature is constant, so the average kinetic energy is unchanged. Only the frequency of collisions increases. Mock 2 calls saying they move faster "a common and costly error".

D15

A fixed mass of gas in a sealed rigid container is at 300 K and a pressure of 180 kPa. It is heated to 375 K.

(a) Calculate the new pressure. (3)
(b) Explain, in terms of the particles, why the pressure changed. (2)

Show answer

(a) P₁ ÷ T₁ = P₂ ÷ T₂ 1

180 ÷ 300 = P₂ ÷ 375 1

P₂ = 0.6 × 375 = 225 kPa 1

(b) The higher temperature means the particles have greater average kinetic energy, so they move faster 1

so they collide with the walls both more often and harder, increasing the force per unit area 1

Compare this deliberately with D14. There the particles did not speed up; here they do. The difference is whether the temperature changed, and the exam tests whether you can tell the two apart.

The temperatures are already in kelvin here. If a question gives Celsius, convert before substituting — this equation is a proportionality and Celsius has no true zero.

D16

A sealed empty plastic bottle is carried onto an aircraft at high altitude, where the cabin pressure is low. As the aircraft descends, the bottle is crushed inwards.

(a) Explain why the bottle is crushed. (2)
(b) Explain, in terms of molecules, how the air outside the bottle exerts a pressure on it. (3)

Show answer

(a) The air pressure outside the bottle increases as the aircraft descends 1

The pressure inside is unchanged, so there is a resultant inward force on the walls of the bottle 1

(b) The molecules move quickly and randomly in all directions 1

They collide with the surface of the bottle, and each collision exerts a force on it 1

Pressure is force ÷ area, so these collisions over the surface produce a pressure 1

2025's examiner: the collision mark was only given if molecules or particles were named — "they hit the walls" scored nothing. And the pressure = force/area point had to be linked into the answer, not just written down. Make it a sentence that joins the collisions to the pressure.

D17

An urn heats 6.0 kg of water from 18 °C to 98 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/kg °C.

(a) Calculate the energy transferred to the water. (3)
(b) The heating element operates at 230 V and draws a current of 10 A. Calculate the time taken to supply this energy, assuming all of it reaches the water. (4)
(c) In practice the urn takes longer than this. Explain why. (2)

Show answer

(a) ΔT = 98 − 18 = 80 °C 1

ΔQ = 6.0 × 4200 × 80 1 = 2 016 000 J (2.016 × 10⁶ J) 1

(b) P = I × V = 10 × 230 1 = 2300 W 1

t = E ÷ P = 2 016 000 ÷ 2300 1 = 877 s, about 15 minutes 1

(c) Some energy is transferred to the thermal store of the surroundings and to the urn itself 1

so not all the electrical energy reaches the water, and more time is needed to deliver the required energy to it 1

ΔT is a difference: 80, not 98. This is a favourite way to lose the first mark.

D18

A heater supplies 9.3 × 10⁵ J of energy to a tank of water, raising its temperature by 45 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/kg °C.

Calculate the mass of water in the tank. (3)

Show answer

ΔQ = m × c × ΔT, so m = ΔQ ÷ (c × ΔT) 1

m = 9.3 × 10⁵ ÷ (4200 × 45) 1

= 4.9 kg (2 s.f.) 1

2025 asked exactly this, and the examiner noted that some students struggled to rearrange the equation for mass. Practise making each symbol the subject in turn — m = ΔQ ÷ (cΔT), c = ΔQ ÷ (mΔT), ΔT = ΔQ ÷ (mc) — until none of them needs thinking about.

D19

The same amount of energy is supplied to 2.0 kg of water and to 2.0 kg of cooking oil. Oil has a lower specific heat capacity than water.

State and explain what happens to the temperature of the oil compared with that of the water. (2)

Show answer

The oil reaches a higher temperature 1

because a lower specific heat capacity means less energy is needed per kilogram for each degree of temperature rise, so the same energy produces a larger temperature change 1

Check the logic against something familiar: water's specific heat capacity is unusually high, which is why a saucepan gets scaldingly hot in seconds while the water in it takes minutes to boil.

D20

A diver descends to a depth of 24 m in sea water of density 1030 kg/m³. Take g = 10 N/kg.

(a) Calculate the pressure due to the water at that depth. (3)
(b) State what happens to the pressure on the diver as she descends further, and why. (2)
(c) The pressure acts on her mask, which has an area of 0.012 m². Calculate the force on the mask due to the water pressure. (3)

Show answer

(a) ΔP = h × g × ρ 1

= 24 × 10 × 1030 1 = 247 200 Pa, about 247 kPa 1

(b) The pressure increases 1

because there is a greater height of water above her pressing down 1

(c) P = F ÷ A, so F = P × A 1

= 247 200 × 0.012 1 = 2966 N, about 3000 N 1

Note that pressure in a liquid acts equally in all directions — it presses on the mask sideways just as hard as it presses down on her shoulders.
