02How it actually works

These marks are available whether or not you can do the physics — a student who has forgotten every equation can still take most of them. They are also the marks most often lost to vague wording, because the examiners are marking for specific vocabulary. Every heading says where it was asked.

The three kinds of variable — say the exact words

2025 Q8(a)(i)–(iii) · Mock 1 Q8(a) · 2023 Q7(a)

  • Independent variable — the one you deliberately change
  • Dependent variable — the one you measure as a result
  • Control variables — everything else you deliberately keep the same

A test for telling the first two apart: the independent variable is the one you decide the values of before you start. If you had to run the experiment to find out what it was, it is the dependent one.

2023's examiner: many students named what to measure but "did not specifically say independent/dependent — use the exact words."

Describing the method is not enough. Write "the independent variable is the height", not "I would drop it from different heights". The mark is for the vocabulary.

Control variables must be specific

2025 Q8(a)(iii) — "I didn't allow 'size' or 'volume'"

A control variable must be something you could actually measure and set. Vague physical properties do not count.

RejectedAccepted
"The rods should be the same size"The rods should be the same length; the rods should be the same diameter
"Use the same equipment"Use the same spring throughout
"Keep conditions the same"Keep the temperature the same; use the same mass of coin

Note also that "same length" and "same diameter" count as two separate control variables, so a two-mark question is answered by naming two specific dimensions rather than one vague one.

Validity and reliability — the distinction that earns marks

2025 Q8(a)(iii)(iv) · Mock 1 Q8(b)

What it meansHow you improve it
ReliabilityWhether you get the same answer if you do it againRepeat the measurements and calculate a mean
ValidityWhether you are actually measuring what you intendedControl the other variables so only one thing changes

The two are independent, which is the point of the distinction. A badly designed experiment repeated five times gives beautifully consistent — reliable — results that are consistently wrong, because it was never valid in the first place.

2023: "Repeats or collect more measurements / take more readings is too vague."

Say what you repeat and what you then do with the results: "repeat the measurement at each height at least three times, and calculate a mean." The mean is half the mark.

"Suggest an improvement" — say what to do

2025 Q8 · 2023 Q4(c)(i)

2025's examiner: "The improvement question says what can be done — just pointing out the flaw ('the rods aren't the same length') lost the mark."

Identifying the problem is not the answer; it is the reason for the answer. Phrase every improvement as an instruction:

✗ "The rods were different lengths."
✓ "Use rods of the same length, so that only the material is changing."

Similarly in 2023, "take more data at different voltages" scored where "take more readings" did not — because it says what to change as well as what to do.

Continuous or categoric — which graph, and the words for it

2025 Q8(b)(ii) · 2023 Q7(b)(iii) · Mock 1 Q8(c)(iii)

  • Continuous data can take any value in a range — force, time, temperature, length. Plot a line graph.
  • Categoric (or discrete) data comes in separate named groups — copper, steel, aluminium. Plot a bar chart.

The justification, which is what actually gets marked: a line graph is appropriate when both variables are continuous, because it shows the trend and lets you read intermediate values off it. A bar chart is right when one variable is categoric, because there is nothing in between "copper" and "steel" to read.

2025: the examiner "was holding out specifically for the right vocab: categoric or discrete." And 2023: "Very few earned the 'continuous' mark."

These two words are worth a mark each, appear nearly every year, and take a minute to learn. Say continuous or categoric — not "it's numbers" or "they're different things".

Plotting — the marks are mechanical

2023 Q4(b)(i) · 2025 Q8(b)(i) · Mock 1 Q8(c)(i)

Nothing here requires understanding, so nothing here should be lost. The checklist:

  1. Label both axes with the quantity and its unit — "Force in N", not "Force"
  2. Use a sensible linear scale that fills at least half the grid. Awkward scales like 3 units per square cause plotting errors
  3. Plot every point, including (0, 0) if it is in the table
  4. Each point accurate to within half a small square
  5. Use clear crosses or large dots

2023's examiner made two complaints, and both are avoidable:

"Common error was missing off (0.0, 0.0)" — if the table includes the origin, plot it. It is a data point like any other.

"Dots for plots that were so small I couldn't actually see all of them — use X or bigger dots, papers get scanned." Your paper is marked as a scanned image. A faint pinprick does not survive scanning. Draw crosses.

Lines and curves of best fit

2023 Q4(b)(ii) · 2023 Q7(b)(i) · Mock 1 Q8(c)(i)

A line of best fit is a single smooth line or straight line that shows the overall trend, with roughly as many points above it as below. The mark schemes want it within about one small square of the points.

It is not dot-to-dot. Joining the points with straight segments treats every measurement as perfect, when each one carries some error — the whole purpose of the line is to average those errors out. A zig-zag also implies the quantity really jumped around between readings, which it did not.

If the data is curved, draw a smooth curve. Do not force a straight line through curved data, and do not draw a curve through data that is genuinely straight. And an anomalous point is ignored when drawing the line — not accommodated by bending it.

Anomalies

Mock 1 Q8(c)(ii)

An anomaly is a point that clearly breaks the pattern the others establish. Spot it by looking at the trend, not at the number in isolation.

The two-mark answer has two halves, and the second is usually forgotten:

  1. Identify it — quote the coordinates of the point
  2. Say what to do: repeat that measurement, and exclude it from the line of best fit if it cannot be reproduced

"Ignore it" alone is not enough. The correct scientific response is to check it first — it might be a recording error, or it might be real.

Reading data off a graph

2023 Q7(b)(ii)(iv) · 2025 Q9(d)

When a question says use data from the graph, it means take actual numbers off it and calculate with them.

2025: "Many candidates referred to the shape of the graph and tried to justify their answers without any data, which was awarded zero marks."

"The graph curves, so it isn't proportional" describes the graph. It scores nothing. Take two pairs of readings, do the arithmetic on each, and compare the results — that is using data.

2023 adds a second rule: "the points had to be on the line or be plotted data points — misread graph points were the most common error." Read from where your line of best fit actually passes, or from a plotted point. Do not read from a gap between them.

Describing a method

2023 Q7(a) — 6 marks · 2024 Q2(b), Q6(a) · Mock 2 Q7(a)

These are worth five or six marks and appear on every paper. Work through a fixed skeleton so nothing is missed:

  1. Name the independent variable and say you will test at least five values of it
  2. Name the dependent variable and the instrument used to measure it — ruler, balance, stopwatch, thermometer
  3. Name two specific control variables
  4. Give one accuracy detail — read at eye level, fully submerged, start the stopwatch and heater together
  5. Repeat each measurement and take a mean
  6. Say what you will calculate or plot at the end

Six numbered points, six marks. Writing it as a list rather than a paragraph makes each one visible to the examiner and makes it much harder to leave one out.

Validity and reliability — the distinction that earns marks

Validity is about whether the experiment tests what it claims to. You improve validity by controlling variables — making it a fair test. Ask: is anything other than my independent variable affecting the result?

Reliability is about whether the results are trustworthy and repeatable. You improve reliability by repeating measurements and calculating a mean, and by identifying and discarding anomalies.

They are examined as separate questions, sometimes back to back in the same question, as in 2025 Q8. Mixing them up costs both marks.

Graph craft — the checklist

  1. Independent variable on the x-axis, dependent on the y-axis
  2. Label both axes with the quantity and the unit, e.g. "Time in s"
  3. Choose a sensible scale: use at least half the grid in both directions, and use 1, 2, 5 or 10 units per square — never 3 or 7
  4. Check the scale works for every data point before you start plotting. If your awkward values won't sit on a line, change the scale now, not after
  5. Plot with a clear X or a large dot. The papers get scanned; tiny faint dots vanish
  6. Include (0,0) if the data starts there
  7. Line or curve of best fit — a single smooth line with points balanced either side. Never dot-to-dot. Never force it through the origin unless the data says so
  8. Circle anomalies and ignore them when drawing the line

Describing a method — the eight-point skeleton

Six-mark method questions are marked against a list. Work through this skeleton and you will hit most of it.

  1. Independent variable — name it and say you will change it
  2. Dependent variable — name it and say you will measure it
  3. Control variables — name at least two specific things kept the same
  4. Equipment — name the measuring instrument for each quantity
  5. Method — numbered steps, in order, specific enough to follow
  6. Range — state how many values and over what range
  7. Repeats — repeat each measurement and take a mean
  8. Accuracy — one specific technique, e.g. read at eye level to avoid parallax, start the stopwatch and the heater simultaneously, subtract background count

The 2023 examiner noted: "A lot of students knew which variables to measure/change but did not specifically say independent/dependent. A lot of students missed MP8." Use the words. Include the accuracy point.

04Understanding it

U1

A student measures the boiling point of water five times and gets 96.2, 96.3, 96.2, 96.3, 96.2 °C. The true value is 100 °C.

Are the results reliable? Are they accurate? What is probably wrong?

Show answer

Reliable — yes. The readings are closely grouped and repeating gives essentially the same answer. They're also precise.

Accurate — no. They are consistently about 3.8 °C below the true value.

What's wrong: a systematic error — most likely a thermometer that is miscalibrated, or being read incorrectly the same way each time. Because the error is the same every time, repeating doesn't reveal it.

The lesson worth taking into the exam: repeating measurements improves reliability but does nothing about systematic error. If a question asks how to make results more accurate, "repeat them" is the wrong answer — you need calibration, a better instrument, or a change of technique.

U2

A student investigates how quickly different metals conduct heat, using rods of different lengths and thicknesses.

Is the problem with validity or reliability? Write the fix as the examiner wants it.

Show answer

Validity. The independent variable is meant to be the type of metal, but length and thickness are also varying. If rod A conducts faster you cannot tell whether that's because of the metal or because it was shorter. The test isn't fair.

Reliability isn't the issue — you could repeat this experiment perfectly consistently and still get an answer that means nothing.

The fix, phrased as an instruction: "Use rods of the same length and the same diameter." Not "the rods were different lengths", which describes the problem rather than improving it. The 2025 examiner deducted marks for exactly that.

U3

Why is dot-to-dot wrong for a line graph, when it passes through every measured point perfectly?

Show answer

Because every measurement carries some uncertainty. Dot-to-dot treats each point as exactly correct, which means it treats measurement error as though it were real physical behaviour — producing kinks and wobbles that reflect nothing but the limits of your ruler.

A line of best fit assumes the underlying relationship is smooth and that the scatter around it is error. It averages out the random errors, which is why a value read off the line is more trustworthy than any individual measured point.

It also lets you spot anomalies. A point far from an otherwise good line is visibly wrong. On a dot-to-dot, every point sits on the line by construction, so nothing looks wrong.

U4

A question asks you to "use data from the graph" to test a claim. Why is "the graph is a curve, so the relationship isn't linear" worth zero marks even when it's true?

Show answer

Because the question asked for data, and shape is not data. The mark scheme awards marks for reading numbers off the graph, doing something with them, and comparing the outcomes.

The 2025 examiner: "Many candidates referred to the shape of the graph, and tried to justify their answers without any data, which was awarded zero marks." Students scored 0 or 4 — nothing in between — because either you read numbers off or you didn't.

The procedure to follow whenever you see "use data from the graph":

  1. Read one pair of values off the graph and write them down
  2. Read a second pair off and write them down
  3. Do the arithmetic the claim requires to each pair
  4. State both results and say explicitly whether they agree

Write the numbers on the page. An examiner cannot award a mark for a calculation you did in your head.

U5

A question asks for a variable that must be controlled when comparing how quickly different metal rods conduct heat. A student writes "the rods should be the same size." The examiner explicitly refused this. Why, and what should be written?

Show answer

Because "size" is not something you can measure or set. It is a vague impression covering length, diameter, mass and volume all at once, and it gives no evidence that the student knows which of those actually matters.

Both do matter here, and for different reasons. Length determines how far the heat has to travel; diameter determines how much cross-section it can travel through. Change either and the comparison between metals is spoiled.

What scores: "the rods must be the same length" and "the rods must be the same diameter" — and note these count as two separate control variables, so a two-mark question is answered by naming two specific dimensions.

2025's examiner: "I didn't allow 'size' or 'volume'." The same applies to "use the same equipment" and "keep conditions the same". Name the actual quantity.

U6

"The rods weren't all the same length." Why does this score zero on a question asking how the investigation could be improved, even though it correctly identifies the flaw?

Show answer

Because the question asks what could be done, and that sentence describes a problem rather than an action. Identifying the flaw is the reason for the improvement, not the improvement itself.

Rewrite every improvement as an instruction:

✗ "The rods weren't all the same length."
✓ "Use rods of the same length, so that only the material is different."

The pattern that always works: a verb, then the specific thing, then why.

The same trap in a different form: in 2023, "take more readings" scored nothing but "take more data at different voltages" did. The first says do more of the same; the second says what to change as well as what to do.

Before writing any improvement, check your sentence begins with a verb. If it begins with "the" or "there wasn't", it is a complaint and not an answer.

U7

Why does the examiner insist on the words "continuous" and "categoric" when asking you to justify a line graph or a bar chart? What is the underlying idea?

Show answer

Because the choice of graph follows from what kind of data you have, and those two words are the names for the distinction.

Continuous data can take any value in a range — force, time, temperature, length. Between 2.0 N and 3.0 N there is 2.5 N, and 2.51 N, and so on. So the space between two plotted points is meaningful, a line through them means something, and you can read intermediate values off it. That is a line graph.

Categoric data comes in separate named groups — copper, steel, aluminium. There is nothing between copper and steel. A line joining them would imply a halfway material that does not exist, so you use separate bars instead.

Why the vocabulary is the mark: saying "a line graph is better because it shows the trend" is true of any line graph and does not show you understand when it is appropriate. The word "continuous" is the evidence.

2025: the examiner "was holding out specifically for the right vocab: categoric or discrete." 2023: "Very few earned the 'continuous' mark." Two words, worth a mark nearly every year.

U8

An experiment is repeated five times and gives almost identical results every time. Does that make it a good experiment? Explain.

Show answer

No — it makes it reliable, which is not the same as valid.

Reliability is about consistency: do you get the same answer if you do it again? Repeating and averaging improves it.

Validity is about whether you are measuring what you intended at all. It depends on the design — on whether the other variables were controlled so that only one thing changed.

The two are independent, and that is the whole point of keeping the words separate. A badly designed experiment repeated five times produces five consistent answers that are all wrong in the same way. Repeating it a sixth time does not help, because the fault is in the method, not in the scatter.

Worked case: measuring a boiling point five times and getting 96.2, 96.3, 96.2, 96.3, 96.2 °C is extremely reliable — and tells you the thermometer is misreading by about 4 degrees. No amount of repetition would reveal that; only checking the instrument would.

So: to improve reliability, repeat and take a mean. To improve validity, control the other variables.

05Exam drill

D1

A student investigates how the time for a coin to fall from a wax-attached metal rod depends on the metal used. Four rods of different metals are connected to one heater.

(a) State the independent variable. (1)
(b) State the dependent variable. (1)
(c) Give two ways to improve the validity of this investigation. (2)
(d) Describe what the student should do to improve the reliability of their results. (2)

Show answer

(a) The type of metal 1

(b) The time taken for the coin to fall 1

(c) Any two, phrased as instructions 11: use rods of the same length and diameter; use the same mass of wax on each rod; use identical coins; position each rod the same distance from the heater.

(d) Repeat the whole investigation at least three times 1 and calculate a mean time for each metal, discarding any anomalous results 1

Part (d) is where "repeat it" alone fails. Say how many repeats and what you do with them.

D2

In the investigation above, the student plots a bar chart.

State why a bar chart is a better choice than a line graph here. (2)

Show answer

The independent variable, type of metal, is categoric rather than continuous 1

There are no intermediate values between the metals, so a trend line would be meaningless — the categories are separate 1

The word "categoric" is what the examiner was waiting for.

D3

Describe an investigation to find the specific heat capacity of a metal block. You have: a block with holes for a heater and thermometer, an immersion heater of known power, a power supply, a stopwatch, a thermometer and a balance. (6)

Show answer

Any six of the following 111111

  • Measure the mass of the block using the balance
  • Insert the heater and thermometer into the holes in the block
  • Record the starting temperature
  • Switch on the heater and start the stopwatch at the same moment
  • After a measured time, switch off and record the highest temperature reached
  • Calculate energy supplied using E = power × time
  • Calculate c using c = E ÷ (m × ΔT)
  • Insulate the block with lagging to reduce energy transfer to the surroundings
  • Repeat and take a mean

Two details that pick up marks reliably: starting the heater and stopwatch simultaneously, and lagging the block. Also worth adding: energy lost to the surroundings means the value obtained is higher than the true value.

D4

A student's results for an investigation are: (0,0), (2.0, 4.1), (4.0, 8.2), (6.0, 11.9), (8.0, 21.0), (10.0, 20.1).

(a) Identify the anomalous result and explain how you can tell. (2)
(b) State what the student should do about it. (2)

Show answer

(a) The point (8.0, 21.0) 1. The other points increase by roughly 4 for each increase of 2 in the independent variable, so a value near 16 would be expected; 21.0 does not fit the pattern and is higher than the following point 1

(b) Repeat the measurement at that value of the independent variable 1; ignore the anomaly when drawing the line of best fit and exclude it from any mean 1

Note "delete it" or "ignore it" alone is weak. The correct response to an anomaly is to re-measure, then exclude if it can't be reproduced.

D5

A graph of range against table height is a smooth upward curve passing through (0.4, 0.85) and (1.6, 1.70).

A student claims range = launch speed × √(height ÷ 5). Using data from the graph, show that the launch speed is approximately 3 m/s. (4)

Show answer

Read a point from the graph: at height = 1.6 m, range = 1.70 m 1

√(1.6 ÷ 5) = √0.32 = 0.566 1

launch speed = range ÷ 0.566 = 1.70 ÷ 0.566 1

= 3.0 m/s 1

D6

x-axis: voltage, 1.0 V per 2 squares — or any scale using the majority of the grid with sensible intervals 1

D12

Describe an investigation to find how the range of a projectile depends on the height it is launched from. (6)

Show answer

Any six of the following 111111

  • The independent variable is the launch height
  • Test at least five different heights
  • The dependent variable is the range — the horizontal distance travelled
  • Measure the height and the range with a ruler or tape measure
  • Control variable: the same launch speed each time
  • Control variable: the same ball, or the same launch angle
  • Use a method to see where the ball lands — carbon paper, a tray of sand, or video
  • Repeat at each height and calculate a mean range
  • Plot a graph of range against height

2023's examiner: many students named what to measure but "did not specifically say independent/dependent — use the exact words." Write the labels, do not just describe the actions.

D13

A student measures the mass and volume of an irregular stone in order to find its density.

(a) Name the apparatus needed for each measurement. (2)
(b) Describe how the volume should be found. (2)
(c) Give two details that would improve the accuracy of the measurements. (2)

Show answer

(a) Mass: a balance 1 · Volume: a measuring cylinder, or a eureka can 1

(b) Part-fill the measuring cylinder with water and record the volume; lower the stone in until fully submerged 1

Record the new volume and subtract to find the volume of the stone 1

(c) Any two 11: ensure the stone is fully submerged · lower it gently on a thread to avoid splashing · read the volume at eye level to avoid parallax · zero the balance before weighing

This is the displacement method, and it appeared in 2024 for four marks. The accuracy details are the marks most often missed.

D14

A student plots current against voltage for a filament lamp and obtains a curve.

(a) Suggest one improvement the student could make to the investigation. (1)
(b) Explain why the graph is a curve rather than a straight line. (2)

Show answer

(a) Take more data at different voltages 1

(b) The current heats the filament, so its temperature rises 1

The resistance changes with temperature, and the changing resistance changes the gradient of the graph 1

In (a), 2023's examiner rejected "repeats or collect more measurements / take more readings" as too vague — the answer must say at different voltages.

In (b), "you must talk about temperature — mentioning it in the wrong causal order didn't score." The chain runs current → temperature → resistance → gradient, in that order.
