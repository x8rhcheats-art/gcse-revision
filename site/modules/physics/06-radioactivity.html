02How it actually works

Half-life is defined in two of the three past papers and calculated in all three. Get that definition exactly right and you have banked marks before the paper starts. Every heading says where it was asked.

The atom, and what the numbers mean

Spec 7.2, 7.3 · 2023 Q2(a)–(d) — six marks of multiple choice

A nucleus of protons and neutrons, with electrons around it. Written as 146C:

  • The bottom number is the atomic (proton) number — the number of protons. It decides which element it is.
  • The top number is the mass (nucleon) number — protons + neutrons together.
  • Neutrons = top − bottom. For carbon-14: 14 − 6 = 8 neutrons.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are both carbon — six protons each — but one has six neutrons and the other eight.

An ion is different again, and is about electrons: adding an electron makes a negative ion, removing one makes a positive ion. The nucleus is untouched.

Why nuclei decay, and the three radiations

Spec 7.4, 7.5 · Mock 2 Q4(b)

Some nuclei are unstable. They emit radiation from the nucleus, at random, to become more stable. All three types are ionising.

What it isStopped byIonising power
Alpha (α)2 protons + 2 neutrons — a helium nucleusA sheet of paper, or a few cm of airStrongly ionising
Beta (β−)A fast-moving electron from the nucleusA few mm of aluminiumModerately
Gamma (γ)A high-frequency electromagnetic waveThick lead or concrete — only reduced, never fully stoppedWeakly ionising

The pattern is worth noticing because it is the reason behind every application: the more strongly ionising a radiation is, the less far it penetrates. Alpha does its damage immediately and stops; gamma passes through almost everything precisely because it interacts so little.

Identifying an unknown source — the standard three marks

The count is unchanged by paper, but drops to background level with 5 mm of aluminium.

It is beta 1 — because paper does not reduce the count, so it is not alpha 1, and 5 mm of aluminium reduces it to background, so it is not gamma, which would penetrate 1.

Two of the three marks are for ruling the other two out. Naming the radiation alone scores one. Always write both eliminations.

Ionisation

Spec 7.4 · 2024 Q5(a)(ii)

Ionisation is the removal of electron(s) from an atom. That is the mark-scheme wording, and it is worth a mark on its own.

It matters because it is how radiation damages living things. An ionising particle rips electrons from the atoms it passes, which breaks molecules apart — including DNA — causing cell damage and mutations.

Decay equations

Spec 7.7, 7.8 · 2024 Q5(a)(i) · Mock 1 Q9(e)

Learn the effect of each emission on the two numbers:

EmissionMass numberAtomic number
AlphaDecreases by 4Decreases by 2
BetaUnchangedIncreases by 1
GammaUnchangedUnchanged
NeutronDecreases by 1Unchanged

Beta looks strange — the nucleus emits an electron and its proton count goes up. The reason: a neutron in the nucleus turns into a proton, and the electron created is thrown out. So the total number of nucleons is the same, but one more of them is a proton.

Balancing is the whole technique: the top numbers must total the same on both sides, and so must the bottom numbers. For the beta decay of carbon-14:

146C → 147N + 0−1e

Top: 14 = 14 + 0 ✓. Bottom: 6 = 7 + (−1) ✓. The beta particle is written 0−1e — no nucleons, charge −1. An alpha particle is 42He.

Background radiation

Spec 7.10 · 2025 Q9(b) · Mock 2 Q4(a)

Ionising radiation that is present all the time, everywhere, even when no source is being used.

Sources: rocks and soil (especially granite), radon gas, cosmic rays from space, food and drink, and medical procedures such as X-rays.

2025's examiner penalised two specific things:

Do not use the word "background" inside the definition — "a number tried to use the word background in their answer which I didn't allow". Defining a term using itself explains nothing.

Do not say it comes only from natural sources — "A number of students were referring to the idea that it must come from natural sources, which I punished." Medical procedures and nuclear industry waste are part of it too.

Activity and half-life

Spec 7.11, 7.12 · 2024 Q5(b)(i) · 2025 Q9(c)(i) · Mock 1 Q9(b) — defined in two of three papers

Activity is the rate at which nuclei decay, measured in becquerels (Bq). One becquerel is one decay per second. Activity decreases over time, because there are fewer undecayed nuclei left to decay.

Half-life is the time taken for the activity of a sample — or the number of undecayed nuclei in it — to halve.

Both papers flag the same error: "The time for a nucleus to halve" scores zero. A single nucleus cannot halve. It either decays or it doesn't, and you cannot predict when.

The thing that halves is a property of the whole sample — its activity, its number of undecayed nuclei, or the mass of the isotope remaining. Say "of a sample" and the mark is safe.

Half-life calculations — and the background trap

Spec 7.13 · 2024 Q5(b)(ii) · 2025 Q9(c)(ii) · Mock 1 Q9(c) · Mock 2 Q4(d)

The method is repeated halving. Count how many halvings fit into the time, then halve that many times.

Worked example — activity after a given time

Initial activity 12 000 Bq, half-life 6.0 hours. What is the activity after 1 day?

1. Number of half-lives: 24 ÷ 6 = 4

2. Halve four times: 12 000 → 6000 → 3000 → 1500 → 750 Bq

Write the chain out. Mark schemes award a mark for the evidence of halving even if the final number slips.

Worked example — finding the half-life from a count rate

Background is 24 counts/min. With the source, the detector reads 424 counts/min. The corrected count rate falls to 50 counts/min after 96 minutes. Find the half-life.

1. Correct the initial reading for background. 424 − 24 = 400 counts/min. (The 50 is already corrected — read the question carefully to see which figures are which.)

2. Count the halvings: 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 is 3 half-lives

3. Divide: 96 ÷ 3 = 32 minutes

Subtract the background before you do anything else. Mock 2 is explicit: "Working from 424 gives a wrong number of half-lives and loses all three marks." The detector counts background whether the source is there or not, so the raw reading is not the source's activity.

From a graph: draw the lines. 2025 wanted "lines drawn on the graph from an activity to half that activity" — pick a starting activity, find where the curve reaches half of it, and read off the time difference. The construction lines are a mark.

And quote numbers. On the 2025 part asking candidates to test a relationship: "many candidates referred to the shape of the graph and tried to justify their answers without any data, which was awarded zero marks." Take readings off the graph and calculate with them.

Randomness

Spec 7.4 · 2023 Q2(e) · Mock 2 Q4(e)

Radioactive decay is a random process: it is impossible to predict when any individual nucleus will decay, and each has the same chance in a given time regardless of how long it has already existed.

Half-life is not a contradiction of this. With enormous numbers of nuclei, the average behaviour is highly predictable even though each individual event is not — in the same way that no one can say which particular people will make a phone call in the next minute, yet the total number of calls is very predictable.

Uses, contamination and dangers

Spec 7.14, 7.15, 7.16 · Mock 1 Q9(d) · 2024 Q5(a)(iii)

Every application follows from the penetration table. The exam asks you to justify a choice, so reason from the properties:

UseRadiationWhy
Medical tracerGammaPenetrating enough to escape the body and be detected; weakly ionising so it does least damage inside. Alpha would be absorbed within centimetres of tissue and never reach the detector, while ionising heavily on the way
Thickness monitoringBetaPartly absorbed by the sheet, so the count rate responds to thickness. Alpha would be stopped completely, gamma would pass through unaffected
Sterilising equipmentGammaPenetrates the sealed packaging to kill bacteria inside
Smoke detectorAlphaIonises the air to allow a small current; smoke blocks it. Short range makes it safe outside the device

Contamination versus irradiation — a distinction the specification names explicitly:

  • Irradiation is being exposed to radiation from a source outside you. It stops when you move away, and the object does not become radioactive.
  • Contamination is getting radioactive material on or inside you. It continues to irradiate you until it is removed or decays, which makes it the more serious.

Dangers and precautions: ionising radiation damages cells and tissue and can cause mutations. Reduce risk by keeping exposure times short, staying at a distance, and using shielding (lead, concrete) and remote handling tongs. Radioactive waste stays dangerous for a very long time and is sealed and stored securely, often deep underground.

Fission and fusion

Spec 7.17 – 7.26

Both release energy from the nucleus, and they are opposites:

FissionFusion
What happensA large nucleus (U-235) is split by a neutronSmall nuclei are joined into a larger one
ProductsTwo radioactive daughter nuclei, a few neutrons, and energy as kinetic energy of the fission productsA larger nucleus, with a loss of mass and a release of energy
WhereNuclear power stationsStars, including the Sun

Chain reaction: each fission releases neutrons, which can strike further U-235 nuclei and cause them to split too, so the process sustains itself.

In a reactor: control rods absorb neutrons to keep the rate steady — pushed in to slow it, withdrawn to speed it up. The moderator slows the neutrons down, because slow neutrons are far more likely to be absorbed and cause fission. Shielding — thick concrete and lead around the reactor — absorbs the radiation and protects the workers outside.

Why fusion needs stars: both nuclei are positively charged, so they repel each other electrostatically. Only at extremely high temperatures and pressures do they move fast enough to overcome that repulsion and get close enough to fuse — which is why it does not happen at everyday conditions.

Three specific rules from the 2025 report on this topic:

Half-life — it's the sample or the number of nuclei. Never "a nucleus". A single nucleus has no half-life; it either decays or it doesn't.

Background radiation — you cannot use the word "background" in the definition, and you must not say it is only natural. Some of it is artificial (medical, nuclear industry). Say: radiation present even when no source is being used.

Sources — acceptable named sources include rocks (especially granite, radon gas), cosmic rays from space, food and drink, and medical procedures. "Stars" was not credited.

Half-life calculations

Method — activity after n half-lives

  1. Number of half-lives = total time ÷ half-life
  2. Halve the starting activity that many times

Example: 800 Bq, half-life 6 days, after 24 days. 24 ÷ 6 = 4 half-lives. 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 Bq.

Method — finding half-life from a graph

  1. Pick a convenient starting activity on the curve
  2. Find where the activity has fallen to half that value
  3. The half-life is the time between those two points
  4. Check it by repeating from a different starting point — it should be the same

If the graph shows a background level, subtract it from all readings first. The curve should tend towards zero, not towards the background.

Randomness, uses and dangers

Radioactive decay is random — you cannot predict when any individual nucleus will decay. Half-life is a statistical average over an enormous number of nuclei, which is why it's reliable for a sample and meaningless for one atom.

04Understanding it

U1

Alpha radiation is the most dangerous of the three if the source is swallowed, but the least dangerous if the source is across the room. Explain this apparent contradiction.

Show answer

Both facts follow from the same property: alpha is strongly ionising and weakly penetrating.

Across the room: alpha particles are absorbed by a few centimetres of air, and cannot penetrate the dead outer layer of your skin. Essentially none of the radiation reaches living tissue, so it does no harm.

Swallowed: the source is now inside, in direct contact with living cells. There's no air gap and no skin barrier. All that strong ionising power is deposited directly into tissue over a very short distance, causing intense localised damage to cells and DNA.

Gamma is the reverse: dangerous from outside because it penetrates, but less damaging per unit of exposure because it ionises weakly and much of it passes straight through.

U2

A sample has a half-life of 10 days. After 10 days half the nuclei have decayed. Why don't the remaining nuclei decay in the next 10 days, leaving none?

Show answer

Because decay is random, not scheduled. Each surviving nucleus has the same probability of decaying in the next 10 days as it always had — about one in two. Nuclei do not age or "queue up".

So in the second 10 days, half of the remaining half decays, leaving a quarter. Then an eighth, then a sixteenth. Each half-life halves whatever is left, which is why the graph is a curve approaching zero rather than a straight line hitting it.

Consequence worth knowing: in principle the activity never reaches exactly zero. In practice it eventually drops below the background level and can't be distinguished from it.

U3

In beta decay, an electron is emitted from the nucleus. But the nucleus contains only protons and neutrons, and electrons orbit outside it. Where does the electron come from?

Show answer

It doesn't come from the orbiting shells. It is created at the moment of decay, when a neutron inside the nucleus turns into a proton and an electron. The proton stays; the electron is ejected at high speed as the beta particle.

This explains the bookkeeping perfectly:

  • Mass number unchanged — one neutron became one proton, so the total nucleon count is the same
  • Atomic number increases by 1 — there is now one more proton, so it is a different element
  • Charge conserved — a neutral neutron became a +1 proton and a −1 electron, net zero

Nuclei emit beta radiation when they have too many neutrons relative to protons. Converting one to the other moves them towards stability.

U4

A medical tracer needs a short half-life. A smoke detector source needs a long one. Explain both, and identify what makes them opposite requirements.

Show answer

Medical tracer — short half-life (hours). It must remain active long enough to be imaged, but decay quickly afterwards so the patient isn't exposed for longer than necessary. A long half-life would mean carrying a radioactive source around for years.

Smoke detector — long half-life (hundreds of years). It must keep working reliably for the whole life of the device without being replaced. A short half-life would mean the activity drops and the detector silently stops working.

The underlying trade-off: half-life determines how long a source stays useful and how long it stays hazardous. Where the source is inside a person, you want it gone fast. Where it's sealed inside a device that nobody touches, you want it to last.

The same reasoning explains why nuclear waste is such a problem: the isotopes that make good long-term fuel are exactly the ones that stay dangerous for millennia.

U5

"Half-life is the time taken for a nucleus to halve." This scores zero in every mark scheme. Why is it wrong — not just badly worded, but actually wrong?

Show answer

Because a single nucleus cannot halve. It has only two possible states: undecayed, or decayed. There is no halfway. And decay is random, so it is impossible to predict when any particular nucleus will go.

What halves is a property of the whole sample. Any of these score:

  • the time taken for the activity of a sample to halve
  • the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei in a sample to halve
  • the time taken for the mass of the isotope remaining to halve

Why the average is predictable when the individual is not: there are astronomically many nuclei in any real sample. Each has a fixed probability of decaying in a given time, so the total number decaying per second is highly consistent — just as no one can say which individuals will phone in the next minute, yet the total number of calls is very predictable.

Two of the three past papers define half-life. Learn one of the three wordings above and write it verbatim.

U6

A detector reads 424 counts per minute with a source present, and 24 counts per minute with it removed. A student calculates half-lives starting from 424. Why does this lose every mark in the question?

Show answer

Because 424 is not the activity of the source. It is the source plus the background radiation that is always present in the room.

The source's true contribution is 424 − 24 = 400 counts per minute. The background does not decay away, so it stays at 24 throughout while the source's contribution halves.

Why it destroys the whole answer: the halving chain from the wrong start never lands on the given values. From 400 you get a clean 400 → 200 → 100 → 50. From 424 you get 424 → 212 → 106 → 53, which matches nothing, so the number of half-lives is wrong and every subsequent figure follows it.

Mock 2: "Part (d) requires subtracting background first. Working from 424 gives a wrong number of half-lives and loses all three marks."

The habit: whenever a question mentions a background count at all, it is telling you to subtract it. It would not be there otherwise.

U7

Alpha radiation is the most strongly ionising but travels the shortest distance. Gamma is the least ionising and travels furthest. Why are these two properties linked, and what does it mean for choosing a source?

Show answer

They are linked because ionising is how radiation loses energy. Every time a particle rips an electron off an atom, it gives up some of its own energy.

An alpha particle is large and highly charged, so it ionises very heavily — and therefore runs out of energy within a few centimetres of air. Gamma is an electromagnetic wave with no charge, so it interacts only rarely, keeps its energy, and travels a long way.

So the two properties are the same fact seen twice: heavy interaction means short range; light interaction means long range.

What it means for choosing a source:

  • Medical tracer → gamma. It must escape the body to be detected, and it should do minimal damage on the way. Alpha would be absorbed in the first few centimetres of tissue, never reach the detector, and cause serious cell damage while doing so.
  • Smoke detector → alpha. You need heavy ionisation of the air to produce a current, and the tiny range means it is harmless a few centimetres outside the device.
  • Thickness monitoring → beta. It must be partly absorbed by the sheet. Alpha would be stopped completely and gamma would pass through unchanged, so neither would respond to thickness.
U8

A nucleus emits a beta particle — an electron — and its atomic number goes up by one. How can throwing something out of the nucleus increase the proton count, when the nucleus contains no electrons to begin with?

Show answer

Because the electron is created at the moment of decay, not stored and released.

Inside the nucleus, a neutron turns into a proton. Doing so produces an electron, which is immediately ejected — that is the beta particle.

Follow the bookkeeping:

  • Neutron count falls by one, proton count rises by one. Total nucleons unchanged, so the mass number stays the same.
  • One extra proton means the atomic number rises by one — it is now a different element.
  • Charge is conserved: a neutral neutron became a positive proton plus a negative electron. Which is why the beta particle is written 0−1e, and why the bottom numbers balance as 6 = 7 + (−1).

Contrast with alpha, which really is a chunk of the nucleus leaving: two protons and two neutrons, so mass number down 4 and atomic number down 2.

U9

Being irradiated and being contaminated both expose you to radiation. Which is more serious, and why does the distinction matter practically?

Show answer

Contamination is more serious.

Irradiation means radiation from a source outside you reaches your body. Move away, or put shielding in between, and it stops immediately. The object being irradiated does not become radioactive itself — which is why irradiated food is perfectly safe to eat.

Contamination means radioactive material has got onto or into you. The source is now travelling with you, so it keeps irradiating you until it is physically removed or decays away. You cannot walk away from it.

Why it changes the danger from each type: the ranking reverses. Outside the body, alpha is the least dangerous — it cannot even penetrate skin — while gamma is the worst because it reaches your organs. Inside the body, alpha is by far the worst, because all of its heavy ionising power is delivered directly into living tissue with nothing to absorb it first.

Practically: irradiation is handled with distance, shielding and short exposure times. Contamination is handled by preventing contact altogether — gloves, sealed sources, remote handling tongs.

Subtract the background first. Mock 2: "Working from [the uncorrected figure] gives a wrong number of half-lives and loses all three marks." The background does not decay — it stays constant while the source's contribution halves.

Whenever a question tells you a background count, it is telling you to subtract it. It would not be mentioned otherwise.

D11

Complete the following decay equations, and name the type of decay in each case. (6)

(a) 22688Ra → ______Rn + 42He
(b) 146C → ______N + 0−1e

Show answer

(a) 22286Rn 11 — alpha decay 1

Mass number falls by 4 (226 − 4 = 222); atomic number falls by 2 (88 − 2 = 86).

(b) 147N 11 — beta decay 1

Mass number unchanged; atomic number rises by 1 (6 → 7).

Check both lines every time. Top: 14 = 14 + 0 ✓. Bottom: 6 = 7 + (−1) ✓. The beta particle's atomic number of −1 is what makes the bottom line balance, and forgetting the minus sign is the usual error.

D12

A hospital uses a gamma-emitting source as a medical tracer.

(a) State the unit of activity. (1)
(b) Explain why gamma radiation is more suitable than alpha for this purpose. (3)
(c) Suggest why a source with a half-life of several years would be unsuitable. (2)

Show answer

(a) The becquerel (Bq) 1

(b) Gamma is highly penetrating, so it can pass out of the body to be detected 1

Alpha would be absorbed within a few centimetres of tissue and never reach the detector 1

Alpha is also strongly ionising, so it would cause serious damage to cells inside the body 1

(c) The source would remain active inside the patient long after the scan 1

continuing to irradiate their tissue for years and increasing the risk of cell damage or mutation, with no medical benefit 1

Tracers use short half-lives — hours, not years — for exactly this reason. The activity must last long enough for the scan and then fall away quickly.

D13

(a) State what is meant by ionisation. (1)
(b) Explain why ionising radiation is dangerous to living cells. (2)
(c) Describe two ways a worker handling a radioactive source can reduce their exposure. (2)

Show answer

(a) The removal of electron(s) from an atom 1

(b) The radiation removes electrons from atoms in the cell, which breaks up molecules including DNA 1

This damages cells and tissue, and can cause mutations which may lead to cancer 1

(c) Any two 11: keep exposure times short · stay as far from the source as possible · use shielding such as lead or concrete · handle the source with remote tongs rather than by hand · store it in a lead-lined container when not in use

D14

Explain the difference between contamination and irradiation, and state which is the more serious. (4)

Show answer

Irradiation is exposure to radiation from a source that is outside the body 1

It stops as soon as the source is removed or you move away, and the irradiated object does not become radioactive 1

Contamination is radioactive material getting onto or inside the body 1

Contamination is more serious, because the source stays with the person and continues to irradiate them until it is removed or decays 1

Note that the ranking of the three radiations reverses between the two cases. Outside the body gamma is the most dangerous and alpha the least, because alpha cannot penetrate skin. Inside the body alpha is by far the worst, because its heavy ionisation is delivered straight into living tissue.

D15

(a) Describe the process of nuclear fission of a uranium-235 nucleus. (3)
(b) Explain how a chain reaction can occur. (2)
(c) Describe the roles of the control rods and the moderator in a nuclear reactor. (3)

Show answer

(a) A neutron collides with the U-235 nucleus and is absorbed 1

The nucleus splits into two radioactive daughter nuclei and a small number of neutrons 1

Energy is released, mainly as the kinetic energy of the fission products 1

(b) The neutrons released by one fission can strike other U-235 nuclei 1

causing them to split as well, so the reaction sustains itself 1

(c) Control rods absorb neutrons 1, and are lowered further in to slow the reaction or withdrawn to speed it up, keeping the rate steady 1

The moderator slows the neutrons down, because slow neutrons are much more likely to be absorbed and cause further fission 1

Shielding is a separate idea: thick concrete and lead around the reactor absorb the radiation and protect the workers outside.

D16

(a) Explain the difference between nuclear fusion and nuclear fission. (3)
(b) Explain why nuclear fusion does not occur at low temperatures and pressures. (3)

Show answer

(a) In fission a large nucleus is split into two smaller nuclei 1

In fusion two small nuclei are joined together to create a larger nucleus 1

Both release energy; fusion does so through a loss of mass, and it is the energy source of stars 1

(b) Both nuclei are positively charged 1

so they repel each other electrostatically 1

Only at very high temperatures and pressures do they move fast enough to overcome this repulsion and get close enough to fuse 1

The word the mark scheme wants is electrostatic repulsion — between the positive charges. "They push each other away" without naming why scores less.

D17

Explain why radioactive decay is described as a random process, and how this is consistent with a source having a predictable half-life. (3)

Show answer

It is impossible to predict when any individual nucleus will decay 1

Each nucleus has the same chance of decaying in a given time, regardless of how long it has already existed 1

A real sample contains an enormous number of nuclei, so although each individual event is unpredictable, the average behaviour of the whole sample is highly consistent — which is why the half-life is a reliable, repeatable quantity 1

The two ideas are not in tension. Nobody can say which individuals will make a phone call in the next minute, yet the total number of calls made is very predictable.
