Magnetism and Electromagnetism

Magnets, the fields that currents make, the force that drives motors and loudspeakers, and the induced voltage that makes generators and transformers work. It came up in every one of the ten real papers. This is a Year 11 topic, taught in Year 11 for the GCSE in summer 2027. Electromagnets, field patterns for coils, the force on a moving charge and everything about transformers are Physics-only (P).

02How it actually works

The whole topic hangs on three ideas. A current makes a magnetic field. A current in a magnetic field feels a force. A changing magnetic field induces a voltage. Motors and loudspeakers use the second; generators and transformers use the third. The units you need are the ampere (A), the volt (V) and the watt (W) (spec 6.1).

Magnets, magnetic materials and induced magnetism

Spec 6.2, 6.3, 6.5 · 2024 Edexcel 1P Q6(b), (d) · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q2(b) · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1P Q1(b)(i), (c)

Like poles repel, unlike poles attract, and a magnet attracts magnetic materials, which are only iron, steel, nickel and cobalt. Copper, aluminium, zinc, plastic and wood are not magnetic. In 2024 the answer was nickel; copper, plastic and zinc were the wrong options.

When a piece of iron is placed in a magnetic field, it becomes a magnet itself. This is induced magnetism. The chain for "why is the iron attracted":

  1. The iron is in the magnet's field, so magnetism is induced in it.
  2. The end of the iron nearest the magnet becomes the opposite pole (next to a south pole it becomes a north pole).
  3. Opposite poles attract, so the iron is pulled towards the magnet.

This is why a magnetic material is attracted to either pole, and never repelled. Only a magnet can be repelled by a magnet.

Hard and soft magnetic materials behave differently when the field is removed:

MaterialBehaviourUsed for
Magnetically hard (steel)Hard to magnetise, but keeps its magnetism; hard to demagnetisePermanent magnets
Magnetically soft (iron)Easily magnetised and loses its magnetism easily when the field is removedElectromagnet cores, transformer cores

✗ "Iron is a soft magnet." ✓ "Iron is a soft magnetic material; it loses its magnetism when it is removed from the field." In 2024 the scheme said "ignore iron is a soft magnet" and "ignore iron is a temporary magnet".

✗ "The iron is charged." Magnetism is not charge. The 2024 scheme: "ignore references to charge".

Field lines, the plotting practical and the uniform field

Spec 6.4, 6.6, 6.7 · 2024 Edexcel 1P Q6(a), (c) · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q2(a), (c) · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1P Q1(a), (b)(ii), (d)

A magnetic field line shows the direction of the force on a north pole placed at that point, so field lines go from north to south outside a magnet. Where the lines are closer together the field is stronger; further apart means weaker. Field lines never cross.

2024 asked for arrows on the lines near a south pole: they point towards the south pole. Any arrow pointing away from it was rejected.

Practical: plotting the field of a bar magnet

Compass method (3 marks): put the magnet on paper. Place a plotting compass near one pole and mark the direction its needle points with a dot. Move the compass so its tail is on the dot and mark again. Repeat, then join the dots to make a field line. Start from different points to draw more lines.

Iron filings method (3 marks): put a sheet of paper or card over the magnet, sprinkle iron filings thinly on it, then tap the card gently so the filings line up along the field lines.

Filings show the shape but not the direction. Only a compass shows the direction.

For two bar magnets, do the same with the magnets placed end to end. Between opposite poles the lines join across the gap; between like poles they push apart, leaving a neutral point in the middle where the field is zero.

A uniform field is the same strength and direction everywhere. To make one, place two magnets with opposite poles (N facing S) close together and parallel. Between them the field lines are:

  1. straight
  2. parallel
  3. evenly spaced

Any two of those three words earned the 2 marks in 2023 Jan 1P Q2(c).

✗ "The field gets weaker because the force is smaller." ✓ "The field lines get further apart, which shows a weaker field." 2024's scheme said "ignore references to forces" here: the answer is about the spacing of the lines.

The magnetic field around a current

Spec 6.8, 6.10P · 2023 Edexcel Jan 2P Q6(a) · 2022 Edexcel Jan 2P Q4

A current in a wire produces a magnetic field around it. Switch the current off and the field disappears. Reverse the current and the field reverses. The field patterns (Physics only, 6.10P):

ShapeField pattern
Straight wireConcentric circles centred on the wire, in a plane at right angles to it. The circles get further apart further from the wire, because the field is weaker there.
Flat circular coilCircles around each side of the coil, which bend together so the lines through the centre of the coil are nearly straight.
Solenoid (a long coil)Like a bar magnet outside. Inside, the lines are straight, parallel and evenly spaced: a strong, uniform field.

For the direction around a straight wire, use the right-hand grip rule: point your right thumb along the current, and your fingers curl the way the field goes. So a current going down through a card, seen from above, gives a clockwise field. That was the direction arrow the 2023 Jan scheme wanted.

2023 Jan 2P Q6(a)(i): the 3-mark drawing

One circle centred on the wire, in the plane of the card · at least two concentric circles · a direction arrow (clockwise for current going down). To show the pattern, use iron filings and tap the card, or several plotting compasses around the wire.

Electromagnets

Spec 6.9P · 2024 Edexcel 2P Q6(a) · 2022 Edexcel Jan 2P Q6(a)

An electromagnet is a coil of wire (a solenoid) wrapped around a soft iron core, with a current in the coil. The four things the mark schemes look for:

  1. a coil of insulated wire (or a solenoid)
  2. a current in the coil, from a cell, battery or power supply
  3. a core inside the coil
  4. made of a named magnetic material: (soft) iron

The iron core makes the field much stronger. It must be soft iron so that the electromagnet loses its magnetism when the current is switched off. That on/off control is the whole point of an electromagnet: scrapyard cranes, relays, door locks and MRI scanners.

To make an electromagnet stronger: increase the current, add more turns to the coil, or use an iron core (if it has none).

2024 2P Q6(a) scheme: "ignore steel", "reject indication of a complete (iron) loop" and "reject explicit reference to bar magnet".

✗ "A bar magnet with wire round it." ✗ "A steel core." ✓ "A coil of wire wrapped around a soft iron core, with a current in the coil."

The motor effect and the left-hand rule

Spec 6.12, 6.13, 6.14 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 2P Q6(b) · 2024 Edexcel 2P Q6(b)(i)

A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force, because the field made by the current interacts with the magnet's field. This is the motor effect. The chain:

  1. The current in the wire produces its own magnetic field around the wire.
  2. This field interacts with the field of the magnet.
  3. The result is a force on the wire.

The force is biggest when the wire is at right angles to the field. If the wire is parallel to the field there is no force.

Fleming's left-hand rule gives the direction. Hold the thumb and first two fingers of your left hand at right angles to each other:

  1. First finger = Field (north to south)
  2. seCond finger = Current (positive to negative)
  3. thuMb = Motion (the direction of the force)

What changes the force (6.14):

ChangeEffect on the force
Bigger currentBigger force
Stronger magnetic fieldBigger force
Reverse the currentForce reverses direction
Reverse the field (swap the poles)Force reverses direction
Reverse bothForce is in the same direction as before

Two parallel wires carrying current in the same direction attract. Each wire sits in the field of the other, so each has a current in a magnetic field and feels a force. The forces are equal in size and opposite in direction (2023 Jan 2P Q6(b)).

✗ "The wires attract because they have opposite charges." The 2023 Jan scheme: "reject reference to opposite charge". It is a magnetic force between currents, not an electrostatic one.

✗ Using the right hand. The left hand is for the force on a current (motors). The right hand only gives the direction of the field around a wire.

The force on a moving charged particle

Spec 6.11P · 2024 Edexcel 2P Q6(b)

A charged particle moving through a magnetic field feels a force, as long as it is not moving parallel to the field. A current is just moving charge, so this is the same motor effect.

Use the left-hand rule with the second finger along the current:

  1. For a positive particle (a proton), the current is in the direction it moves.
  2. For a negative particle (an electron), the current is opposite to the direction it moves.

The force is always at right angles to the velocity. So it changes the particle's direction but not its speed, and the particle curves round in an arc. As the direction changes, the direction of the force changes too, but its size stays the same.

2024 2P Q6(b)(iii) asked why the proton's velocity changes. The answer is that its direction changes. The scheme said "ignore idea of speed or magnitude of velocity changes". The speed does not change.

Using the motor effect: d.c. motors and loudspeakers

Spec 6.12 · 2022 Edexcel Jan 1P Q12(b) · 2023 Edexcel 1P Q12(c)

In a motor, the forces on the two sides of a coil are in opposite directions, so the coil turns.

  1. There is a current in the coil, so the coil has a magnetic field.
  2. This field interacts with the field of the permanent magnet.
  3. This produces a force on each side of the coil.
  4. The current goes one way along one side and back along the other, so the forces on opposite sides are in opposite directions. One side is pushed up, the other down, and the coil rotates.

A split-ring commutator reverses the current in the coil every half turn, so the forces keep turning the coil the same way. To make the motor faster: bigger current, stronger magnet, more turns on the coil. To reverse it: reverse the current or swap the poles.

A loudspeaker uses the same effect with alternating current:

  1. An alternating current passes through a coil attached to the cone.
  2. The coil's field interacts with the field of a permanent magnet, causing a force on the coil.
  3. The a.c. keeps changing direction, so the force keeps changing direction.
  4. The coil and cone are pushed back and forth: they vibrate, making sound waves in the air.

2022 Jan 1P Q12(b)(i) scheme: "ignore references to attraction / repulsion". ✗ "The north pole of the coil is attracted to the south pole of the magnet." ✓ "The fields interact, producing a force; the forces on opposite sides of the coil are in opposite directions."

2023 1P Q12(c) scheme on the loudspeaker: "ignore fields cutting". Cutting field lines is induction, a different effect. A loudspeaker is the motor effect.

Electromagnetic induction

Spec 6.15 · 2023 Edexcel Jan 1P Q2(d) · 2022 Edexcel 1P Q12(a)–(c) · 2023 Edexcel 1P Q12(b)(ii)

A voltage is induced in a conductor when it moves through a magnetic field and cuts the field lines, or when the magnetic field through a coil changes. If the conductor is part of a complete circuit, the induced voltage drives an induced current.

No movement and no change means no induced voltage. A wire held still between two magnets gives a zero reading. A wire moved along the field lines (not cutting them) also gives zero.

To increase the induced voltage:

  1. move the wire (or magnet) faster
  2. use a stronger magnet (or move the magnets closer together)
  3. turn the wire into a coil, or use more turns on the coil

To reverse it: move the wire the other way, or reverse the poles. 2022 1P Q12 showed a reading of 2.8 mA; with the poles reversed the answer was −2.8 mA (same size, opposite direction); moving faster gave a bigger reading.

The metal detector in 2023 1P Q12 used a changing field instead of movement: the a.c. in the transmitter coil makes a changing magnetic field; this field cuts the gold ring; an alternating voltage is induced in the ring.

2023 Jan 1P Q2(d)(i) scheme: "ignore wire interacting with field lines". ✗ "The wire interacts with the field." ✓ "The wire cuts the magnetic field lines."

Part (d)(ii) also said "ignore "bigger" magnets". A bigger magnet is not necessarily a stronger one. Say stronger.

Generators

Spec 6.16

A generator makes electricity by rotating a magnet inside a coil, or rotating a coil inside a magnetic field, so that the coil keeps cutting field lines and a voltage is induced.

TypeWhat turnsExample
Rotating magnetA magnet spins inside a fixed coil, so the field through the coil keeps changingA bicycle dynamo
Rotating coilA coil spins between the poles of a magnet, so its sides cut the field linesA simple a.c. generator

Each side of the coil moves up through the field, then down through it, so the induced voltage keeps reversing. The output is alternating.

The size of the induced voltage increases if you:

  1. rotate the coil or magnet faster
  2. use a stronger magnet
  3. use more turns on the coil

Rotating faster also makes the a.c. change direction more often, so the frequency goes up too.

Transformers and the two transformer equations

Spec 6.17P, 6.19P, 6.20P · 2022 Edexcel 2P Q3(a), (c) · 2023 Edexcel 2P Q6(a)(ii)–(iii), (b)

A transformer is two coils of wire, the primary and the secondary, wound on the same soft iron core. It changes the size of an alternating voltage.

How it works, the 4-mark chain from 2022 2P Q3(c):

  1. An alternating current in the primary coil produces a magnetic field.
  2. Because the current keeps changing, this is a changing magnetic field in the iron core.
  3. The core carries the changing field to the secondary coil, where the field lines cut the coil.
  4. This induces an alternating voltage in the secondary coil.

Step-up transformer: more turns on the secondary, so the output voltage is bigger. Step-down: fewer turns on the secondary, so the output voltage is smaller.

input voltage ÷ output voltage = primary turns ÷ secondary turns
both voltages in the same unit · the turns are just numbers

input power = output power: primary voltage × primary current = secondary voltage × secondary current
for a 100% efficient transformer · voltages in V · currents in A

2023 2P Q6(a)(iii): the 3-mark calculation

Input 15 kV, output 340 kV, 1400 turns on the primary. Find the secondary turns.

Substitution: 15 ÷ 340 = 1400 ÷ ns · rearrangement: ns = 1400 × 340 ÷ 15 · evaluation: ns = 31 733, about 32 000 turns.

The kV cancel out, so there is no need to convert to volts, as long as both voltages are in the same unit.

The power equation follows because a 100% efficient transformer wastes no energy: input power = output power. So if the voltage is stepped up, the current is stepped down by the same factor. 2022's phone charger: output 5.0 V × 1.2 A = 6.0 W, so input current = 6.0 ÷ 230 = 0.026 A.

2023 2P Q6(b): a transformer will not work on constant d.c. A constant current gives a constant magnetic field; induction needs a changing field; so no voltage is induced in the secondary.

When stating the formula (2023 2P Q6(a)(ii)) the scheme said "reject phrase `turns ratio'". Write the full equation, input voltage ÷ output voltage = primary turns ÷ secondary turns, not "voltage ratio = turns ratio".

Transformers in the National Grid

Spec 6.18P · 2023 Edexcel 2P Q6(a)(i)

Electricity is sent across the country at a very high voltage so that the current in the cables is small, which means less heating and less energy wasted.

power = current × voltage
power in watts (W) · current in amperes (A) · voltage in volts (V)

  1. At the power station, a step-up transformer increases the voltage to hundreds of thousands of volts.
  2. For the same power, P = I × V, a higher voltage means a lower current.
  3. A lower current means less heating in the cables, so less energy is wasted and thinner cables can be used.
  4. Near homes, step-down transformers reduce the voltage to a safe level, 230 V in the UK.

✗ "A high voltage wastes less energy." That skips the key step. ✓ "Stepping up the voltage reduces the current; a smaller current means less heating in the wires." Those are the two marks in 2023 2P Q6(a)(i). The scheme also said "ignore unqualified reference to efficiency".

Protecting the user: insulation, earthing, fuses and circuit breakers

Spec 2.2 · 2023 Edexcel 1P Q3(b)

Each safety feature either stops you touching a live part, or cuts off the current when it gets too big.

FeatureHow it protects
InsulationPlastic or rubber around the wires. Plastic is an electrical insulator, so you cannot touch the live metal.
Double insulationThe whole outer case is made of plastic (an insulator), so even if a wire comes loose the case cannot become live. No earth wire is needed.

03Cheat sheet

The sentences behind most of the marks

Field lines: go from N to S; closer together means a stronger field.

Uniform field: opposite poles facing; lines straight, parallel, evenly spaced.

Induced magnetism: the near end of the iron becomes the opposite pole, and opposite poles attract.

Electromagnet: a coil with a current, wrapped round a soft iron core.

Motor effect: the field of the current interacts with the magnet's field, producing a force. No force if the wire is parallel to the field.

Induction: the conductor cuts the field lines, or the field through the coil changes, so a voltage is induced.

Transformer: a.c. in the primary makes a changing field in the core, which induces a voltage in the secondary. No change, no voltage: it will not work on d.c.

Grid: step up the voltage, so the current is lower, so there is less heating in the cables.

04Understanding it

These are the "why" questions behind the exam answers. Try each one properly before opening the answer.

U1

An unmagnetised iron nail is attracted to the north pole of a magnet, and also to the south pole. Why is it never repelled? And what is the only sure test that a piece of metal is a magnet?

Think about Which pole is induced at the end nearest the magnet?

Show answer

The nail becomes a magnet itself when it is in the field: magnetism is induced in it.

The end nearest the magnet always becomes the opposite pole. Next to a north pole it becomes a south pole; next to a south pole it becomes a north pole. Opposite poles attract, so the nail is attracted either way.

So attraction proves nothing: a magnet attracts both magnets and magnetic materials. Repulsion is the only sure test. Only two magnets can push each other away.

U2

Permanent magnets are made of steel. Electromagnet and transformer cores are made of soft iron. Why not the other way round?

Show answer

A permanent magnet has to keep its magnetism. Steel is magnetically hard: once magnetised, it stays magnetised.

An electromagnet has to lose its magnetism when the current is switched off, or the crane could never drop the car. Soft iron does that.

A transformer core is even more demanding. The a.c. reverses many times a second, so the core has to be magnetised, demagnetised and remagnetised the other way, over and over. Soft iron follows those changes easily. A steel core would resist them.

U3

A wire carrying a current lies between two magnets. Turned one way it feels a strong force. Turned so it runs along the field lines, it feels no force at all. Why?

Show answer

The force comes from charges moving across the field. A current is moving charge, and a charge moving through a magnetic field feels a force only if it is not moving parallel to the field (spec 6.11P).

At right angles, the charges cross the field lines as directly as possible, so the force is biggest. Along the field lines, the charges never cross them, so there is no force.

This is also why the left-hand rule only works with your fingers at right angles. The rule describes the case where the current is perpendicular to the field.

U4

Without a split-ring commutator, a motor coil turns about a quarter of a turn and then just rocks to a stop. Why? And how does the commutator fix it?

Show answer

One side of the coil is pushed up and the other down. That turns the coil until it is upright. Past that point, the side that was pushed up is now on the other side of the axle. The same forces now turn the coil back. So it rocks and stops.

The commutator reverses the current in the coil every half turn. Just as the sides swap places, the current in each side reverses, so the forces reverse too. The side on the left is always pushed up and the side on the right always pushed down, so the coil keeps turning the same way.

U5

A student connects a transformer to a battery. The secondary coil gives no output, even though there is a current in the primary and the core is magnetised. Explain.

Show answer

The core is magnetised, so there is a magnetic field through the secondary coil. But induction does not need a field; it needs a changing field.

A battery gives a constant d.c. current, so the field in the core is constant. Nothing changes through the secondary coil, so no voltage is induced.

That is the whole reason mains electricity is a.c. and why transformers only work on a.c. (There is a brief flick of voltage at the instant the switch is closed, because the field changes as it builds up. Then nothing.)

U6

A power station sends out 100 MW. Compare the current in the cables at 25 kV and at 400 kV. Why does the difference matter?

Show answer

From P = I × V, the current is I = P ÷ V.

At 25 kV: I = 100 000 000 ÷ 25 000 = 4000 A. At 400 kV: I = 100 000 000 ÷ 400 000 = 250 A. The current is 16 times smaller.

The heating in a cable depends on the current. A smaller current means much less heating, so much less energy is wasted heating the cables and the surroundings. That is why the step-up transformer is there.

The high voltage is dangerous, so step-down transformers bring it down to 230 V before it reaches homes.

U7

A step-up transformer turns 230 V into 4600 V. A student says "you get twenty times more out than you put in". What is wrong?

Show answer

The voltage goes up twenty times, but the power does not. At best (100% efficient) input power = output power, so Vp × Ip = Vs × Is.

If the voltage goes up by 20 times, the current must go down by 20 times. With 2.0 A in the primary: 230 × 2.0 = 460 W in, so the secondary current is 460 ÷ 4600 = 0.10 A.

A transformer trades voltage for current. It can never give out more energy than it takes in; that would break conservation of energy.

U8

A metal kettle has an earth wire and a fuse. A plastic hairdryer has a fuse but no earth wire. Why does the kettle need both, and why is the hairdryer safe without an earth?

Show answer

The kettle: if the live wire comes loose and touches the metal case, the case becomes live. Anyone touching it would get a shock.

The earth wire connects the case to earth through a very low resistance. So a large current flows straight to earth. That large current is more than the fuse rating, so the fuse melts and breaks the circuit. The earth wire and fuse work as a team: the earth wire makes the big current happen, and the fuse then cuts it off.

The hairdryer is double insulated. Its case is plastic, an insulator, so a loose live wire cannot make the case live. There is nothing for an earth wire to protect. It still has a fuse, to stop the cable overheating if too much current flows.
