Forces and Motion

The second-largest block on the paper, and the one where marks are lost to language rather than physics. Get the vocabulary exact and this topic pays.

~16% of marks Spec 1.1 – 1.33P Appeared in 2024, 2025 Est. 2–3 sessions

01What actually comes up

Across the three past papers in the pack, this topic supplied roughly 42 of 255 marks. It never appears as one big question — it's split between a short moments calculation and a long multi-part question at the end of the paper.

Two patterns are worth noticing. Moments turns up every year, usually early, usually with the equation asked for separately as a free mark. And the paper's biggest single question is often this topic, ending in a long "explain in terms of forces" answer worth 5 or 6 marks.

On the 2025 paper, the 5-mark forces question was where the year group haemorrhaged marks. The examiner wrote: "A number of answers focussed either partly or exclusively on energy which was ignored. The danger with introducing energy concepts here is that they become muddled with forces. Unbalanced forces cause accelerations, NOT GPE/KE."

They also noted that several students "didn't see this question, despite it saying clearly on the page before that the question continued on the next page." Fifteen marks, at the back of the paper. Turn the page.

02How it actually works

This topic is 16% of the paper but it never arrives as one big question — it is a short moments calculation early on and a long multi-part question at the back. Every heading below says where it was actually asked. Note throughout that this paper uses g = 10 N/kg, which is what the mark schemes use.

Moments — the marks that appear every single year

Spec 1.30P · 2024 Q7(b) · 2025 Q3(a) · Mock 1 Q3(a) — and the equation is asked for separately, as its own mark

A moment is the turning effect of a force about a pivot.

moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot
moment in newton metres (N m) · force in newtons (N) · distance in metres (m)

The word perpendicular is doing real work. The distance you want is measured at right angles from the pivot to the line the force acts along — not along the arm if the force is at an angle to it. This is also why a longer spanner loosens a stubborn nut without you pushing harder: you have increased d, so the moment increases.

The unit conversion is the whole trap. Distances are given in centimetres and answers are demanded in newton metres. Mock 1 gave 24 cm and wanted N m: 24 cm = 0.24 m, so 85 × 0.24 = 20.4 N m. An answer of 2040 is a unit error, not a physics error, and it costs the marks anyway.

But check which unit is actually demanded. In 2025 the answer was wanted in N cm and no conversion was needed at all — converting would have been wrong. Read the demanded unit before you convert anything.

The principle of moments

Spec 1.32P, 1.33P · 2024 Q7(c) — worth 3 marks including the statement

When something balances and does not turn:

total clockwise moment = total anticlockwise moment

That single sentence is often a mark on its own, so write it down before calculating. Then the method is always the same:

  1. Find the moment you can calculate completely — force × distance.
  2. Set it equal to the moment on the other side, written as (unknown force) × (its distance).
  3. Rearrange and divide.

In 2024: the gate's weight gave 480 × 0.84 = 403 N m, and the push acted at 3.2 m, so F = 403.2 ÷ 3.2 = 130 N. Notice the answer is smaller than the weight — acting further from the pivot means less force is needed for the same turning effect.

Related and examinable: the weight of an object acts through its centre of gravity (spec 1.31P). For a uniform beam or rule that is its midpoint, which is how you handle the beam's own weight in a balancing problem.

Vectors, scalars, and adding forces up

Spec 1.13, 1.14, 1.15 · Mock 1 Q10(b)(i)

A scalar has size only — mass, speed, distance, time, energy. A vector has size and direction — force, weight, velocity, acceleration, momentum.

That distinction is why speed and velocity are different words. Speed is how fast; velocity is how fast and which way. An object going round a bend at a steady 20 m/s has constant speed but changing velocity — and because velocity is changing, it is accelerating, even though the speedometer is still.

Because force is a vector, forces along a line combine by adding those in one direction and subtracting those in the other. The answer — the resultant force — needs a direction as well as a number. In mock 1: 750 N down minus 300 N up gives 450 N, and the mark scheme wanted "450 N downwards".

Friction always opposes motion (spec 1.16), so it points backwards along the direction of travel. Air resistance is friction with the air and behaves the same way.

Speed, velocity and acceleration

Spec 1.4, 1.6 · 2024 Q8(d) · Mock 2 Q5(b), (d)

average speed = distance moved ÷ time taken
speed in metres/second (m/s) · distance in metres (m) · time in seconds (s)

a = (v − u) ÷ t
a = acceleration in m/s² · v = final velocity (m/s) · u = initial velocity (m/s) · t = time (s)

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity — how much the velocity changes each second. So m/s² reads as "metres per second, per second". A negative acceleration means slowing down, and mark schemes accept either "deceleration of 2.4 m/s²" or "acceleration of −2.4 m/s²".

2024 ran this backwards to find a reaction time: it gave a speed and a thinking distance, and wanted the time. Same equation, rearranged to t = distance ÷ speed.

Motion graphs — the three things they ask

Spec 1.3, 1.7, 1.8, 1.9 · Mock 2 Q5 · 2024 Q8(c)

GraphGradient meansArea underneath means
Distance–timeSpeed. Steeper = faster; flat = stationaryNothing useful

The area rule is worth understanding rather than memorising. For a flat section, distance = speed × time, which is exactly the area of that rectangle. For a sloping section the speed changes, but the same reasoning applied to thin strips gives the area of the triangle. So the area is always the distance.

The method for "determine the total distance"

Chop the graph into rectangles and triangles, work out each, and add. From mock 2:

Triangle 0–10 s: ½ × 10 × 12 = 60 m  ·  rectangle 10–25 s: 15 × 12 = 180 m  ·  triangle 25–30 s: ½ × 5 × 12 = 30 m

Total = 270 m. Then average speed for the journey = 270 ÷ 30 = 9.0 m/s.

Note that the average speed is not the average of the velocities — it is the total distance over the total time. These give different answers and only one is right.

When you are asked to plot, the marks are for labelling both axes with quantity and unit, choosing a scale that uses most of the grid, plotting every point correctly, and joining them as instructed.

v² = u² + 2as — the one for "no time given"

Spec 1.10 · 2024 Q8(e) · 2025 Q10(c)(i)

v² = u² + 2as
v = final speed (m/s) · u = initial speed (m/s) · a = acceleration (m/s²) · s = distance moved (m)

Reach for this whenever a question gives you two speeds and a distance but no time. That is its entire purpose, and spotting it is most of the difficulty.

Two situations where signs matter. Something slowing to rest has v = 0, so the equation gives a negative a — report it as a deceleration. Something thrown upwards also has v = 0 at the top, with a = −10 m/s²: 2025's rubber band gave 0 = 13² + 2 × (−10) × s, so s = 169 ÷ 20 = 8.5 m.

In 2024 candidates used the stopping distance or the thinking distance where the question wanted the braking distance. That capped them at 3 of 4 marks. Read which distance is being asked for — the graph gives you all three.

2025: "A significant number gave 2 d.p. instead of 2 s.f." — 8.45 was rejected where 8.5 was wanted. Give the number of significant figures asked for.

Weight, mass and F = ma

Spec 1.17, 1.18 · Mock 1 Q10(a), (b)(ii)

W = m × g
W = weight in newtons (N) · m = mass in kilograms (kg) · g = gravitational field strength in N/kg (use 10)

F = m × a
F = resultant force in newtons (N) · m = mass in kilograms (kg) · a = acceleration in m/s²

Mass and weight are not the same thing. Mass is the quantity of matter in kilograms and does not change wherever you take it. Weight is the force of gravity on that mass, in newtons, and changes with the gravitational field — the same astronaut has the same mass on the Moon but about a sixth of the weight.

The critical word in F = ma is resultant. It is not any old force in the problem, it is the total after everything has been combined. Mock 1's sequence is the standard one and worth learning as a unit: weight = 75 × 10 = 750 N, resultant = 750 − 300 = 450 N, acceleration = 450 ÷ 75 = 6.0 m/s².

Falling objects and terminal velocity — in terms of forces

2025 Q10(c)(ii) — 5 marks · Mock 1 Q10(c) — 4 marks. "This is where the year group haemorrhaged marks."

This is the single most valuable thing in the module, and the marks are lost on how the answer is written rather than on the physics.

The sequence, which is one mark per step:

  1. At the start, weight acts downwards and there is little or no air resistance, so there is a resultant downward force and the object accelerates.
  2. As its speed increases, the air resistance increases.
  3. So the resultant force decreases, and therefore the acceleration decreases — it is still speeding up, just less quickly.
  4. Eventually air resistance equals weight, so the resultant force is zero.
  5. With no resultant force there is no acceleration, so it falls at a constant velocity — terminal velocity.

The examiner on 2025 Q10: answers "focussed either partly or exclusively on energy which was ignored… Unbalanced forces cause accelerations, NOT GPE/KE."

If the question says "in terms of forces", every mention of kinetic or gravitational potential energy scores zero, no matter how correct it is. Mock 1 makes the point sharply by asking part (c) in terms of forces and part (d) in terms of energy — the same fall, and answers that work for one score nothing for the other. Read which one you are being asked for.

Two words to get right: say weight, not "gravity" — "gravity" was ignored. And say air resistance or drag, not "wind resistance", which was not accepted.

One more thing from that paper: "several students didn't see this question — fifteen marks, at the back of the paper." Turn the page.

Stopping distance

Spec 1.19, 1.20 · 2024 Q8(a)–(c)

stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance

Two separate phases, affected by different things — which is exactly what the multiple-choice parts test:

What it isIncreased by
Thinking distanceHow far you travel while reacting, before the brakes are touchedGreater speed · longer reaction time (tiredness, alcohol, drugs, distraction)
Braking distanceHow far you travel once the brakes are appliedGreater speed · greater mass · wet or icy road · worn brakes or tyres

The distinction that earns marks: road conditions affect only the braking distance — ice does not slow your reactions. Tiredness affects only the thinking distance — it does not change the grip of the tyres. Speed increases both.

Hooke's law and elastic behaviour

Spec 1.22, 1.23, 1.24 · 2024 Q7(a), (d) · 2025 Q10(b)

Hang increasing weights on a spring, measure the extension each time, and plot force against extension. The result starts as a straight line through the origin: extension is directly proportional to force. That initial straight part is Hooke's law.

Beyond a certain point — the elastic limit — the line bends and the material stops obeying it.

Elastic behaviour is the ability of a material to return to its original shape once the forces deforming it are removed. On a graph, that shows as the line returning to the origin when it is unloaded, which is exactly what 2025 asked for.

2024 asked what happens when a spring is stretched past its elastic limit. The mark scheme wanted the elastic limit being exceeded, and permanent deformation — it does not return to its original shape. "The spring breaks" was ignored: the point is permanent deformation, not breakage.

In part (a), "force is proportional to extension" earned both marks — describing the graph as a straight line through the origin and saying the relationship is linear are what is wanted, not a vague "it goes up".

Newton's third law

Spec 1.29P

For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. The part that is actually tested: the two forces in a third-law pair act on two different objects, and they are always the same type of force.

So a book resting on a table is not a third-law pair, even though the two forces are equal and opposite: the book's weight and the table's push both act on the book, and they are different kinds of force. They are balanced forces on one object, which is a different idea. The genuine third-law partner of the book's weight is the book's gravitational pull on the Earth.

The terminal velocity script

Learn this as a three-beat sequence. Almost every version of the question wants these beats in this order.

  1. At the start, weight is greater than air resistance, so there is a resultant force downwards and the object accelerates.
  2. As speed increases, air resistance increases. The resultant force therefore gets smaller, so the acceleration decreases.
  3. When air resistance equals weight, the resultant force is zero, so the object travels at a constant velocity — terminal velocity.

The variant that catches people

The 2025 paper asked about a rubber band falling from its maximum height and explicitly said assume it does not reach terminal velocity. So the script above doesn't apply — but the reasoning does. The answer needed: weight acts downwards, air resistance acts upwards and is smaller, so there is a resultant force downwards; the band accelerates; as it speeds up air resistance grows, so the resultant force decreases and the acceleration decreases — but it never reaches zero.

Read the assumptions in the box. They are there because they change the answer.

04Understanding it

These aren't exam questions. They're the questions worth thinking about slowly, because they attack the misconceptions that make exam questions go wrong. Try each one properly before opening the answer.

U1

A book lies at rest on a table. The table pushes up on the book with a force equal in size to the book's weight.

Is this an example of Newton's third law? Explain your reasoning.

Think about Which object is each force acting on?

Show answer

No. This catches almost everyone.

Newton's third law pairs act on different objects. The book's weight is a force from the Earth on the book; its third-law partner is the force from the book on the Earth. The table's push on the book has as its partner the book's push down on the table.

The weight and the table's push are both acting on the same object — the book. They're equal here because the book is in equilibrium (resultant force zero), not because of Newton's third law. If you put the book in a lift accelerating upwards, they'd no longer be equal — but the third-law pairs would still be equal.

The test: a third-law pair always acts on two different bodies, and is always the same type of force.

U2

A skydiver reaches terminal velocity. She then opens her parachute.

Describe what happens to her speed immediately afterwards, and explain why she reaches a new, lower terminal velocity — in terms of forces.

Think about What changed when the parachute opened? Weight, or air resistance?

Show answer

Opening the parachute massively increases the surface area, so air resistance increases sharply. Her weight is unchanged.

Air resistance is now greater than weight, so there's a resultant force upwards. She therefore decelerates — note she is still moving downwards, just more slowly. Deceleration is not the same as moving backwards.

As she slows, air resistance falls again. When air resistance once more equals weight, the resultant force is zero and she falls at a new, lower constant velocity.

Why this matters: the whole question turns on the fact that air resistance depends on speed and area, weight doesn't. If you can say that sentence, you can answer any version of this question.

U3

Why does the area under a velocity–time graph give the distance travelled? Explain it without just asserting that it does.

Think about Start with a graph that's a flat horizontal line.

Show answer

Take the simplest case first: an object moving at a constant 5 m/s for 4 s. The graph is a horizontal line at 5, and the region under it is a rectangle of height 5 and width 4. Its area is 20 — and the distance travelled is speed × time = 5 × 4 = 20 m. The area is the multiplication.

Now take a sloping line. Chop the region under it into very thin vertical strips. Over each strip the velocity is almost constant, so each strip's area is a small distance. Add all the strips and you have the total distance.

This is why the units work: the vertical axis is in m/s and the horizontal in s, so area has units of m/s × s = m.

Practical payoff: when the shape is a trapezium, you can either use the trapezium formula or split it into a rectangle plus a triangle. The second is harder to get wrong under pressure.

U4

A car doubles its speed from 15 m/s to 30 m/s.

(a) What happens to its thinking distance?
(b) What happens to its braking distance?
(c) Why are the answers different?

Think about Which equation governs each one?

Show answer

(a) Thinking distance doubles. During the reaction time the car moves at constant speed, so distance = speed × time. Reaction time is unchanged, so doubling speed doubles the distance.

(b) Braking distance quadruples. Rearranging v² = u² + 2as with final speed zero gives s = u² ÷ (2a). Distance depends on the square of the initial speed, so doubling speed multiplies the distance by four.

(c) Because thinking distance is linear in speed and braking distance is quadratic. This is exactly why the graph in the 2024 paper shows the braking distance curving upwards while the thinking distance stays a straight line — and why speeding is disproportionately dangerous.

Watch for: road conditions, brake condition and vehicle mass affect braking distance only. Tiredness, alcohol, drugs and distraction affect thinking distance only. Speed affects both.

U5

A metre rule is balanced on a pivot at its centre. A 2.0 N weight hangs 40 cm to the left of the pivot. Where must a 4.0 N weight be hung to balance it?

Then: explain why the answer doesn't depend on the weight of the rule itself.

Think about Where does the rule's own weight act?

Show answer

Anticlockwise moment = 2.0 × 40 = 80 N cm. For balance, the clockwise moment must also be 80 N cm. So 4.0 × d = 80, giving d = 20 cm to the right of the pivot.

The rule's own weight acts through its centre of gravity, which for a uniform rule is at its midpoint — which is exactly where the pivot is. Its perpendicular distance from the pivot is therefore zero, so its moment is zero. It has no turning effect.

The follow-up worth knowing: if the pivot were not at the centre, the rule's weight would produce a moment and you'd have to include it. Exam questions usually say "uniform" and pivot at the centre to avoid this — but if they don't, that's the trap.

U6

A student stretches a spring past its elastic limit and then removes the force.

(a) What does the force–extension graph look like?
(b) What happens to the spring?
(c) Is this the same as the limit of proportionality?

Show answer

(a) The graph starts as a straight line through the origin. Beyond the limit of proportionality it curves, with extension increasing more rapidly for each additional newton.

(b) The spring is permanently deformed — it does not return to its original length when unloaded. It has undergone plastic rather than elastic deformation.

(c) Not quite. The limit of proportionality is where the graph stops being a straight line — Hooke's law no longer holds. The elastic limit is where permanent deformation begins. They're close together and IGCSE often treats them loosely, but if a question asks about the graph shape the answer is proportionality; if it asks about permanent damage the answer is elastic limit.

U7

Mock 1 asks about a skydiver twice: part (c) says "explain, in terms of forces, how she reaches terminal velocity" and part (d) asks what happens to her gravitational potential energy. Why can a correct answer to one score zero on the other?

Show answer

Because the two parts are testing different models of the same event, and each mark scheme ignores the other model entirely.

"In terms of forces" wants weight, air resistance, resultant force and acceleration. Writing "she loses GPE which becomes KE" is true, but it answers a question that wasn't asked, so it scores nothing.

"What happens to her GPE" wants energy stores and transfers. Writing "air resistance balances her weight" is again true and again scores nothing.

The 2025 examiner made this exact complaint about the whole year group: answers "focussed either partly or exclusively on energy which was ignored… Unbalanced forces cause accelerations, NOT GPE/KE."

What to do: before writing, find the phrase "in terms of…" in the question and underline it. It is not decoration — it defines which vocabulary earns marks.

U8

An astronaut travels to the Moon, where the gravitational field strength is about 1.6 N/kg. What happens to her mass, and what happens to her weight? Why does the distinction matter in a calculation?

Show answer

Her mass is unchanged. Mass is the quantity of matter in her body, measured in kilograms, and travelling somewhere does not add or remove any of it.

Her weight falls to roughly a sixth. Weight is the force of gravity acting on that mass, measured in newtons, and it depends on the field she is in: W = m × g, with g dropping from 10 N/kg to 1.6 N/kg.

Why it matters in a calculation: F = m × a needs the mass in kilograms, never the weight. If a question gives you a weight in newtons and asks for an acceleration, you must divide by mass — so you may have to get the mass back out of the weight first. Substituting 750 instead of 75 is a standard way to lose every mark in a part.

Everyday speech makes this worse: bathroom scales report "weight" in kilograms, which is a mass. In physics the two words are not interchangeable.

U9

A car goes round a roundabout at a steady 20 m/s. Its speed never changes. Is it accelerating?

Show answer

Yes. This is the clearest demonstration of why speed and velocity are different words.

Speed is a scalar — size only. It is 20 m/s throughout, so the speed is constant.

Velocity is a vector — size and direction. Going round the roundabout the direction changes continuously, so the velocity changes continuously.

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, not of speed. A changing direction is a changing velocity, so the car is accelerating even though the speedometer reads the same number.

The consequence worth remembering: acceleration requires a resultant force, so there must be one — provided by friction between the tyres and the road, pointing towards the centre of the roundabout. On ice that force disappears and the car carries straight on.

U10

A student cannot loosen a nut with a spanner. Explain, using the moment equation, two different ways of getting more turning effect — and why one of them is usually easier.

Show answer

Moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot, so there are exactly two things you can change.

1. Increase the force — push harder. Often not possible; if they could push harder they already would have.

2. Increase the perpendicular distance — use a longer spanner, or slide a pipe over the handle. This is usually far easier, because doubling the length doubles the moment for exactly the same effort.

This is the standard one-mark answer: "use a longer spanner, increasing the perpendicular distance from the pivot". Naming the quantity you are increasing is what earns it — "use a bigger spanner" alone is vague.

The word doing the work is perpendicular. Pulling the spanner along its own length, straight towards the nut, has a perpendicular distance of zero and produces no turning effect at all, however hard you pull.

U11

A cyclist accelerates from rest to 12 m/s over 10 s, holds 12 m/s for 15 s, then stops in 5 s. A student calculates the average speed as (0 + 12 + 12 + 0) ÷ 4 = 6 m/s. What is wrong, and what is the right answer?

Show answer

The student has averaged the velocity readings. Average speed is not the average of the speeds — it is total distance ÷ total time, and the readings are not held for equal lengths of time.

Do it properly, from the area under the velocity–time graph:

Speeding up (triangle): ½ × 10 × 12 = 60 m  ·  steady (rectangle): 15 × 12 = 180 m  ·  stopping (triangle): ½ × 5 × 12 = 30 m

Total distance = 270 m over a total time of 30 s, so average speed = 270 ÷ 30 = 9.0 m/s.

It is higher than 6 m/s because the cyclist spent most of the journey at the top speed, and the crude average gave the brief slow parts equal weight.

U12

On a distance–time graph and on a velocity–time graph, a horizontal line means two completely different things. What are they, and how do you avoid confusing them under pressure?

Show answer

Distance–time, horizontal: the distance is not changing, so the object is stationary.

Velocity–time, horizontal: the velocity is not changing, so the object is moving at a constant velocity — which is the opposite of stopped, unless the line happens to sit on zero.

How to avoid the mix-up: read the vertical axis label before reading the shape. Every time. The graphs look identical and only the axis tells you which you have.

The same discipline sorts out the other two facts: on a distance–time graph the gradient is the speed, and on a velocity–time graph the gradient is the acceleration and the area is the distance. Area under a distance–time graph means nothing at all.

D3

(a) Calculate the deceleration. (3)
(b) Calculate the braking force. (2)

Show answer

(a) v² = u² + 2as, with v = 0 1

D4

A parachutist of mass 80 kg falls from a helicopter. Take g = 10 N/kg.

(a) Calculate her weight. (2)
(b) At one moment the air resistance acting on her is 500 N. Calculate her acceleration at that moment. (3)
(c) Explain, in terms of forces, what happens to her acceleration as she continues to fall. (3)

Show answer

(a) W = m × g = 80 × 10 1 = 800 N 1

D8

Show answer

This is the 5- or 6-mark question this topic builds towards. Structure it as up, top, down.

Going up: weight acts downwards and air resistance also acts downwards, because air resistance always opposes motion 1. The resultant force is therefore downwards and larger than the weight alone, so the ball decelerates 1.

At the top: the ball is momentarily at rest, so there is no air resistance. The only force is its weight, so the resultant force is downwards and it begins to accelerate downwards 1.

Coming down: weight acts downwards, air resistance now acts upwards, opposing the downward motion 1. The resultant force is downwards but smaller than the weight, so the ball accelerates downwards at less than 10 m/s² 1. As speed increases, air resistance increases, so the resultant force and therefore the acceleration decrease 1.

The mark that separates good answers: air resistance changes direction because it opposes motion, not because it opposes weight. That single sentence is worth learning.

The biggest block on the paper — about 48 of 255 marks — and it arrives in pieces rather than one question, which makes it easy to underestimate. More marks are lost here to unit conversion and to imprecise wording than to not understanding the physics. Every heading says where it was asked.

Particles in solids, liquids and gases

Spec 5.10P · 2023 Q5(a)(b) · 2025 Q3(b) · Mock 1 Q3(b) — asked in some form every year

Two things get asked separately and score separately: the arrangement and the motion. Answer both, in those terms.

ArrangementMotion
SolidClosely packed, regular patternVibrate about fixed positions
LiquidClosely packed, but random / irregularSlide or flow past one another
GasFar apart, randomMove quickly and randomly in all directions

Two specific phrases cost the year group marks.

Liquids are still closely packed. 2023's examiner noted students "regularly lost the third mark by stating the opposite". Going from solid to liquid changes the order, not really the spacing — that is why melting barely changes the volume, while boiling changes it enormously.

For liquid motion, "move freely" was ignored. The marks needed slide, flow, or move past each other. "The particles move" without "past each other" scores nothing.

Changes of state, and the flat bit on the graph

Spec 5.8P, 5.9P, 5.11P · Mock 2 Q9(c)

Heat a solid steadily and plot temperature against time. The graph rises, then goes flat while it melts, then rises again, then goes flat again while it boils.

Energy is being supplied throughout, so why does the temperature stop rising? Because the energy is doing a different job:

  • On the slopes: the energy increases the kinetic energy of the particles — they move faster — and temperature is a measure of that. So the temperature rises.
  • On the flat sections: the energy is used to break the bonds, or overcome the forces of attraction, between the particles. It is not increasing their kinetic energy, so the temperature stays constant while the state changes.

That is the three-mark answer, and all three marks are in that second bullet: energy breaks the bonds 1, rather than increasing kinetic energy 1, so the temperature stays constant during the change of state 1.

Density, and the displacement method

Spec 5.3, 5.4 · 2024 Q2(b)(c) · Mock 2 Q2(a)(b)

ρ = m ÷ V
ρ = density in kg/m³ (or g/cm³) · m = mass in kg (or g) · V = volume in m³ (or cm³)

Finding the volume of a regular shape is just multiplying its dimensions. The exam is far more interested in an irregular object, where you use displacement:

  1. Part-fill a measuring cylinder with water and record the volume.
  2. Lower the object in fully submerged, on a thread if needed so it doesn't splash.
  3. Record the new volume and subtract. The difference is the object's volume.

Mass comes from a balance, zeroed first. Accuracy marks come from details: fully submerged, no splashing, read at eye level to avoid parallax.

Watch the units the answer is demanded in. g/cm³ and kg/m³ are both legitimate but differ by a factor of 1000. If a question gives grams and cubic centimetres and asks for g/cm³, do not convert anything.

2024's final mark was awarded specifically for giving the answer to 2 significant figures. As the examiner put it: 2 s.f. is not 2 decimal places.

Pressure, and pressure in a liquid

Spec 5.5, 5.6, 5.7 · Mock 2 Q2(c)(d) · 2025 Q6(c)

P = F ÷ A
P = pressure in pascals (Pa) · F = force in newtons (N) · A = area in m²

One pascal is one newton per square metre. The same force spread over a smaller area gives a greater pressure — which is why a drawing pin has a sharp point and a snowshoe is wide.

Pressure at a point in a liquid or gas at rest acts equally in all directions (spec 5.6), not just downwards. That is why a submerged object is squeezed from every side, and why the collapsing-bottle question works.

ΔP = h × g × ρ
ΔP = pressure difference in pascals (Pa) · h = height (depth) in metres (m) · g = gravitational field strength in N/kg (use 10) · ρ = density in kg/m³

Pressure increases with depth simply because there is a greater height of liquid above pressing down. Note this equation needs kg/m³ and metres — it is the one place where converting from g/cm³ genuinely matters.

How a gas exerts pressure

Spec 5.15 · 2025 Q6(c) · Mock 1 Q6(b) — and the examiner refused a whole year group's wording

Learn this as four beats, in order. Each is a mark.

  1. The molecules (or particles) move quickly and randomly in all directions
  2. They collide with the walls of the container
  3. Each collision exerts a force on the wall
  4. Pressure = force ÷ area, so those collisions produce a pressure

The 2025 examiner: the collision mark was only given if molecules or particles were named — "they hit the walls" scored nothing. The word "they" is not good enough; name what is hitting.

And "the pressure = force/area point had to be linked into the answer, not just written down". Write the fourth beat as a sentence that connects the collisions to the pressure, not as a formula floating at the end.

Temperature, kelvin and absolute zero

Spec 5.16, 5.17, 5.18, 5.19 · 2025 Q7(a)(b)(i) · Mock 1 Q6(a)

K = °C + 273

Raising the temperature increases the average kinetic energy of the molecules, so they move faster. Cool a gas down and they move more slowly — and at −273 °C they would have essentially no kinetic energy left. You cannot remove energy that isn't there, so no lower temperature is possible. That is absolute zero, and it is where the Kelvin scale starts.

The reason the Kelvin scale matters: the kelvin temperature of a gas is proportional to the average kinetic energy of its molecules. Double the kelvin temperature and you double the average kinetic energy. Celsius has no such property — its zero is an arbitrary point about water, so doubling 10 °C to 20 °C does not double anything physical.

Say proportional to the kinetic energy. 2025's examiner made the point that temperature is not kinetic energy — it is a measure that is proportional to the average.

The two gas laws

Spec 5.20, 5.21, 5.22 · Mock 1 Q6(c)(d) · Mock 2 Q9(a)(b)

Both apply to a fixed mass of gas, and each holds one thing constant.

Held constantEquationParticle explanation for the change
Volume (a sealed rigid container)P₁ ÷ T₁ = P₂ ÷ T₂  (T in kelvin)Higher temperature → greater average kinetic energy → particles hit the walls more often and harder
Temperature (squeezing a syringe)P₁V₁ = P₂V₂The same number of particles in a smaller volume → they hit the walls more often, but not harder

The two explanations are different and the difference is examined. In the constant-temperature case the particles do not speed up — the temperature has not changed, so the average kinetic energy has not changed. Only the frequency of collisions increases, because the walls are closer. Mock 2's mark scheme calls saying they move faster "a common and costly error".

Kelvin is compulsory for the pressure–temperature law. Using Celsius gives a badly wrong answer, because the equation is a proportionality and only the Kelvin scale has a true zero. The pressure–volume law has no temperature in it, so no conversion arises.

Specific heat capacity

Spec 5.12P, 5.13P · 2023 Q5(c) · 2024 Q6(b) · 2025 Q7(b)(iii) · Mock 1 Q7 · Mock 2 Q7 — the most-asked calculation in the module

ΔQ = m × c × ΔT
ΔQ = change in thermal energy in joules (J) · m = mass in kilograms (kg) · c = specific heat capacity in J/kg °C · ΔT = change in temperature in °C

Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to raise the temperature of one kilogram of a substance by one degree Celsius. Water's is unusually large at 4200 J/kg °C, which is why the sea takes so long to warm up and why water is used in heating systems.

A large c means a substance needs a lot of energy per degree, so it heats up slowly. A small c heats up quickly — which is the answer to "the same energy is supplied to oil instead of water; what happens?" The oil, with the smaller c, reaches the higher temperature.

Three conversions account for most lost marks here, and each has been specifically flagged:

Grams to kilograms. 2023's examiner: "most marks lost were due to power-of-ten errors — students failing to convert from g to kg". 16 g is 0.016 kg.

Minutes to seconds, when energy comes from power × time. Mock 2: 5.0 minutes is 300 s — "using 5 gives an answer 60 times too small".

ΔT is a difference, not a temperature. From 16 °C to 65 °C is ΔT = 49, not 65. And because it is a difference, a change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K — so you never convert temperature here, unlike in the gas laws.

2025 also asked it rearranged for mass. Practise making every symbol the subject: m = ΔQ ÷ (c × ΔT) and c = ΔQ ÷ (m × ΔT).

Measuring specific heat capacity

Spec 5.14P · 2024 Q6(a) — 5 marks · Mock 2 Q7(a) — 5 marks

A five-mark "describe the investigation" question in two separate papers, so it is worth having a rehearsed answer. For a metal block with an immersion heater:

  1. Measure the mass of the block on a balance
  2. Insert the heater and thermometer into the holes in the block, and record the starting temperature
  3. Switch on the heater and start the stopwatch at the same moment
  4. After a measured time, switch off and record the highest temperature reached
  5. Calculate the energy supplied from E = P × t, then use c = E ÷ (m × ΔT)
  6. Insulate the block with lagging to reduce energy transfer to the surroundings

Point 4 has a reason worth knowing: the temperature keeps rising for a while after switching off, because energy is still spreading through the block. Taking the reading too early makes ΔT too small.

Why the answer usually comes out too high: some energy is transferred to the surroundings instead of into the block. The calculation assumes all of the electrical energy went into the block, so it credits the block with more energy than it actually received — and c = E ÷ (mΔT) with an overstated E gives an overstated c. That is a two-mark explanation and it comes up.

Thermal energy transfer and cooling curves

Spec 5.8P · 2023 Q8 — 11 marks

Put hot water and cold water in contact and thermal energy is transferred from the hotter to the colder, until both reach the same temperature — thermal equilibrium. On a temperature–time graph the hot line falls, the cold line rises, both curve, and they level off towards a common temperature.

The curves flatten because the rate of transfer depends on the temperature difference: as the two get closer, the transfer slows.

2023 wanted the final temperature closer to the cold water's starting temperature, because there was much more cold water. The amount of each matters, not just the temperatures.

And "'heat rises' scores nothing — name the transfer mechanism." Say conduction (through the metal), convection, radiation, or evaporation. Better insulation reduces the transfer to the surroundings, so the equilibrium temperature is higher and the cooling takes longer.

Particles — the exact wording

Also from 2023: correct candidates on the solid-versus-liquid comparison "usually referred to liquids being incompressible" — a detail worth having.

How a gas exerts pressure — learn this as four beats

  1. Gas molecules move rapidly and randomly in all directions.
  2. They collide with the walls of the container.
  3. Each collision changes the molecule's momentum, so the wall exerts a force on the molecule and the molecule exerts a force on the wall.
  4. Pressure is force per unit area, so the total force from many collisions over the wall's area gives the pressure.

Say "molecules" or "particles", never "they". Reference P = F ÷ A and use it in the sentence rather than writing it in the margin — the examiner refused the mark for the equation floating unattached.

One more line the specification asks for: because the molecules move randomly in every direction, the pressure at a point in a gas or a liquid at rest acts equally in all directions — not just downwards. That is why a bottle is crushed inwards from every side, and why depth alone fixes the pressure in a liquid.

Specific heat capacity

Definition: the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C.

Water's is 4200 J/kg °C — unusually high, which is why water heats slowly, cools slowly, and is used in central heating and cooling systems. A substance with a low SHC changes temperature by a lot for the same energy input. That is the whole answer to the 2024 "why does the air heat more than the water" question.

Two practicals you must be able to describe

Density of an irregular solid

  • Part-fill a measuring cylinder with water and record the volume

  • ρ = m ÷ V

  • Measure the mass of water using a balance

04Understanding it

U1

A sealed rigid steel can of gas is heated. The volume cannot change. Explain, at particle level, exactly why the pressure rises — and identify two separate reasons.

Show answer

Heating increases the average kinetic energy of the molecules, so they move faster. That produces two distinct effects, and good answers name both:

One: faster molecules cross the container more quickly, so they hit the walls more often — more collisions per second.

Two: each molecule now arrives with more momentum, so each collision exerts a larger force.

More frequent and harder collisions mean a greater total force on the same area, so by P = F ÷ A the pressure rises.

Most students give only the first reason. The second is where the extra mark lives.

U2

Ice, water and steam are all H₂O. When ice melts at 0 °C, energy is supplied but the temperature doesn't change. Where is the energy going?

Show answer

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles. During melting, the energy supplied is not increasing that — it is going into breaking the bonds between particles, overcoming the forces of attraction that hold them in a fixed lattice.

So the energy is being transferred to the potential energy of the particles, not their kinetic energy. The particles gain freedom to move past each other, but not extra speed.

On a temperature–time graph this shows as a horizontal plateau. There is one at the melting point and a longer one at the boiling point — longer because boiling requires breaking all the remaining bonds rather than just loosening them.

U3

Two identical beakers each receive 10,000 J of energy. One contains 1 kg of water (c = 4200), the other 1 kg of copper (c = 385).

Which gets hotter, and by how much? Then explain why saucepans are made of metal but filled with water.

Show answer

Rearranging: ΔT = ΔQ ÷ (m × c).

Water: 10 000 ÷ (1 × 4200) = 2.4 °C

Copper: 10 000 ÷ (1 × 385) = 26 °C

The copper gets hotter by roughly eleven times as much, because its specific heat capacity is about eleven times smaller.

The saucepan: the metal has a low SHC so it heats up rapidly for little energy, transferring heat to the food quickly. The water has a high SHC so it holds a lot of energy and its temperature stays stable, cooking food evenly rather than scorching it.

This is the same reasoning as the 2024 question about air heating more than water. Low SHC means big temperature change.

U4

A sealed plastic bottle is closed at high altitude where the air pressure is low. On landing, it is crushed. Why? And why would a rigid glass bottle not be?

Show answer

When sealed, the pressure inside equals the outside pressure at that altitude — low. Descending, the outside pressure rises, but the trapped air inside stays at the low pressure.

There is now a pressure difference, with greater pressure outside than inside. Pressure acting on the bottle's surface area produces a force pushing inwards, larger than the outward force from the air inside. The resultant inward force crushes the bottle.

A rigid glass bottle doesn't collapse because glass is strong enough to withstand that resultant force without deforming — but the pressure difference is still there.

The 2025 examiner: "If missed MP2 its because they didn't specify that it was force causing it to collapse — instead kept saying pressure difference. Some didn't specify where the greater pressure was."

So: name the pressure difference, say which side is greater, and say that it produces a force.

U5

Why do you need to convert to kelvin for the gas laws, but not for the specific heat capacity equation?

Show answer

The gas laws involve ratios of temperature. Doubling the kelvin temperature genuinely doubles the average kinetic energy — because the kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, where kinetic energy is zero. Going from 10 °C to 20 °C is not doubling anything physically; it's 283 K to 293 K, a rise of about 3.5%.

The SHC equation uses ΔT, a change in temperature. A rise of 20 °C is a rise of 20 K — the two scales have identical interval sizes, so the difference is the same number either way.

Rule of thumb: absolute temperature needs kelvin, temperature change doesn't care. The 2023 examiner noted students converting ΔT to kelvin, which was harmless but unnecessary.

U6

A liquid flows and takes the shape of its container, while a solid holds its shape. Yet the mark scheme insists that a liquid's particles are still closely packed. How can both be true?

Show answer

Because flowing is about order, not about spacing.

In a solid the particles are closely packed and in a regular, fixed pattern, so each one vibrates about a fixed position and the shape is held.

In a liquid the particles are still touching — essentially as close together as before — but the regular pattern has broken down. They are randomly arranged, so they can slide past one another. Close together, but not locked in place.

The evidence is in the volumes. Melting ice changes its volume by only a few per cent, because the spacing barely changes. Boiling water expands it about 1600 times, because that is when the particles genuinely move far apart. If liquids were not closely packed, melting would cause a huge expansion too.

2023's examiner noted students "regularly lost the third mark by stating the opposite". Say closely packed and randomly arranged.

U7

A syringe of gas is compressed to half its volume at constant temperature, and the pressure doubles. A student explains: "the particles are squashed together so they move faster and hit the walls harder." Two parts of that are wrong. Which, and what is the correct explanation?

Show answer

Wrong 1 — they do not move faster. The temperature is constant, and temperature is a measure of average kinetic energy. If the particles sped up, the temperature would have risen. It hasn't, so they haven't.

Wrong 2 — they do not hit harder. The force of each individual collision depends on how fast the particle is moving, which is unchanged.

What actually changes is how often they hit. The same number of particles now occupies half the space, so the walls are closer and each particle reaches a wall more frequently. More collisions per second on each square metre means a greater total force per unit area — which is a greater pressure.

Contrast with heating at constant volume: there the particles genuinely do speed up, so they hit both more often and harder. Two different laws, two different explanations, and the exam tests whether you know which is which.

Mock 2 calls "they move faster" here "a common and costly error".

U8

The pressure at the bottom of a swimming pool depends on the depth, but not on how wide the pool is. Why not — surely a wider pool holds far more water pressing down?

Show answer

It does hold more water — but that extra weight is spread over a proportionally larger area of floor, so the force per unit area is unchanged.

Pressure is force ÷ area. Doubling the width of the pool doubles the weight of water and doubles the floor area at the same time, and the two cancel exactly.

You can see it in the equation: ΔP = h × g × ρ contains the depth, the gravitational field strength and the density — and no term for width, area or total volume at all. The only things that matter are how deep you are and what the liquid is made of.

The practical consequence: a dam's thickness is determined by the depth of the reservoir, not by how many kilometres of lake stretch out behind it.

U9

A student measuring the specific heat capacity of a metal block gets a value higher than the accepted one. Their arithmetic is correct. Explain why the method pushes the answer the wrong way — and why it is always too high rather than sometimes too low.

Show answer

The calculation assumes all the electrical energy supplied went into the block. In reality some is transferred to the surroundings — through the air, the bench and the wires.

So the energy actually used to raise the block's temperature was less than the figure used in the sum. Since c = E ÷ (m × ΔT), an overstated E gives an overstated c.

Why always too high: because the error is one-directional. Energy always leaks out of a block that is hotter than the room — it never leaks in. So the measured temperature rise is always smaller than the supplied energy would suggest, and the calculated c is always too large.

How to reduce it: insulate the block with lagging, and keep the temperature rise modest so there is less time and less temperature difference for losses to occur.

This "suggest why" question is worth two marks and appears in both mocks. Naming the direction of the error and linking it to the equation is what earns them.

U10

You must convert to kelvin for the pressure–temperature gas law, but you must not convert for the specific heat capacity equation. Why the difference?

Show answer

Because one uses a temperature and the other uses a temperature difference.

The gas law is a proportionality. P₁ ÷ T₁ = P₂ ÷ T₂ says pressure is proportional to temperature, and a proportionality only makes sense measured from a true zero. Celsius zero is arbitrary — it is about water freezing, not about particles stopping — so "twice 10 °C" means nothing physical. Kelvin zero is the point where molecular kinetic energy is essentially zero, so doubling the kelvin temperature really does double the average kinetic energy.

The SHC equation uses ΔT, a change. A rise of 20 degrees is the same size on both scales, because the two scales have identical-sized divisions and only differ in where they start. 20 °C of change is 20 K of change, so converting achieves nothing — and converting the two temperatures separately before subtracting also works, since the 273s cancel.

The rule: a single temperature in a proportionality needs kelvin. A temperature difference never does.

U11

Water has a specific heat capacity of 4200 J/kg °C; copper's is 385 J/kg °C. Explain what this means physically, and give two everyday consequences.

Show answer

It means one kilogram of water needs 4200 joules to warm by one degree, while one kilogram of copper needs only 385 — about eleven times less. Water is unusually resistant to changing its temperature.

Consequence 1 — coastal climates are milder. The sea absorbs enormous amounts of energy in summer while warming only slightly, then releases it slowly in winter. Places near large bodies of water have smaller temperature swings than places inland.

Consequence 2 — water is used as a coolant and in heating systems. Central heating pipes and car radiators carry water because it can pick up a great deal of energy from the boiler or engine without its own temperature shooting up, then release it elsewhere.

A third, if you want it: a copper saucepan heats up almost instantly on the hob while the water in it takes minutes — same energy going in, wildly different temperature rises.

The exam version of this is "the same energy is supplied to oil, which has a lower specific heat capacity — what happens?" The oil gets hotter, because less energy is needed per kilogram per degree.

02How it actually works

The cheat sheet in the next section is what you reread the night before. This is where the rules come from, so that when you can't remember one you can rebuild it. Every heading below says where it was actually asked — nothing is here because it is interesting.

Drawing the investigation circuit

2023 Q4(a) · 2025 Q5(a) · Mock 1 Q5(a) — 4 marks on every paper

This is the most predictable four marks in the whole exam. The question changes component — filament lamp in 2023, diode in 2025, thermistor in the mock — but the circuit is always the same:

  • Cell, variable resistor, ammeter and the component all in series, in one closed loop
  • Voltmeter in parallel across the component only — not across anything else
  • Correct symbols throughout
  • Every wire joined — no gaps, no loose ends

The variable resistor is what makes the experiment work: turning it changes the voltage across the component so you can take a range of readings instead of one. Leave it out and there is no method, and you lose the mark.

The meters go where they do because of what they measure. An ammeter counts the charge flowing through something, so it has to be in the path — series. A voltmeter measures a difference between two points, so it has to touch both of them — parallel.

2025: "students dropped marks for not connecting wires, leaving gaps in circuit. Incorrect circuit symbol for variable resistor, often drawing thermistor."

Two checks before you move on. Trace the loop with your finger from one terminal of the cell back to the other — if your finger comes off the line, that's a mark. And check the variable resistor has a diagonal arrow through the rectangle; a line that hooks upward at the end is a thermistor.

Charge, current and the junction rule

Spec 2.14, 2.15, 2.17 · 2025 Q5(b)(i) · Q = It also appears in the static electricity questions

Current is the rate of flow of charge. Learn that wording — it is a one-mark definition and it comes up. "Rate of" means "per second", so:

Q = I × t
Q = charge in coulombs (C) · I = current in amperes (A) · t = time in seconds (s)

So one amp is one coulomb per second. A current of 3 A means three coulombs pass any point every second.

Charge cannot be created, destroyed or leak out of the wire, which gives you two rules for free:

  • In a single loop the current is the same everywhere. Whatever goes into a component comes out of it.
  • At a junction, current in = current out. If 0.90 A arrives and 0.35 A goes down one branch, the other branch carries 0.55 A.

Students write that a bulb "uses up the current". It doesn't — it uses up energy. Every electron that goes in comes out again. If you ever write that the current is smaller after a component, stop.

Also: current in a solid metal is a flow of negatively charged electrons. That exact phrase is worth a mark on its own.

Voltage, and why the voltages add up

Spec 2.20, 2.21 · 2024 Q4(b)(iii) — "voltages of two components in series add to the supply voltage"

Voltage is the energy transferred per unit charge passed. That is the spec wording, and it means one volt is one joule per coulomb.

E = Q × V
E = energy transferred in joules (J) · Q = charge in coulombs (C) · V = voltage in volts (V)

A 6 V battery hands 6 joules to every coulomb it pushes round. A component with 2 V across it takes 2 joules back out of every coulomb that passes.

Which immediately explains the rule the 2024 paper tested. Follow one coulomb round a 9 V circuit with two components: the battery gives it 9 J, the first component takes 5 J (so 5 V across it), the second takes 4 J (so 4 V), and it arrives back at the battery with nothing left — because it is back where it started. 9 = 5 + 4. The voltages across components in series add up to the supply voltage, because energy is conserved.

The move this gives you

In 2024 the paper gave the supply as 1.5 V, had you calculate 0.57 V across one component, then asked for the voltage across the other. You do not calculate it — you subtract: 1.5 − 0.57 = 0.93 V. One mark for knowing the voltages add, one for the arithmetic.

Whenever you know the supply and all but one of the series voltages, the last one is free.

Current flows through a component. Voltage is always across it — never through it. A voltage is a difference between two points, which is why it is properly called potential difference. "The voltage through the resistor" can cost you the mark in an explain question.

V = IR — the workhorse

Spec 2.13 · used in every single circuits question across all three past papers and both mocks

V = I × R
V = voltage in volts (V) · I = current in amperes (A) · R = resistance in ohms (Ω)

Resistance is the opposition to the flow of current, measured in ohms. Physically it comes from the moving electrons colliding with the vibrating metal ions — which is also why a component carrying a current gets hot.

You will be asked to rearrange it in both directions, so be fluent: I = V ÷ R and R = V ÷ I.

Papers routinely award a mark simply for stating the formula before you use it — 2024 Q4(b)(i), 2025 Q5(b)(ii), mock-1 Q5(c)(i) and mock-2 Q3(b)(i) all did. Write the equation down every time, even when you can do the sum in your head. It is the easiest mark on the paper and it is thrown away constantly.

Series: resistances add, voltage shares out

Spec 2.19 · 2025 Q5(b)(iv) · Mock 2 Q3(b)

In series the current is the same through both components and the voltages add. That gives you the rule in one line: V = IR₁ + IR₂ = I(R₁ + R₂), so Rtotal = R₁ + R₂. Resistances in series simply add.

It also gives you something more useful. Since the same current passes through both, and V = IR, each component's share of the voltage matches its share of the resistance. Twice the resistance, twice the voltage.

Worked example — the standard series calculation

A 12 Ω and an 18 Ω resistor are in series with a 9.0 V battery.

1. Total resistance: 12 + 18 = 30 Ω

2. Current (supply voltage with total resistance): I = 9.0 ÷ 30 = 0.30 A, the same through both.

3. Each voltage (that current with each resistance): 0.30 × 12 = 3.6 V and 0.30 × 18 = 5.4 V

4. Check: 3.6 + 5.4 = 9.0 V ✓ — and the bigger resistor took the bigger share.

That check takes five seconds and catches nearly every slip. Do it every time.

Mock 2 ran this backwards: given the current and the fixed resistor, find its voltage, then get the thermistor's resistance from what's left. Same three moves, different starting point.

Parallel: same voltage, currents add

Spec 2.18, 2.17 · Mock 1 Q5(c)

In parallel the current reaches a junction and has a choice of routes. Two rules:

  • Every branch has the full supply voltage across it — each branch is its own complete path from one terminal to the other.
  • The branch currents add up to the current from the battery.

Which means adding a parallel branch makes the total resistance go down. You are not adding an obstacle to an existing path, you are opening a new one — like opening a second till in a supermarket. More gets through per second, which is exactly what lower resistance means.

The method that cannot go wrong

You are not required to know the reciprocal formula, and reaching for it is where marks are lost. One branch at a time:

  1. Every branch has the full supply voltage.
  2. Each branch's current on its own: I = V ÷ R.
  3. Add the branch currents for the battery current.
  4. Only if asked for total resistance: R = V ÷ Itotal, at the very end.

15 Ω and 10 Ω across 3.0 V: I₁ = 3.0 ÷ 15 = 0.20 A, I₂ = 3.0 ÷ 10 = 0.30 A, total = 0.50 A.

Sanity check: a parallel total is always smaller than the smallest branch. Here R = 3.0 ÷ 0.50 = 6.0 Ω, less than 10 Ω ✓.

In 2025 candidates gave 13 Ω as a parallel total — they had added the branches as if in series. The method above makes that impossible, because you never add resistances at all.

Domestic lighting is wired in parallel for three reasons worth knowing (spec 2.7): each lamp gets the full mains voltage so runs at full brightness; each can be switched independently; and one failing leaves the others' circuits complete.

Comparing two circuits — the question that failed the year group

2025 Q5(b)(iv) — "poorly answered throughout the year group"

This is the hardest routine question in the topic and it is worth learning as a procedure, because the marks are awarded for structure as much as for physics.

In 2025: circuit 1 had a 19 Ω component; circuit 2 had 19 Ω and 13 Ω in series. Which carries more current?

  1. Work out the total resistance of each. 19 Ω, and 19 + 13 = 32 Ω.
  2. Say which is larger. Circuit 2 has the greater resistance.
  3. Work out both currents. 2.9 ÷ 19 ≈ 0.15 A and 2.9 ÷ 32 ≈ 0.09 A.
  4. State the conclusion in words. The current in circuit 2 is smaller, because its total resistance is larger while the supply voltage is unchanged.

The examiner: "A combination of correct calculations along with working or explanation required for 4 marks. Calculation alone without comparison of values was insufficient for full marks."

The words compare, comment on and explain the difference all require a sentence. Numbers on their own cap you well below full marks, however right they are. Write the "so it is bigger/smaller, because…" line every time.

LDR and thermistor — the consequence chain

Spec 2.11 · 2024 Q4(c) · Mock 2 Q3(c)

Two components, and the same direction for both — which is the opposite of what people guess:

ComponentBehaviour
LDRMore light → lower resistance (and the relationship is a curve, not a straight line)
ThermistorHigher temperature → lower resistance

Put either in series with a fixed resistor and the exam asks what happens to a voltage or a current. It catches people because two things change at once in opposite directions, so applying V = IR to the sensor itself proves nothing — its R falls while I rises.

The chain that always works

Argue through the component whose resistance has not changed. Taking 2024's LDR as light increases:

  1. Light up → LDR resistance down
  2. Total resistance down → current up
  3. Fixed resistor: same R, bigger I → its voltage rises
  4. The two voltages still add to the fixed supply → the LDR's voltage falls

Mock 2 asked the thermistor version — warmed, so resistance falls, so total resistance falls, so the current increases. Identical chain.

Learn the four steps as a unit. Each one is typically a mark, and writing only the conclusion scores one of four.

I–V graphs: the filament lamp and the diode

The filament lamp explanation is worth three marks and the chain must be complete: current → temperature → ion vibration → collisions → resistance increases. Skipping a link loses a mark.

Getting resistance off the graph. Take the coordinates of the point and do R = V ÷ I. Do not take the gradient of a curve — gradient equals 1 ÷ R only for a straight line through the origin.

For the "how would you improve this" mark, 2023's mark scheme wanted "take more data at different voltages". Repeating readings and averaging also scores.

Power, energy and fuses

Spec 2.3, 2.4, 2.5 · Mock 1 Q7(b) uses E = IVt

P = I × V  and  E = I × V × t
P = power in watts (W) · E = energy transferred in joules (J) · I = current (A) · V = voltage (V) · t = time in seconds (s)

P = IV follows from the definitions: voltage is joules per coulomb, current is coulombs per second, so multiplying gives joules per second. Note E = IVt is the same thing as E = QV, since Q = It — use whichever the question gives you.

Fuses use the heating effect as protection: a thin wire that melts and breaks the circuit if the current gets too high. To choose one, find the normal current with I = P ÷ V, then take the next standard rating above it — above, so it doesn't blow in normal use; only just above, so it still acts on a fault.

Time must be in seconds and currents in amps. Questions give minutes and milliamps precisely because the conversion gets forgotten: 4 minutes is 240 s, 250 mA is 0.25 A. Two conversions in one question is normal.

The 2025 examiner on the static questions: "Very few used standard form — please use it!" Large or small answers are safer written as 2.76 × 10⁶ than as a row of zeros.

04Understanding it

U1

Two bulbs are in series. Does the first one "use up" the current, leaving less for the second?

Show answer

No. Current is the same at every point in a series circuit. Charge is conserved — electrons are not consumed, they circulate.

What does get used up is energy. Each bulb transfers energy from the charge passing through it, which is why the voltage drops across each one and the drops add up to the supply voltage.

The useful mental model: current is like the rate of water flow round a closed loop — the same everywhere. Voltage is like the pressure drop across each obstacle. Water isn't destroyed by a waterwheel; its energy is extracted.

U2

A 20 Ω and a 30 Ω resistor are connected in parallel. Without calculating, is the total resistance more or less than 20 Ω? Explain why.

Show answer

Less than 20 Ω — and this surprises people, because "adding a resistor" sounds like adding resistance.

Adding a parallel branch gives the current an extra route. More total current flows from the battery for the same voltage. Since R = V ÷ I, a larger current at the same voltage means a smaller total resistance.

Checking: 6 V across each gives 0.3 A and 0.2 A, total 0.5 A, so R = 6 ÷ 0.5 = 12 Ω. Less than either branch, as it must be.

Sanity check to use in the exam: a parallel combination is always less resistive than its smallest branch. If your answer isn't, you've made an error.

U3

Why does the I–V graph for a filament lamp curve, while the one for a fixed resistor stays straight? Both are made of metal.

Show answer

Both obey the same physics — the difference is temperature.

A filament lamp is designed to get white hot. As the current rises, the filament's temperature rises sharply. The metal ions in the lattice vibrate with greater amplitude, so electrons collide with them more frequently, so resistance increases. Increasing resistance means the current rises less and less for each extra volt — hence the curve flattening.

A fixed resistor is designed to dissipate its heat and stay near room temperature, so its resistance stays effectively constant and the line stays straight.

This is why the 2023 question asked whether a straight line was appropriate for a metal. The answer: current is only directly proportional to voltage at constant temperature, and a filament lamp is precisely the case where that condition fails.

U4

An LDR is in series with a fixed resistor across a cell. The lights come on and the LDR's resistance falls. Explain what happens to the voltage across the LDR — and why it isn't obvious.

Show answer

It's not obvious because two things change at once, in opposite directions.

Total resistance falls, so the current in the circuit increases. For the LDR, V = IR with I up but R down — so which wins?

Work it from the other component. The fixed resistor's resistance is unchanged, and the current through it has increased, so its voltage rises. The two voltages must still add to the supply voltage, which is fixed. So the voltage across the LDR must fall.

The general principle: in a series pair, the component with the larger share of the resistance takes the larger share of the voltage. As the LDR's share of total resistance drops, so does its share of the voltage.

U5

In 2024 the paper gave a supply of 1.5 V, had candidates calculate 0.57 V across one component, then asked for the voltage across the other component in series with it. Many tried to calculate it from scratch. What should they have done, and why does it work?

Show answer

Subtract: 1.5 − 0.57 = 0.93 V. No calculation from resistances is needed at all.

It works because the voltages across components in series must add up to the supply voltage. Each coulomb of charge is given 1.5 J by the supply and must spend all of it before returning — so if one component takes 0.57 J per coulomb, the other must take the remaining 0.93 J.

The mark scheme gave one mark for stating that the series voltages add to the supply, and one for the arithmetic.

Generalise it: whenever you know the supply voltage and all but one of the series voltages, the last one is free. Look for that before starting a longer route — it is quicker and it is what is being tested.

U6

A 2 Ω and a 4 Ω resistor are in series. Without doing a full calculation, which one has the larger voltage across it, and by what factor?

Show answer

The 4 Ω resistor, and it has twice the voltage of the 2 Ω one.

The reasoning takes one line. In series the same current flows through both. Since V = IR and I is common to both, the voltage across each is directly proportional to its resistance. Twice the resistance, twice the voltage.

So the supply voltage splits in the ratio 2 : 4, or 1 : 3 and 2 : 3 of the total. On a 6 V supply that is 2 V and 4 V.

Worth internalising: "share of the resistance = share of the voltage" turns most series questions into mental arithmetic, and it is the whole basis of the LDR and thermistor questions.

U7

An ammeter goes in series and a voltmeter goes in parallel. Rather than memorising that, what is it about what each one measures that forces the placement?

Show answer

Ammeter — measures current, which is charge flowing through something. To count what passes through a component, the meter has to be in the same path, so that all of that current goes through the meter too. That means in series with it.

Voltmeter — measures voltage, which is a difference between two points. A difference cannot be measured at one place; the meter has to be connected to both points at once. That means in parallel, across the component.

This is also why the language differs: current through, voltage across. The preposition tells you where the meter goes.

Mock 2 asked exactly this: add an instrument to measure the current, and state where it must be placed. One mark for "ammeter", one for "in series".

U8

A student measures a filament lamp and finds V = 3.0 V when I = 0.50 A. They then read the gradient of their curved I–V graph at that point and get a different value for the resistance. Which is right, and why do they disagree?

Show answer

The R = V ÷ I calculation is right: 3.0 ÷ 0.50 = 6.0 Ω. The gradient is not the resistance here.

R = V ÷ I is the definition of resistance, so it works at any point on any graph for any component. It uses the coordinates of the point — how far across, how far up.

The gradient tells you something different: how much the current changes for a small change in voltage. Gradient equals 1 ÷ R only when the graph is a straight line through the origin, because only then is the ratio V : I the same as the rate of change.

A filament lamp's graph is curved precisely because its resistance is not constant, so the two numbers must disagree — and only the point calculation means anything.

The exam-safe rule: to get resistance from a graph, read off a point and divide. Never measure a gradient on a curve.

U9

Domestic lighting is wired in parallel rather than in series. Give three separate reasons why.

Show answer

1. Every lamp gets the full mains voltage. In parallel the voltage across each branch equals the supply, so every lamp runs at its designed brightness. In series they would share the voltage out, so the more lamps you added, the dimmer they would all become.

2. Each can be switched independently. Each branch can have its own switch that breaks only that branch. In series, one switch would control everything.

3. One lamp failing does not affect the others. A blown filament breaks its own branch only; the other branches are still complete loops. In series, one failure opens the single loop and the whole house goes dark — which is exactly how old Christmas tree lights behaved.

The question is usually worth three marks and expects three distinct points, so don't write the same idea three ways.

U10

"A battery supplies a constant current." Is that statement right? Explain.

Show answer

No. A battery supplies a roughly constant voltage — the current depends on what you connect to it.

A 9 V battery gives 9 joules to every coulomb it pushes round, whatever the circuit. But how many coulombs per second flow is set by the resistance: I = V ÷ R. Connect a 9 Ω resistor and you get 1 A; connect a 90 Ω resistor and you get 0.1 A. Same battery, ten times less current.

This is why adding components changes the current but not the supply voltage, and it is the assumption behind every calculation in this module: the supply voltage is the fixed thing, the current is the consequence.

U11

A thermistor is in series with a fixed resistor. Someone claims: "the thermistor gets hot, so its resistance goes up, so it takes more of the voltage." Two things in that sentence are wrong. Find them.

Show answer

Error 1 — the direction is backwards. A thermistor's resistance falls as temperature rises. This is the opposite of an ordinary metal resistor, and it is the single most common slip in the topic. Same for an LDR: more light means less resistance.

Error 2 — the conclusion doesn't follow anyway. Even if the resistance had risen, saying "so it takes more voltage" skips the reasoning. You have to argue through the other component: its resistance is unchanged, the current has changed, so V = IR gives its new voltage, and the two must still add to the supply.

Corrected: the thermistor heats up → its resistance falls → total resistance falls → current rises → the fixed resistor's voltage rises (unchanged R, larger I) → so the thermistor's voltage falls.

U12

Two identical bulbs are connected in parallel across a battery. A third identical bulb is now added in parallel with them. What happens to the current drawn from the battery, and to the brightness of the original two?

Show answer

Current from the battery increases — by half as much again, since there are now three branches instead of two.

The original two are unchanged in brightness (assuming an ideal battery). Each branch still has the full supply voltage across it and the same resistance, so each still carries the same current and transfers energy at the same rate.

That is the point of parallel wiring, and it is why it is used for domestic lighting: adding a lamp does not dim the existing ones. Adding a branch does not take anything away from the branches already there — it just asks more of the battery.

Contrast with series: adding a third bulb in series would increase the total resistance, reduce the current, and make all three dimmer than the original two.

In reality a battery has some internal resistance, so a very large number of branches would eventually dim everything. That is not on this specification — the ideal answer above is the one being marked.

02How it actually works

A lot of this topic is straight recall — the spectrum in order, one use and one danger per band — and it is marked strictly, word by word. The rest is one equation used in three disguises. Every heading says where it was asked.

What a wave is, and what it does not carry

Spec 3.4

Waves transfer energy and information without transferring matter. That sentence is the definition, and the second half is the part that earns marks.

Watch a duck on a pond as ripples pass: it bobs up and down and stays put. The water is not travelling towards the shore — each bit of water moves about its own position and passes the disturbance on. Same with sound: the air by your ear vibrates, but no air travels from the speaker to you.

So what moves along is the disturbance, carrying energy with it. This is why a wave can be enormously powerful without anything being thrown at you.

Transverse and longitudinal

Spec 3.2 · 2025 Q4(a) · 2024 Q2(a)(iii) · Mock 2 Q6(a) — asked in some form on every paper

The difference is the direction the particles vibrate, relative to the direction the wave travels.

Particles vibrateExamples
TransversePerpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of travelAll EM waves, water ripples, waves on a rope
LongitudinalParallel to the direction of travelSound, ultrasound

A longitudinal wave is a pattern of squashed regions and stretched regions — compressions and rarefactions — travelling along. Push and pull the end of a slinky and you can watch them move.

2024's mark scheme gives one mark just for mentioning vibrations or oscillations before you say anything about direction. Start your answer with the word "vibrate" and you have banked a mark.

Then use parallel and perpendicular. "Up and down" and "side to side" are not precise enough, because they depend on which way you have drawn the wave.

Amplitude, wavelength, frequency and period

Spec 3.3, 3.24P · 2023 Q3(a) · Mock 1 Q4(a) — a 2-mark definition

  • Amplitude — the maximum displacement from the undisturbed position. Measured from the middle to a peak, not from peak to trough.
  • Wavelength (λ) — the distance for one complete wave, measured in metres. Peak to next peak works.
  • Frequency (f) — the number of complete waves passing a point per second, in hertz.
  • Period (T) — the time for one complete wave to pass, in seconds.
  • Wavefront — a line joining points on a wave that are all at the same stage, such as a line of crests.

"Explain what a frequency of 30 kHz means" — two marks

The mark scheme splits it in two, so say both halves: 30 000 complete waves 1 pass a point each second 1.

Saying only "30 000 hertz" scores nothing — that is restating the number, not explaining it. And remember the prefix: kHz means × 1000, and unit conversions like this are where the calculation marks go.

Worth knowing alongside: human hearing runs from 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz, so 30 kHz is ultrasound — above the range we can hear.

v = f λ — and the mark for quoting it

Spec 3.5, 3.7 · 2023 Q3(b) · 2025 Q4(c) · Mock 1 Q4(b)

v = f × λ
v = wave speed in metres/second (m/s) · f = frequency in hertz (Hz) · λ = wavelength in metres (m)

It works for every wave — sound, light, water, radio — which is why it appears three or four times a paper in different costumes.

2025's examiner: students "confused velocity for distance and did not quote v = fλ — quote the equation." There is a mark for writing it down before substituting, and it is regularly thrown away.

Same paper on significant figures: "a problem for a lot of students" — 5.5 × 10¹⁴ was rejected where 5.6 × 10¹⁴ was wanted. Carry the full number through your working and round only at the very end.

Watch the powers of ten. Light is 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s, wavelengths of light are around 10⁻⁷ m, and a slip of a single power costs the answer.

Echoes and sonar — the factor of two

2023 Q3(b)(iii) · Mock 1 Q4(c) — "the single most common error in echo and sonar problems"

A sonar pulse travels down to the sea bed and back again. The time you are given is for the whole round trip, so the distance you calculate from speed × time is twice the depth.

The method, with the halving made explicit

A pulse returns 0.16 s after being emitted. Sound travels at 1500 m/s in sea water. How deep is it?

1. Total distance travelled: 1500 × 0.16 = 240 m

2. That is there and back, so the depth = 240 ÷ 2 = 120 m

The factor of two is its own mark in both mark schemes. Write the words "there and back" in your working so the examiner can see you have used it.

The same applies in reverse: if you are given the depth and asked for the time, double the distance before dividing by the speed. In 2023, candidates who forgot scored 3 out of 4.

Period, frequency and the oscilloscope

Spec 3.6, 3.26P, 3.27P · Mock 2 Q6(c)

f = 1 ÷ T
f = frequency in hertz (Hz) · T = time period in seconds (s)

Frequency and period are two ways of saying the same thing. If one wave takes 0.005 s, then 200 fit into a second, so the frequency is 200 Hz.

A microphone connected to an oscilloscope turns a sound wave into a trace on a screen, letting you measure it. The horizontal axis is time, controlled by the time base setting — so many milliseconds per division.

Reading it, step by step

One complete wave spans 3 divisions; the time base is 2 ms per division.

1. Period: 3 × 2 ms = 6 ms = 0.006 s

2. Frequency: f = 1 ÷ 0.006 = 167 Hz

Convert milliseconds to seconds before taking the reciprocal. Forgetting gives an answer 1000 times out.

Note also what the trace shows: a taller wave means a larger amplitude, so a louder sound. A wave with more cycles across the screen means a higher frequency, so a higher pitch.

Sound: pitch, loudness, and measuring its speed

Spec 3.25P, 3.28P, 3.29P

Two straightforward links, and they are not interchangeable:

  • Pitch depends on the frequency of vibration of the source. Faster vibration, higher pitch.
  • Loudness depends on the amplitude of vibration of the source. Bigger vibration, louder sound.

To measure the speed of sound in air: stand a measured distance from a large flat wall, make a sharp sound, and time the delay before the echo returns. Speed = (2 × distance) ÷ time — the factor of two again, because the sound goes there and back. Repeat and average, and use a long distance so the time is big enough to measure reliably against your reaction time.

The Doppler effect

Spec 3.8 · 2025 Q4(b) · Mock 2 Q6(b) — 4 marks

When a source moves relative to you, the observed frequency and wavelength change. A siren sounds higher-pitched approaching and drops as it passes.

The reason is geometry, not physics of sound. As the source moves towards you it partly catches up with the waves it has already emitted, so they arrive bunched together — a shorter wavelength. Behind it, the waves are stretched out — a longer wavelength.

The four-mark answer, two marks for each direction:

  1. Approaching: the waves are squashed together, so the wavelength is shorter
  2. The wave speed is unchanged, so from v = fλ a shorter wavelength means a higher observed frequency — a higher pitch
  3. Receding: the waves are stretched out, so the wavelength is longer
  4. Again the speed is unchanged, so the observed frequency is lower — the pitch drops

Mock 2 is explicit: "do not write that the sound travels faster or slower. The speed is fixed by the air." The speed of sound depends on the medium, not on how fast the source is moving. What changes is the wavelength, and therefore the frequency you receive.

Note also that the source has not changed. The siren is emitting exactly the same note throughout — the driver hears no change at all.

The electromagnetic spectrum

Spec 3.10, 3.11 · 2025 Q1(a) · Mock 1 Q1(a)(c)

In order of increasing frequency and decreasing wavelength:

radio · microwave · infrared · visible · ultraviolet · X-ray · gamma

Within visible light, the same order runs red → orange → yellow → green → blue → indigo → violet: red has the longest wavelength and lowest frequency, violet the shortest and highest.

What all EM waves have in common, which is a two-mark question in its own right:

  • All are transverse
  • All transfer energy
  • All travel at the same speed in a vacuum (3.0 × 10⁸ m/s)
  • All can be reflected and refracted

Notice the pattern that makes the dangers memorable: the higher the frequency, the more energy each wave carries, and the more damaging it is. Radio waves pass through you harmlessly; gamma rays cause mutations.

Reflection and refraction

Spec 3.9

All waves can be reflected and refracted — sound as well as light.

Reflection bounces a wave off a surface; an echo is reflected sound. Refraction is the change of direction when a wave enters a different medium and changes speed.

The key to refraction questions: when a wave crosses into a new medium, its frequency stays the same — it is set by the source, and the waves cannot pile up at the boundary. So if the speed changes, from v = fλ the wavelength must change in proportion. Light entering water slows down, so its wavelength shortens.

Sound and light behave oppositely here, which is one of the standard "differences between sound and light" marks: light slows when it enters a denser medium, while sound speeds up — it travels faster in water than in air, and faster still in solids.

The Doppler effect

When a source moves towards an observer, the waves ahead of it are squashed together: wavelength decreases and observed frequency increases. Moving away, the waves are stretched: wavelength increases, frequency decreases.

The wave speed itself does not change — it's set by the medium. Only the observed wavelength and frequency change. Saying "the waves speed up" loses the mark.

04Understanding it

U1

You see lightning several seconds before you hear the thunder, even though both happen at the same instant. Estimate how far away a storm is if the gap is 6 seconds — and explain why you can ignore the travel time of the light.

Show answer

Sound: distance = speed × time = 340 × 6 = about 2000 m, or 2 km.

Light covers that same 2 km in 2000 ÷ (3 × 10⁸) ≈ 7 × 10⁻⁶ s — about seven millionths of a second. Compared with 6 seconds, that is utterly negligible, so you can treat the flash as instantaneous.

The wider point: light is roughly 900,000 times faster than sound. Any question involving both — thunder, a starting pistol seen and heard, an echo timed with a stopwatch — you assume the light arrives instantly.

U2

A wave passes from air into water and slows down. Its frequency stays the same. What happens to its wavelength, and why must the frequency be unchanged?

Show answer

From v = fλ, if v decreases and f is fixed, then λ must decrease. The wave gets shorter.

Why frequency can't change: frequency is set by the source. If 500 waves per second arrive at the boundary, 500 waves per second must leave it — otherwise waves would be piling up at the boundary or vanishing there, and neither happens. Waves are neither created nor destroyed at a boundary.

This is also the mechanism of refraction: the change of speed at the boundary is what bends the wave.

U3

An ambulance siren sounds higher-pitched as it approaches. Does the siren itself change? Explain what is physically happening to the waves.

Show answer

The siren doesn't change at all. It emits exactly the same frequency throughout. What changes is what reaches your ear.

As the ambulance moves towards you, it partially catches up with the waves it has already emitted. Each successive wave is emitted from a position slightly closer to you than the last, so the waves bunch up. The distance between them — the wavelength — decreases.

The waves still travel at 340 m/s through the air. So with v fixed and λ smaller, v = fλ means the frequency you receive is higher, and you hear a higher pitch.

As it passes and recedes, the waves stretch out, wavelength increases and pitch drops — which is why the sound falls audibly at the moment of passing.

U4

Why is ultraviolet dangerous to skin while visible light and infrared, which reach you in far greater quantities from the Sun, are not — despite all three being electromagnetic waves?

Show answer

Because the danger depends on frequency, not on quantity.

Higher frequency means each wave carries more energy. Infrared and visible light have relatively low frequencies — they deposit energy as heat, which the body handles routinely.

Ultraviolet sits above visible light in frequency, and carries enough energy per wave to be ionising: it can knock electrons off atoms and damage DNA in skin cells. Damaged DNA can cause mutations, which can lead to skin cancer. X-rays and gamma rays sit higher still and are correspondingly more dangerous.

The pattern to remember: moving right along the spectrum, frequency rises, energy per wave rises, and the biological danger shifts from heating to ionisation.

U5

A sonar question gives you the time for a pulse to return, and you calculate a distance of 240 m. Why is the answer to "how deep is the sea" not 240 m — and why is this described as the single most common error in the topic?

Show answer

Because the pulse travelled down to the sea bed and back up again. The 240 m is the whole round trip, so the depth is 240 ÷ 2 = 120 m.

Why it catches so many people: the calculation itself is easy — speed × time — and it produces a confident-looking number. Nothing in the arithmetic reminds you that the journey was doubled. You have to spot it from the physical situation, and under time pressure that step gets skipped.

The habit that fixes it: write the words "there and back" in your working before you divide. Both mark schemes award the factor of two as its own mark, so making it visible earns it even if you then slip in the arithmetic.

It works in reverse too. If you are given the depth and asked for the time, double the distance first. In 2023, candidates who forgot scored 3 out of 4.

U6

A police car passes you with its siren on and the pitch drops. A student says the sound waves slow down as the car moves away. What is wrong with that, and what actually changes?

Show answer

The speed of sound does not change at all. It is fixed by the medium — the air — and has nothing to do with how fast the source is moving. Mock 2's mark scheme says so explicitly.

What changes is the wavelength. As the car recedes, each wave is emitted from slightly further away than the last, so the waves arrive stretched out — a longer wavelength.

Then use v = fλ. The speed v is unchanged and the wavelength λ has increased, so the frequency f you receive must have decreased — and lower frequency means lower pitch.

The detail that proves the source hasn't changed: the driver hears no change whatsoever. The siren emits exactly the same note throughout. The effect exists only for an observer the source is moving relative to.

U7

Light slows down when it enters water, but sound speeds up when it enters water. Both are waves. Why do they behave in opposite ways?

Show answer

Because they travel by completely different mechanisms.

Sound needs particles to carry it — it is passed on by particles bumping into their neighbours. The closer together and more strongly linked those particles are, the faster the disturbance is handed along. So sound is fastest in solids, slower in liquids, slowest in gases, and cannot travel through a vacuum at all.

Light is an electromagnetic wave and needs no medium. It travels fastest in a vacuum, and anything it passes through only impedes it. So a denser medium slows light down.

What they share: in both cases the frequency is unchanged when the wave crosses the boundary — it is set by the source. So from v = fλ, whichever way the speed goes, the wavelength goes the same way.

This contrast is one of the standard marks in "give three differences between sound and light", along with longitudinal versus transverse and needing a medium versus not.

U8

The 2025 examiner refused "microwaving food" as a use of microwaves and "curing cancer" as a use of gamma rays. Both sound correct. Why were they rejected, and what should be written instead?

Show answer

Because this part of the specification is assessed as recall of specific wording, and near-misses do not score.

"Microwaving food" uses the name of the wave to describe its own use — it is circular and says nothing about what is happening. The specification wording is cooking, or heating food. (The other listed use is satellite transmissions.)

"Curing cancer" overstates it and is not the specification's phrase. Gamma rays are used for sterilising food and medical equipment, and for treating cancer. "Medicine" and "medical procedures" were also rejected for being too vague to show you know anything specific.

Mock 1 adds two more: "warming things up" is not "heaters" for infrared, and "tanning" is not "fluorescent lamps" for ultraviolet.

What to do about it: this is the one part of the course where paraphrasing is actively punished. Learn the table of uses and dangers in the exact words and reproduce them. There are only about a dozen phrases and they are worth several marks a year.

U9

Amplitude is measured from the middle of the wave to a peak, not from peak to trough. Why does it matter which one you use, and what does amplitude correspond to for a sound?

Show answer

Because measuring peak to trough gives you double the amplitude. Amplitude is defined as the maximum displacement from the undisturbed position — from the rest line to the top, not the full height of the wave.

On an oscilloscope trace this is an easy mark to throw away: the eye is drawn to the full vertical height of the wave, which is twice what is being asked for.

For a sound, amplitude means loudness. A larger amplitude of vibration at the source produces a louder sound. Frequency is separate and controls the pitch.

The two are independent, which is worth checking you believe: you can play the same note quietly or loudly (same frequency, different amplitude), and you can play a high note and a low note at the same volume (different frequency, same amplitude). On a screen, taller means louder and more-cycles-across means higher-pitched.

02How it actually works

A small topic — seven spec points — but it appears on every paper and the 2025 report on it is the bluntest in the pack: "Overall this part not done well." Almost all of the lost marks come from four specific wordings, which are flagged below.

Only electrons move

Spec 2.24P, 2.26P · 2023 Q1 · Mock 2 Q8(a) · Mock 1 Q2(a)

Everything in this topic is one idea: rubbing transfers electrons from one material to the other. That is the whole mechanism, and every explanation is a version of it.

An atom has a positive nucleus and negative electrons around it. The nucleus is locked in place; the outer electrons can be scraped off by friction. So:

  • The material that gains electrons becomes negatively charged
  • The material that loses electrons is left positively charged

Note what that second line does not say. A positive charge is not created by adding anything — it is what remains when negatives are removed. Protons never move. If you find yourself writing that positive charges were transferred, that is wrong and it is a common way to lose the mark.

It also follows that the two objects always end up with equal and opposite charges, because every electron one gains is one the other lost. Charge is conserved.

The three-mark script for "explain how X becomes charged"

1. Friction (rubbing) between X and Y 1

2. Electrons are transferred from one to the other 1

3. X gains electrons and so becomes negatively charged, while Y is left positively charged 1

Name the direction the electrons move. "They become charged" without saying which way scores at most one.

Conductors, insulators, and why only insulators stay charged

Spec 2.22P, 2.23P · 2025 Q2(a)

Metals are conductors — they contain free electrons that can move through the material. Plastics are insulators — their electrons are bound to their atoms and cannot move.

This explains a result that otherwise looks arbitrary: rub a plastic rod and it charges; rub a metal rod held in your hand and nothing happens.

In both cases friction transfers electrons. The difference is what happens next. On the insulator the charge cannot move, so it stays where it was put. On the metal, the charge flows freely through the rod, through you, and away to earth — so no charge accumulates and there is nothing to detect.

2025 asked this as a car being polished: the pad charges on the plastic parts and not on the earthed metal ones, for exactly this reason.

Forces between charges

Spec 2.25P

Like charges repel. Unlike charges attract. Two negatives push apart, two positives push apart, a positive and a negative pull together.

2025's examiner: "Any mention of magnets or electromagnetic force automatically lost all marks."

This is not magnetism. Magnets have north and south poles and act on iron; charges are positive and negative and act on charged objects. The words "magnetic", "magnetised" and "attracted like a magnet" destroy an answer here, however well the rest is written.

Earthing — and the mark almost everyone loses

Spec 2.27P · 2024 Q3(c) · Mock 1 Q2(b)

Earthing means connecting a charged object to the ground with a conductor, so charge can flow away instead of building up.

The mark scheme is unusually specific here, in both papers. From 2025: "Some missed out on MP4 as they didn't state that the e-s went to earth and simply repeated the stem." And mock 1: "the electrons must be stated to flow to earth. 'It's earthed' restates the situation and scores nothing."

So the answer must say where the charge goes:

✗ "The aircraft is earthed, so it is safe."
✓ "Connect an earthing wire, so that electrons flow to earth and charge cannot build up."

The examiner is testing whether you understand earthing as a path for charge rather than as a magic word.

Attracting things that are not charged

Spec 2.26P · Mock 2 Q8(b) · 2023 Q1 — cling film

A charged rod attracts an uncharged stream of water, or scraps of paper, or sticks cling film to a plate. If the water is uncharged, why is there any force at all?

Because the charged rod rearranges the charges inside the neutral object without adding any:

  1. A negative rod is brought near. It repels the electrons in the water, pushing them to the far side of the stream.
  2. The near side is left with an induced positive charge; the far side is negative. The object is still neutral overall.
  3. The near side is closer to the rod than the far side, so the attraction beats the repulsion, and the stream bends towards the rod.

Mock 2: "any suggestion that the water is charged, or that it is repelled, loses marks. The department was emphatic about this." And from 2025: "Any mention of repulsion of the water stream lost the 2nd mark."

The stream is always attracted, whichever sign the rod carries — because the rod induces whichever charge it needs on the near side. Say induced, and say the stream stays uncharged overall.

The gold-leaf electroscope

Spec 2.23P · 2024 Q3(a) · Mock 2 Q8(c) — "loads didn't know the name"

A metal cap on a metal stem, with a thin gold leaf hinged against the stem, all inside a case. It detects charge.

Touch a charged rod on the cap and the leaf rises. The three-mark explanation:

  1. Charge from the rod spreads through the metal cap and stem to the leaf — the metal is a conductor 1
  2. The stem and the leaf now carry the same charge 1
  3. Like charges repel, so the leaf is pushed away from the stem and rises 1

The larger the charge, the further the leaf rises — so it measures as well as detects.

2025: "Loads didn't know the name of the gold leaf electroscope — lost a mark if so, as this is specifically mentioned in the spec." Learn the name. It is free marks for a piece of vocabulary.

Dangers

Spec 2.27P · 2024 Q3(c) · Mock 1 Q2(b)

The danger is always the same chain, and each link is a mark:

  1. Friction (fuel flowing through a pipe, a tanker driving along) transfers electrons, so charge builds up
  2. The charge builds until it is large enough that a spark jumps
  3. The spark can ignite the fuel vapour, causing a fire or explosion
  4. Prevented by earthing or bonding, so electrons flow to earth and charge cannot accumulate

Note that it is the vapour that ignites, not the liquid, and that the spark is the mechanism — "static electricity is dangerous" on its own explains nothing.

Uses

Spec 2.28P

Two named in the specification, both working by "unlike charges attract":

The photocopier. An image of the page is projected onto a charged plate. Where light falls the charge leaks away; the dark parts stay charged. Oppositely charged toner powder is attracted only to those charged regions, and is then pressed and heated onto the paper.

The inkjet printer. The ink is broken into tiny droplets which are given a charge as they leave the nozzle. They then pass between charged plates that deflect them by a controlled amount, steering each droplet to the right point on the paper.

The calculations — and standard form

2024 Q3(b) · 2025 Q2(b) · Mock 1 Q2(c) — "Very few used standard form — please use it!"

Two equations from the circuits topic reappear here, applied to very small charges:

Q = I × t
Q = charge in coulombs (C) · I = current in amperes (A) · t = time in seconds (s)

E = Q × V
E = energy transferred in joules (J) · Q = charge in coulombs (C) · V = voltage in volts (V)

Static charges are tiny — of the order of 10⁻⁷ to 10⁻⁹ C — so the numbers are always in powers of ten and the currents come out around 10⁻¹⁰ A.

2025: "Very few used standard form — please use it!" and far too many power-of-ten errors. Write 8.3 × 10⁻⁷ C, not 0.00000083 C — it is easier to get right and it is what the mark scheme expects.

Practise entering these on your calculator using the EXP or ×10ˣ key rather than typing "× 10 ^". Typing it out long-hand is where the stray zero creeps in.

The one rule everything follows from

Only electrons move. Protons never move.

An object becomes negatively charged by gaining electrons. An object becomes positively charged by losing electrons. There is no such thing as gaining or losing protons in electrostatics.

Every explanation in this topic is a sentence about which way electrons went.

Charging by friction

  1. Two insulators are rubbed together.
  2. Friction transfers electrons from one to the other.
  3. The material that gains electrons becomes negatively charged.
  4. The material that loses electrons becomes positively charged, by an equal amount.

Note that the two charges are always equal and opposite — charge is conserved, it's only been moved.

Forces between charges

SituationForce
Like charges (both + or both −)Repel
Unlike charges (+ and −)Attract
Charged object and uncharged insulatorAttract — see below

Why a charged rod attracts uncharged paper: the rod's charge causes the electrons in the paper to shift slightly — repelled to the far side if the rod is negative, drawn to the near side if positive. The near face of the paper therefore carries an opposite charge to the rod and is attracted, while the more distant like charge is repelled less strongly. This is induced charge, and it's the only way a charged object attracts something neutral.

The famous demonstration is bending a stream of water with a charged rod. The 2025 examiner: "Any mention of repulsion of the water stream lost the 2nd mark – too many think water is charged!"

The water is neutral. It bends because charge is induced in it and it is attracted. It always bends towards the rod, whichever sign the rod carries.

Conductors, insulators and earthing

Conductor (metal)Insulator (plastic, rubber, glass)
ElectronsFree to move throughoutBound in place, cannot move through the material
Holds static charge?Not if it's earthed or handheld — charge flows away instantlyYes — charge stays where it's put

Earthing means connecting an object to the ground with a conductor. If the object is negatively charged, the excess electrons flow through the conductor to earth. If it is positively charged, electrons flow from earth onto the object. Either way the object ends up neutral.

This is where the 2025 marks were lost. It is not enough to write "it is earthed so the charge goes away". You must say which way the electrons flow, and that they flow to (or from) earth. The examiner refused the mark for answers that "simply repeated the stem".

The gold-leaf electroscope

Learn the name. It is in the specification and a mark was deducted for not knowing it.

How it works: a metal cap connects via a metal rod to a thin piece of gold leaf. Bring a charged object near, or touch the cap with it, and charge spreads through the metal to the rod and the leaf. The rod and leaf now carry the same charge, so they repel, and the leaf lifts away from the rod. The larger the charge, the further the leaf rises.

It's a detector of charge, and the deflection is the evidence.

Dangers and uses

Danger: refuelling an aircraft or tanker

  • Fuel flowing through the pipe rubs against it, transferring electrons — charge builds up by friction
  • If enough charge accumulates, a spark can jump between the pipe and the aircraft
  • The spark can ignite the fuel vapour, causing an explosion
  • Prevention: a bonding wire earths the aircraft and the fuel pipe, so electrons flow to earth and no charge builds up

Danger: lightning and touching a tap after walking on carpet

Same physics: friction charges an insulated object, charge builds, and it discharges suddenly through a conductor. A lightning conductor gives the charge a safe path to earth. Carpets with thin copper wires conduct the charge away as it forms.

Uses

Photocopier: a charged drum attracts oppositely charged toner powder, which is transferred to paper and fused by heat.

Inkjet printer: ink droplets are given a charge, then deflected by charged plates to steer them precisely onto the paper.

Electrostatic spray painting: paint droplets are given a charge and the object the opposite charge, so paint is attracted evenly and wraps round the object, wasting less.

04Understanding it

U1

You rub a metal rod with a cloth while holding it in your hand. Nothing happens. You rub a plastic rod the same way and it charges. Explain.

Show answer

Friction transfers electrons in both cases. The difference is what happens next.

The metal rod is a conductor, and you are holding it — your body and the ground provide a conducting path. The transferred electrons flow straight through the metal, through you, to earth. The rod cannot retain a charge, so it stays neutral.

The plastic rod is an insulator. Its electrons cannot move through the material, so the charge stays exactly where friction put it. The rod remains charged.

The test question: could you charge the metal rod? Yes — hold it by an insulating handle so there's no path to earth. That proves it's the earthing, not the metal.

U2

A positively charged rod is brought near a gold-leaf electroscope without touching it. The leaf rises. The rod is taken away without touching. The leaf falls again. Explain both.

Show answer

Leaf rises: the positive rod attracts free electrons in the metal, drawing them up to the cap. This leaves the rod and leaf deficient in electrons, so both are positively charged. Like charges repel, so the leaf lifts away.

Leaf falls: the electroscope's total charge never changed — it was only redistributed. Remove the rod and the electrons flow back down, everything returns to neutral, and the leaf drops.

The contrast worth knowing: if you had touched the cap with the rod, electrons would have transferred permanently onto the rod. The electroscope would keep a positive charge and the leaf would stay up after the rod was removed. Induction is temporary; contact is permanent.

U3

Two objects are rubbed together. One ends up with a charge of −4 × 10⁻⁹ C. What is the charge on the other, and why can you be certain without knowing what the objects were?

Show answer

+4 × 10⁻⁹ C — exactly equal in size, opposite in sign.

You can be certain because charge is conserved. Friction doesn't create charge, it moves electrons from one object to the other. Every electron gained by one is an electron lost by the other. If one gains a charge of −Q, the other must be left with +Q.

This is worth checking in any question giving both charges — if they aren't equal and opposite, either something else has been earthed or you've misread.

U4

Why is a spark dangerous during refuelling but not when you touch a door handle after walking across carpet — given both involve roughly similar amounts of charge?

Show answer

The spark itself is much the same. The difference is entirely in what surrounds it.

A door handle sits in ordinary air. The spark releases a tiny amount of energy, you feel a sting, nothing else happens.

During refuelling, the air around the nozzle is full of fuel vapour, which is flammable. The spark supplies enough energy to ignite it, and the fuel supplies the rest — an explosion whose energy comes from the fuel, not the spark.

The exam-relevant point: when a question asks why static is dangerous, the answer is rarely the charge itself. It's that a spark acts as an ignition source in a flammable atmosphere, or that a large discharge can pass through a person.

U5

A charged rod attracts a thin stream of uncharged water. A student writes: "the water must have picked up a small charge, which the rod attracts." The mark scheme rejects this outright. What is actually happening?

Show answer

The water stays uncharged overall. Nothing is transferred to it — the rod only rearranges the charge already inside it.

  1. Bring a negative rod close. It repels the electrons in the water, pushing them to the far side of the stream.
  2. The near side is left with an induced positive charge; the far side is negative. Total charge: still zero.
  3. The near side is closer to the rod, so its attraction is stronger than the far side's repulsion. Net result: the stream bends towards the rod.

The test that proves it is induction: the stream is attracted whichever sign the rod carries. A positive rod would pull electrons towards the near side instead, inducing a negative charge there — and again, attraction. If the water genuinely held a fixed charge, one sign of rod would repel it.

Mock 2: "any suggestion that the water is charged, or that it is repelled, loses marks. The department was emphatic about this." Use the word induced.

U6

Why can you never charge an object positively by adding positive charge to it? What actually happens instead, and what does this tell you about the other object involved?

Show answer

Because the positive charge lives in the nucleus of each atom, and nuclei do not move. Friction can scrape off loosely held outer electrons; it cannot dislodge protons.

So a positive charge is always produced by removing electrons. The object is left with more protons than electrons, and the imbalance is what we call a positive charge.

What it tells you about the other object: those electrons went somewhere. Whatever was rubbed against it has gained exactly the electrons the first one lost, so it carries an equal and opposite negative charge.

That is why you can answer questions like "one object ends up with −4 × 10⁻⁹ C; what is on the other?" without knowing anything about the materials. It must be +4 × 10⁻⁹ C. Charge is conserved.

Writing that "positive charges were transferred" is a standard way to lose a mark. Only electrons move.

U7

"The aircraft is earthed, so refuelling is safe." The mark scheme gives this no credit at all, even though it is true. Why, and what would score?

Show answer

Because it restates the situation rather than explaining it. The question has already told you there is an earthing wire; saying so back demonstrates nothing.

The examiners want evidence that you know what earthing physically does: it provides a conducting path along which electrons can flow to the ground, so charge cannot accumulate to the point where a spark jumps.

What scores: "An earthing wire connects the aircraft to the ground, so electrons flow to earth and charge does not build up. Without this, charge would accumulate until a spark jumped, which could ignite the fuel vapour and cause an explosion."

Both mark schemes flag this specifically. From 2025: "Some missed out on MP4 as they didn't state that the e-s went to earth and simply repeated the stem."

The general lesson: if your sentence could be written by someone who had only read the question, it will not score. Name the mechanism.

U8

A photocopier and an inkjet printer both rely on static electricity, but they use it in different ways. Explain how each works, and identify the single principle they share.

Show answer

The shared principle: unlike charges attract, and a charge can be used to move small particles precisely.

Photocopier — charge is used to decide where powder sticks. An image of the page is projected onto a charged plate. Where light lands the charge leaks away; the dark regions of the image stay charged. Toner powder carrying the opposite charge is then attracted only to those still-charged areas, reproducing the image, and is pressed and heated onto the paper to fix it.

Inkjet printer — charge is used to steer droplets. The ink is broken into tiny droplets, each given a charge as it leaves the nozzle. They then pass between charged deflecting plates, which pull them sideways by an amount that depends on their charge — so each droplet can be aimed at exactly the right point on the page.

The difference in one line: the photocopier uses charge to control where the powder is attracted to; the printer uses it to control where a moving droplet lands.

02How it actually works

Half-life is defined in two of the three past papers and calculated in all three. Get that definition exactly right and you have banked marks before the paper starts. Every heading says where it was asked.

The atom, and what the numbers mean

Spec 7.2, 7.3 · 2023 Q2(a)–(d) — six marks of multiple choice

A nucleus of protons and neutrons, with electrons around it. Written as 146C:

  • The bottom number is the atomic (proton) number — the number of protons. It decides which element it is.
  • The top number is the mass (nucleon) number — protons + neutrons together.
  • Neutrons = top − bottom. For carbon-14: 14 − 6 = 8 neutrons.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are both carbon — six protons each — but one has six neutrons and the other eight.

An ion is different again, and is about electrons: adding an electron makes a negative ion, removing one makes a positive ion. The nucleus is untouched.

Why nuclei decay, and the three radiations

Spec 7.4, 7.5 · Mock 2 Q4(b)

Some nuclei are unstable. They emit radiation from the nucleus, at random, to become more stable. All three types are ionising.

What it isStopped byIonising power
Alpha (α)2 protons + 2 neutrons — a helium nucleusA sheet of paper, or a few cm of airStrongly ionising
Beta (β−)A fast-moving electron from the nucleusA few mm of aluminiumModerately

The pattern is worth noticing because it is the reason behind every application: the more strongly ionising a radiation is, the less far it penetrates. Alpha does its damage immediately and stops; gamma passes through almost everything precisely because it interacts so little.

Identifying an unknown source — the standard three marks

The count is unchanged by paper, but drops to background level with 5 mm of aluminium.

It is beta 1 — because paper does not reduce the count, so it is not alpha 1, and 5 mm of aluminium reduces it to background, so it is not gamma, which would penetrate 1.

Two of the three marks are for ruling the other two out. Naming the radiation alone scores one. Always write both eliminations.

Ionisation

Spec 7.4 · 2024 Q5(a)(ii)

Ionisation is the removal of electron(s) from an atom. That is the mark-scheme wording, and it is worth a mark on its own.

It matters because it is how radiation damages living things. An ionising particle rips electrons from the atoms it passes, which breaks molecules apart — including DNA — causing cell damage and mutations.

Decay equations

Spec 7.7, 7.8 · 2024 Q5(a)(i) · Mock 1 Q9(e)

Learn the effect of each emission on the two numbers:

Beta looks strange — the nucleus emits an electron and its proton count goes up. The reason: a neutron in the nucleus turns into a proton, and the electron created is thrown out. So the total number of nucleons is the same, but one more of them is a proton.

Balancing is the whole technique: the top numbers must total the same on both sides, and so must the bottom numbers. For the beta decay of carbon-14:

146C → 147N + 0−1e

Top: 14 = 14 + 0 ✓. Bottom: 6 = 7 + (−1) ✓. The beta particle is written 0−1e — no nucleons, charge −1. An alpha particle is 42He.

Background radiation

Spec 7.10 · 2025 Q9(b) · Mock 2 Q4(a)

Ionising radiation that is present all the time, everywhere, even when no source is being used.

Sources: rocks and soil (especially granite), radon gas, cosmic rays from space, food and drink, and medical procedures such as X-rays.

2025's examiner penalised two specific things:

Do not use the word "background" inside the definition — "a number tried to use the word background in their answer which I didn't allow". Defining a term using itself explains nothing.

Do not say it comes only from natural sources — "A number of students were referring to the idea that it must come from natural sources, which I punished." Medical procedures and nuclear industry waste are part of it too.

Activity and half-life

Spec 7.11, 7.12 · 2024 Q5(b)(i) · 2025 Q9(c)(i) · Mock 1 Q9(b) — defined in two of three papers

Activity is the rate at which nuclei decay, measured in becquerels (Bq). One becquerel is one decay per second. Activity decreases over time, because there are fewer undecayed nuclei left to decay.

Half-life is the time taken for the activity of a sample — or the number of undecayed nuclei in it — to halve.

Both papers flag the same error: "The time for a nucleus to halve" scores zero. A single nucleus cannot halve. It either decays or it doesn't, and you cannot predict when.

The thing that halves is a property of the whole sample — its activity, its number of undecayed nuclei, or the mass of the isotope remaining. Say "of a sample" and the mark is safe.

Half-life calculations — and the background trap

Spec 7.13 · 2024 Q5(b)(ii) · 2025 Q9(c)(ii) · Mock 1 Q9(c) · Mock 2 Q4(d)

The method is repeated halving. Count how many halvings fit into the time, then halve that many times.

Worked example — activity after a given time

Initial activity 12 000 Bq, half-life 6.0 hours. What is the activity after 1 day?

1. Number of half-lives: 24 ÷ 6 = 4

2. Halve four times: 12 000 → 6000 → 3000 → 1500 → 750 Bq

Write the chain out. Mark schemes award a mark for the evidence of halving even if the final number slips.

Worked example — finding the half-life from a count rate

Background is 24 counts/min. With the source, the detector reads 424 counts/min. The corrected count rate falls to 50 counts/min after 96 minutes. Find the half-life.

1. Correct the initial reading for background. 424 − 24 = 400 counts/min. (The 50 is already corrected — read the question carefully to see which figures are which.)

2. Count the halvings: 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 is 3 half-lives

3. Divide: 96 ÷ 3 = 32 minutes

Subtract the background before you do anything else. Mock 2 is explicit: "Working from 424 gives a wrong number of half-lives and loses all three marks." The detector counts background whether the source is there or not, so the raw reading is not the source's activity.

From a graph: draw the lines. 2025 wanted "lines drawn on the graph from an activity to half that activity" — pick a starting activity, find where the curve reaches half of it, and read off the time difference. The construction lines are a mark.

And quote numbers. On the 2025 part asking candidates to test a relationship: "many candidates referred to the shape of the graph and tried to justify their answers without any data, which was awarded zero marks." Take readings off the graph and calculate with them.

Randomness

Spec 7.4 · 2023 Q2(e) · Mock 2 Q4(e)

Radioactive decay is a random process: it is impossible to predict when any individual nucleus will decay, and each has the same chance in a given time regardless of how long it has already existed.

Half-life is not a contradiction of this. With enormous numbers of nuclei, the average behaviour is highly predictable even though each individual event is not — in the same way that no one can say which particular people will make a phone call in the next minute, yet the total number of calls is very predictable.

Uses, contamination and dangers

Spec 7.14, 7.15, 7.16 · Mock 1 Q9(d) · 2024 Q5(a)(iii)

Contamination versus irradiation — a distinction the specification names explicitly:

  • Irradiation is being exposed to radiation from a source outside you. It stops when you move away, and the object does not become radioactive.
  • Contamination is getting radioactive material on or inside you. It continues to irradiate you until it is removed or decays, which makes it the more serious.

Dangers and precautions: ionising radiation damages cells and tissue and can cause mutations. Half-life calculations

Method — activity after n half-lives

  1. Number of half-lives = total time ÷ half-life
  2. Halve the starting activity that many times

Example: 800 Bq, half-life 6 days, after 24 days. 24 ÷ 6 = 4 half-lives. 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 Bq.

Method — finding half-life from a graph

  1. Pick a convenient starting activity on the curve
  2. Find where the activity has fallen to half that value
  3. The half-life is the time between those two points
  4. Check it by repeating from a different starting point — it should be the same

If the graph shows a background level, subtract it from all readings first. The curve should tend towards zero, not towards the background.

Radioactive decay is random — you cannot predict when any individual nucleus will decay. Half-life is a statistical average over an enormous number of nuclei, which is why it's reliable for a sample and meaningless for one atom.

04Understanding it

U1

Alpha radiation is the most dangerous of the three if the source is swallowed, but the least dangerous if the source is across the room. Explain this apparent contradiction.

Show answer

Both facts follow from the same property: alpha is strongly ionising and weakly penetrating.

Across the room: alpha particles are absorbed by a few centimetres of air, and cannot penetrate the dead outer layer of your skin. Essentially none of the radiation reaches living tissue, so it does no harm.

Swallowed: the source is now inside, in direct contact with living cells. There's no air gap and no skin barrier. All that strong ionising power is deposited directly into tissue over a very short distance, causing intense localised damage to cells and DNA.

Gamma is the reverse: dangerous from outside because it penetrates, but less damaging per unit of exposure because it ionises weakly and much of it passes straight through.

U2

A sample has a half-life of 10 days. After 10 days half the nuclei have decayed. Why don't the remaining nuclei decay in the next 10 days, leaving none?

Show answer

Because decay is random, not scheduled. Each surviving nucleus has the same probability of decaying in the next 10 days as it always had — about one in two. Nuclei do not age or "queue up".

So in the second 10 days, half of the remaining half decays, leaving a quarter. Then an eighth, then a sixteenth. Each half-life halves whatever is left, which is why the graph is a curve approaching zero rather than a straight line hitting it.

Consequence worth knowing: in principle the activity never reaches exactly zero. In practice it eventually drops below the background level and can't be distinguished from it.

U3

In beta decay, an electron is emitted from the nucleus. But the nucleus contains only protons and neutrons, and electrons orbit outside it. Where does the electron come from?

Show answer

It doesn't come from the orbiting shells. It is created at the moment of decay, when a neutron inside the nucleus turns into a proton and an electron. The proton stays; the electron is ejected at high speed as the beta particle.

This explains the bookkeeping perfectly:

  • Mass number unchanged — one neutron became one proton, so the total nucleon count is the same
  • Atomic number increases by 1 — there is now one more proton, so it is a different element
  • Charge conserved — a neutral neutron became a +1 proton and a −1 electron, net zero

Nuclei emit beta radiation when they have too many neutrons relative to protons. Converting one to the other moves them towards stability.

U4

A medical tracer needs a short half-life. A smoke detector source needs a long one. Explain both, and identify what makes them opposite requirements.

Show answer

Medical tracer — short half-life (hours). It must remain active long enough to be imaged, but decay quickly afterwards so the patient isn't exposed for longer than necessary. A long half-life would mean carrying a radioactive source around for years.

Smoke detector — long half-life (hundreds of years). It must keep working reliably for the whole life of the device without being replaced. A short half-life would mean the activity drops and the detector silently stops working.

The underlying trade-off: half-life determines how long a source stays useful and how long it stays hazardous. Where the source is inside a person, you want it gone fast. Where it's sealed inside a device that nobody touches, you want it to last.

The same reasoning explains why nuclear waste is such a problem: the isotopes that make good long-term fuel are exactly the ones that stay dangerous for millennia.

U5

"Half-life is the time taken for a nucleus to halve." This scores zero in every mark scheme. Why is it wrong — not just badly worded, but actually wrong?

Show answer

Because a single nucleus cannot halve. It has only two possible states: undecayed, or decayed. There is no halfway. And decay is random, so it is impossible to predict when any particular nucleus will go.

What halves is a property of the whole sample. Any of these score:

  • the time taken for the activity of a sample to halve
  • the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei in a sample to halve
  • the time taken for the mass of the isotope remaining to halve

Why the average is predictable when the individual is not: there are astronomically many nuclei in any real sample. Each has a fixed probability of decaying in a given time, so the total number decaying per second is highly consistent — just as no one can say which individuals will phone in the next minute, yet the total number of calls is very predictable.

Two of the three past papers define half-life. Learn one of the three wordings above and write it verbatim.

U6

A detector reads 424 counts per minute with a source present, and 24 counts per minute with it removed. A student calculates half-lives starting from 424. Why does this lose every mark in the question?

Show answer

Because 424 is not the activity of the source. It is the source plus the background radiation that is always present in the room.

The source's true contribution is 424 − 24 = 400 counts per minute. The background does not decay away, so it stays at 24 throughout while the source's contribution halves.

Why it destroys the whole answer: the halving chain from the wrong start never lands on the given values. From 400 you get a clean 400 → 200 → 100 → 50. From 424 you get 424 → 212 → 106 → 53, which matches nothing, so the number of half-lives is wrong and every subsequent figure follows it.

U7

Alpha radiation is the most strongly ionising but travels the shortest distance. Gamma is the least ionising and travels furthest. Why are these two properties linked, and what does it mean for choosing a source?

Show answer

They are linked because ionising is how radiation loses energy. Every time a particle rips an electron off an atom, it gives up some of its own energy.

An alpha particle is large and highly charged, so it ionises very heavily — and therefore runs out of energy within a few centimetres of air. Gamma is an electromagnetic wave with no charge, so it interacts only rarely, keeps its energy, and travels a long way.

So the two properties are the same fact seen twice: heavy interaction means short range; light interaction means long range.

What it means for choosing a source:

  • Medical tracer → gamma. It must escape the body to be detected, and it should do minimal damage on the way. Alpha would be absorbed in the first few centimetres of tissue, never reach the detector, and cause serious cell damage while doing so.
  • Smoke detector → alpha. You need heavy ionisation of the air to produce a current, and the tiny range means it is harmless a few centimetres outside the device.
  • Thickness monitoring → beta. It must be partly absorbed by the sheet. Alpha would be stopped completely and gamma would pass through unchanged, so neither would respond to thickness.
U8

A nucleus emits a beta particle — an electron — and its atomic number goes up by one. How can throwing something out of the nucleus increase the proton count, when the nucleus contains no electrons to begin with?

Show answer

Because the electron is created at the moment of decay, not stored and released.

Inside the nucleus, a neutron turns into a proton. Doing so produces an electron, which is immediately ejected — that is the beta particle.

Follow the bookkeeping:

  • Neutron count falls by one, proton count rises by one. Total nucleons unchanged, so the mass number stays the same.
  • One extra proton means the atomic number rises by one — it is now a different element.
  • Charge is conserved: a neutral neutron became a positive proton plus a negative electron. Which is why the beta particle is written 0−1e, and why the bottom numbers balance as 6 = 7 + (−1).

Contrast with alpha, which really is a chunk of the nucleus leaving: two protons and two neutrons, so mass number down 4 and atomic number down 2.

U9

Being irradiated and being contaminated both expose you to radiation. Which is more serious, and why does the distinction matter practically?

Show answer

Contamination is more serious.

Irradiation means radiation from a source outside you reaches your body. Move away, or put shielding in between, and it stops immediately. The object being irradiated does not become radioactive itself — which is why irradiated food is perfectly safe to eat.

Contamination means radioactive material has got onto or into you. The source is now travelling with you, so it keeps irradiating you until it is physically removed or decays away. You cannot walk away from it.

Why it changes the danger from each type: the ranking reverses. Outside the body, alpha is the least dangerous — it cannot even penetrate skin — while gamma is the worst because it reaches your organs. Inside the body, alpha is by far the worst, because all of its heavy ionising power is delivered directly into living tissue with nothing to absorb it first.

Practically: irradiation is handled with distance, shielding and short exposure times. Contamination is handled by preventing contact altogether — gloves, sealed sources, remote handling tongs.

Plotting — the marks are mechanical

Lines and curves of best fit

2023 Q4(b)(ii) · 2023 Q7(b)(i) · Mock 1 Q8(c)(i)

A line of best fit is a single smooth line or straight line that shows the overall trend, with roughly as many points above it as below. The mark schemes want it within about one small square of the points.

It is not dot-to-dot. Joining the points with straight segments treats every measurement as perfect, when each one carries some error — the whole purpose of the line is to average those errors out. A zig-zag also implies the quantity really jumped around between readings, which it did not.

If the data is curved, draw a smooth curve. Do not force a straight line through curved data, and do not draw a curve through data that is genuinely straight. And an anomalous point is ignored when drawing the line — not accommodated by bending it.

Anomalies

Mock 1 Q8(c)(ii)

An anomaly is a point that clearly breaks the pattern the others establish. Spot it by looking at the trend, not at the number in isolation.

The two-mark answer has two halves, and the second is usually forgotten:

  1. Identify it — quote the coordinates of the point
  2. Say what to do: repeat that measurement, and exclude it from the line of best fit if it cannot be reproduced

"Ignore it" alone is not enough. The correct scientific response is to check it first — it might be a recording error, or it might be real.

Reading data off a graph

2023 Q7(b)(ii)(iv) · 2025 Q9(d)

When a question says use data from the graph, it means take actual numbers off it and calculate with them.

2025: "Many candidates referred to the shape of the graph and tried to justify their answers without any data, which was awarded zero marks."

"The graph curves, so it isn't proportional" describes the graph. It scores nothing. Take two pairs of readings, do the arithmetic on each, and compare the results — that is using data.

Describing a method

These are worth five or six marks and appear on every paper. Work through a fixed skeleton so nothing is missed:

  1. Name the independent variable and say you will test at least five values of it
  2. Name the dependent variable and the instrument used to measure it — ruler, balance, stopwatch, thermometer

04Understanding it

U1

A student measures the boiling point of water five times and gets 96.2, 96.3, 96.2, 96.3, 96.2 °C. The true value is 100 °C.

Are the results reliable? Are they accurate? What is probably wrong?

Show answer

Reliable — yes. The readings are closely grouped and repeating gives essentially the same answer. They're also precise.

Accurate — no. They are consistently about 3.8 °C below the true value.

What's wrong: a systematic error — most likely a thermometer that is miscalibrated, or being read incorrectly the same way each time. Because the error is the same every time, repeating doesn't reveal it.

The lesson worth taking into the exam: repeating measurements improves reliability but does nothing about systematic error. If a question asks how to make results more accurate, "repeat them" is the wrong answer — you need calibration, a better instrument, or a change of technique.

U2

A student investigates how quickly different metals conduct heat, using rods of different lengths and thicknesses.

Is the problem with validity or reliability? Write the fix as the examiner wants it.

Show answer

Validity. The independent variable is meant to be the type of metal, but length and thickness are also varying. If rod A conducts faster you cannot tell whether that's because of the metal or because it was shorter. The test isn't fair.

Reliability isn't the issue — you could repeat this experiment perfectly consistently and still get an answer that means nothing.

The fix, phrased as an instruction: "Use rods of the same length and the same diameter." Not "the rods were different lengths", which describes the problem rather than improving it. The 2025 examiner deducted marks for exactly that.

U3

Why is dot-to-dot wrong for a line graph, when it passes through every measured point perfectly?

Show answer

Because every measurement carries some uncertainty. Dot-to-dot treats each point as exactly correct, which means it treats measurement error as though it were real physical behaviour — producing kinks and wobbles that reflect nothing but the limits of your ruler.

A line of best fit assumes the underlying relationship is smooth and that the scatter around it is error. It averages out the random errors, which is why a value read off the line is more trustworthy than any individual measured point.

It also lets you spot anomalies. A point far from an otherwise good line is visibly wrong. On a dot-to-dot, every point sits on the line by construction, so nothing looks wrong.

U4

A question asks you to "use data from the graph" to test a claim. Why is "the graph is a curve, so the relationship isn't linear" worth zero marks even when it's true?

Show answer

Because the question asked for data, and shape is not data. The mark scheme awards marks for reading numbers off the graph, doing something with them, and comparing the outcomes.

The 2025 examiner: "Many candidates referred to the shape of the graph, and tried to justify their answers without any data, which was awarded zero marks." Students scored 0 or 4 — nothing in between — because either you read numbers off or you didn't.

The procedure to follow whenever you see "use data from the graph":

  1. Read one pair of values off the graph and write them down
  2. Read a second pair off and write them down
  3. Do the arithmetic the claim requires to each pair
  4. State both results and say explicitly whether they agree

Write the numbers on the page. An examiner cannot award a mark for a calculation you did in your head.

U5

A question asks for a variable that must be controlled when comparing how quickly different metal rods conduct heat. A student writes "the rods should be the same size." The examiner explicitly refused this. Why, and what should be written?

Show answer

Because "size" is not something you can measure or set. It is a vague impression covering length, diameter, mass and volume all at once, and it gives no evidence that the student knows which of those actually matters.

Both do matter here, and for different reasons. Length determines how far the heat has to travel; diameter determines how much cross-section it can travel through. Change either and the comparison between metals is spoiled.

What scores: "the rods must be the same length" and "the rods must be the same diameter" — and note these count as two separate control variables, so a two-mark question is answered by naming two specific dimensions.

2025's examiner: "I didn't allow 'size' or 'volume'." The same applies to "use the same equipment" and "keep conditions the same". Name the actual quantity.

U6

"The rods weren't all the same length." Why does this score zero on a question asking how the investigation could be improved, even though it correctly identifies the flaw?

Show answer

Because the question asks what could be done, and that sentence describes a problem rather than an action. Identifying the flaw is the reason for the improvement, not the improvement itself.

Rewrite every improvement as an instruction:

✗ "The rods weren't all the same length."
✓ "Use rods of the same length, so that only the material is different."

The pattern that always works: a verb, then the specific thing, then why.

The same trap in a different form: in 2023, "take more readings" scored nothing but "take more data at different voltages" did. The first says do more of the same; the second says what to change as well as what to do.

Before writing any improvement, check your sentence begins with a verb. If it begins with "the" or "there wasn't", it is a complaint and not an answer.

U7

Why does the examiner insist on the words "continuous" and "categoric" when asking you to justify a line graph or a bar chart? What is the underlying idea?

Show answer

Because the choice of graph follows from what kind of data you have, and those two words are the names for the distinction.

Continuous data can take any value in a range — force, time, temperature, length. Between 2.0 N and 3.0 N there is 2.5 N, and 2.51 N, and so on. So the space between two plotted points is meaningful, a line through them means something, and you can read intermediate values off it. That is a line graph.

Categoric data comes in separate named groups — copper, steel, aluminium. There is nothing between copper and steel. A line joining them would imply a halfway material that does not exist, so you use separate bars instead.

Why the vocabulary is the mark: saying "a line graph is better because it shows the trend" is true of any line graph and does not show you understand when it is appropriate. The word "continuous" is the evidence.

2025: the examiner "was holding out specifically for the right vocab: categoric or discrete." 2023: "Very few earned the 'continuous' mark." Two words, worth a mark nearly every year.

U8

An experiment is repeated five times and gives almost identical results every time. Does that make it a good experiment? Explain.

Show answer

No — it makes it reliable, which is not the same as valid.

Reliability is about consistency: do you get the same answer if you do it again? Repeating and averaging improves it.

Validity is about whether you are measuring what you intended at all. It depends on the design — on whether the other variables were controlled so that only one thing changed.

The two are independent, and that is the whole point of keeping the words separate. A badly designed experiment repeated five times produces five consistent answers that are all wrong in the same way. Repeating it a sixth time does not help, because the fault is in the method, not in the scatter.

Worked case: measuring a boiling point five times and getting 96.2, 96.3, 96.2, 96.3, 96.2 °C is extremely reliable — and tells you the thermometer is misreading by about 4 degrees. No amount of repetition would reveal that; only checking the instrument would.

So: to improve reliability, repeat and take a mean. To improve validity, control the other variables.

Stores and transfers — two different lists

Energy is not "types" any more. It sits in stores, and it moves between them by transfers. Questions ask for one or the other and the two lists are not interchangeable.

The eight storesThe four transfers
chemical · kinetic · gravitational · elastic · thermal · magnetic · electrostatic · nuclearmechanically (by a force doing work) · electrically · by heating · by radiation (light and sound)

So a battery-powered drill: the chemical store in the battery decreases, energy is transferred electrically to the motor, and then mechanically from the drill bit to the wood.

The giveaway in the question wording: "state the energy store" wants a word from the left column; "state the method by which energy is transferred" wants one from the right. Answering "heat energy" to either is wrong twice over — the store is thermal, and the transfer is by heating.

Note that stretching something fills the elastic store, and the transfer into it is mechanical, done by whoever pulled it. 2025 asked exactly that about a stretched rubber band, including who supplied the energy — the answer being the person.

Conservation of energy

Spec 4.3 · 2023 Q6(a)

Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one store to another.

Which raises the obvious question: if energy is always conserved, what does "wasted energy" mean? It means energy transferred to a store where it is not useful — almost always the thermal store of the surroundings, spread out so thinly that it cannot be gathered up and used again.

So nothing is ever lost in the sense of ceasing to exist. It is dissipated — scattered into the surroundings. That word is worth using, because it is precise about what actually happened.

Work done, and why it equals energy transferred

Spec 4.11, 4.12 · 2023 Q6(c)

W = F × d
W = work done in joules (J) · F = force in newtons (N) · d = distance moved in the direction of the force, in metres (m)

Work done is equal to the energy transferred. They are the same quantity in joules — "work done" simply describes energy being moved by a force.

The phrase that matters is "in the direction of the force". Carrying a suitcase along a level corridor does no work on the suitcase, however tiring it feels: the force you apply is upwards and the movement is horizontal, so the distance in the direction of the force is zero.

2023 ran this backwards — given the energy transferred and the distance, find the force: F = W ÷ d = 45 ÷ 7.5 = 6.0 N.

Gravitational potential and kinetic energy

Spec 4.13, 4.14

GPE = m × g × h
GPE = gravitational potential energy in joules (J) · m = mass in kilograms (kg) · g = gravitational field strength in N/kg (use 10) · h = height in metres (m)

KE = ½ × m × v²
KE = kinetic energy in joules (J) · m = mass in kilograms (kg) · v = velocity in metres/second (m/s)

The velocity is squared, and the ½ applies to everything. On a calculator, work out v² first, then multiply by the mass, then halve — doing it left to right without brackets is a standard way to get it wrong.

The squaring has a real consequence worth knowing: doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy. That is why braking distance grows so sharply with speed — the brakes must remove four times as much energy, over four times the distance.

Converting between GPE and KE

Spec 4.15 · Mock 1 Q10(d)

Conservation of energy links the two. For something falling with no air resistance, the GPE lost equals the KE gained:

m × g × h = ½ × m × v²

The mass appears on both sides and cancels — which is the physics behind the famous result that, without air resistance, everything falls at the same rate regardless of mass. A feather and a hammer land together on the Moon.

Worked example

A 0.20 kg ball is dropped from 5.0 m. Find its speed on landing, ignoring air resistance.

1. GPE lost: 0.20 × 10 × 5.0 = 10 J

2. This becomes KE: 10 = ½ × 0.20 × v², so v² = 10 ÷ 0.10 = 100

3. Speed: v = 10 m/s

Don't forget the final square root — stopping at v² = 100 and writing 100 m/s is a common slip.

When air resistance is not ignored, some of the GPE is dissipated to the thermal store of the air instead, so the KE gained is less and the ball lands more slowly. In the extreme case of terminal velocity, the speed is constant so the KE does not change at all, and every joule of GPE lost is dissipated to the surrounding air.

Efficiency — and two words that are worth a mark

Spec 4.4 · 2023 Q6(b) · Mock 2 Q1(c)

efficiency = (useful energy output ÷ total energy input) × 100%

Both mark schemes refuse the shortened version. 2023: "useful energy / total energy without reference to output not allowed." Mock 2: "'useful energy ÷ total energy' without the words output and input was explicitly refused by the department."

Write useful energy output and total energy input. It is one mark, every time it is asked, for four extra words.

Efficiency is always less than 100% because some energy is always dissipated, usually as thermal energy through friction. Two useful rearrangements:

  • Useful output = efficiency × total input — 2023 gave 15% and 165 J, so useful = 0.15 × 165 = 25 J
  • Wasted energy = total input − useful output — so 165 − 25 = 140 J

Watch whether the answer is wanted as a decimal or a percentage. 1200 ÷ 4000 = 0.30, which is 30%.

Sankey diagrams — marked strictly

Spec 4.5 · 2023 Q6(b)(iii) · Mock 2 Q1(c)(iii) — the most detailed examiner complaint in the pack

A Sankey diagram shows energy flowing through a device, with the width of each arrow proportional to the amount of energy. Input enters from the left, useful output continues straight on, and waste branches away.

The five things being marked

  1. An input arrow. No input arrow means no mark at all — the single most common failure.
  2. Joined up. One connected diagram, not separate floating arrows.
  3. Recognisably arrows, with arrowheads.
  4. Every arrow labelled with its store and its energy value — "chemical, 4000 J", "kinetic, 1200 J", "thermal, 2800 J".
  5. Widths to scale, and state the scale. If waste is 2800 J and useful is 1200 J, the waste arrow must be visibly wider — a bit over twice.

The 2023 report on this one question:

"Needed to have joined up diagram (eg no mark if no input line), needs to be easily identifiable as arrows… 'transferred to surroundings' not accepted, needed reference to thermal/heat. Lots of students only marked output arrows and not input arrow so lost marks… scale marked strictly. Many students used same width for input energy and wasted energy."

Three specific things to do: draw the input arrow first; label waste as thermal or heat, never "transferred to the surroundings"; and check the widths against each other before moving on. The input arrow must be the widest — it equals the two outputs added together.

Power

Spec 4.16, 4.17 · 2024 Q1(b)

P = W ÷ t
P = power in watts (W) · W = work done or energy transferred in joules (J) · t = time in seconds (s)

Power is the rate of transfer of energy, or the rate of doing work. One watt is one joule per second. It is not how much energy — it is how fast.

So two cranes lifting the same load to the same height do the same work, but the faster one has the greater power.

2024 gave three marks purely for unit handling: recognising that power is a rate, that 2.2 kW = 2200 W, and that 1 hour = 3600 s. Free marks, and only free if the conversions are automatic.

GPE, KE and the standard conversion

When an object falls, GPE converts to KE. If air resistance is negligible, the KE gained equals the GPE lost:

m × g × h = ½ × m × v²m = mass (kg) · g = gravitational field strength (N/kg) · h = height fallen (m) · v = speed at the bottom (m/s) — the mass cancels, so the speed does not depend on it

Mass cancels, which is why all objects fall at the same rate in a vacuum. Rearranged: v = √(2gh).

If air resistance is not negligible, KE at the bottom is less than GPE at the top, and the difference has been dissipated to the thermal store of the surroundings by friction.

04Understanding it

U1

A car brakes to a stop. Its kinetic energy was 200 kJ. Where did it go, and does this violate conservation of energy?

Show answer

Nothing is violated. The 200 kJ was transferred to the thermal store of the brakes, tyres, road and surrounding air, by friction — a mechanical transfer that ends up heating things. The brake discs get genuinely hot; on a long descent they can glow.

The energy still exists. What's changed is that it is now dissipated — spread thinly across a huge number of particles in the surroundings, at a temperature barely above ambient. You cannot practically gather it back up and use it to move the car again.

Why this matters for the exam: "energy is lost" invites the follow-up "lost where?". Say dissipated to the thermal store of the surroundings and you've answered both.

Regenerative braking in electric cars exists precisely to intercept some of this energy before it dissipates, transferring it to a chemical store in the battery instead.

U2

Doubling a car's speed doubles its momentum but quadruples its kinetic energy. Using the KE equation, explain why — and connect it to braking distance.

Show answer

KE = ½mv². Velocity is squared, so doubling v multiplies KE by 2² = 4. At 20 m/s a car has four times the kinetic energy it had at 10 m/s.

The braking connection: to stop, all that kinetic energy must be transferred away by the braking force doing work. Work done = force × distance. If the braking force is roughly constant (limited by tyre grip), then four times the energy requires four times the distance.

This is the same result you get from v² = u² + 2as in the forces module — arrived at from energy instead. Both routes give braking distance ∝ speed².

Being able to reach the same answer two ways is a good check under exam pressure.

U3

A car engine is about 25% efficient. A kettle is about 95%. Why can't we simply build a better engine and reach 95% too?

Show answer

Because the two are asking for different things.

A kettle's useful output is thermal energy — heating water. Nearly all the waste is also heat, and much of that ends up in the water anyway. It's easy to be efficient when the thing you want is the thing everything degrades into.

A car engine's useful output is kinetic energy, and it gets there by burning fuel to make heat first. Converting heat into motion is fundamentally limited: a large fraction must be rejected as waste heat through the exhaust and radiator. No amount of engineering removes that.

The general principle: energy tends to spread out into thermal stores. Devices whose useful output is thermal can be highly efficient. Devices trying to convert thermal energy into anything else are always poor.

An electric motor sidesteps this — around 90% efficient — because it goes from electrical to kinetic without a heat stage. That's a large part of why electric cars are more efficient than petrol ones.

U4

You carry a heavy suitcase 100 m along a level corridor at constant speed. In physics terms, how much work have you done on the suitcase? Does that match how it feels?

Show answer

Zero. Work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force. The force you exert on the suitcase is upwards, supporting its weight. The motion is horizontal. There is no movement in the direction of the force, so no work is done on the suitcase.

Consistent with energy: the suitcase's stores are unchanged. Same height, so same GPE. Constant speed, so same KE.

Why it feels exhausting: your muscles do work internally maintaining tension, which is a real biological cost, and you do work against friction and air resistance moving your own body. But none of that is work done on the suitcase.

Exam relevance: W = F × d always means the distance moved in the direction of the force. If a question gives a force at an angle to the motion, that qualifier is doing work.

U5

Two mark schemes explicitly refuse "efficiency = useful energy ÷ total energy". The formula is arithmetically identical to the accepted one. Why is it rejected?

Show answer

Because without the words output and input, the statement is ambiguous about which energies are being compared.

A device has energy going in and energy coming out. "Useful energy" and "total energy" do not say which side of the device each one is on — and the whole idea of efficiency is the comparison across the device: what you got out that was useful, against what you put in altogether.

What scores: efficiency = (useful energy output ÷ total energy input) × 100

2023: "useful energy / total energy without reference to output not allowed." Mock 2: "without the words output and input was explicitly refused by the department."

This is worth treating as a free mark you must not drop. It is asked almost every year, it is one line, and the only difference between full marks and zero is four words.

U6

If energy cannot be created or destroyed, what does it mean to say a device "wastes" energy? And why can't the wasted energy simply be collected and reused?

Show answer

Wasted energy is energy transferred to a store where it is not useful — almost always the thermal store of the surroundings. Not a single joule has been destroyed; it has just ended up somewhere useless.

Why it cannot be recovered: it has been dissipated — spread out thinly across a huge amount of surrounding air, machinery and ground. A car engine's wasted energy raises the temperature of the whole street by an unmeasurably small amount. There is no way to gather it back up, because it is no longer concentrated anywhere.

That is the real distinction: not between energy that exists and energy that doesn't, but between energy that is concentrated and useful, and energy that is spread out and useless.

Use the word "dissipated" rather than "lost". Mark schemes accept it and it describes what actually happened. Saying energy is "lost" invites the follow-up question about conservation.

U7

A skydiver at terminal velocity falls 40 m at a constant speed. Her gravitational potential energy is clearly decreasing. Where is it going, given that her kinetic energy is not changing?

Show answer

All of it is dissipated to the thermal store of the surrounding air, by the work done against air resistance.

The reasoning in three steps:

  1. She is losing height, so her GPE decreases.
  2. Her speed is constant, so her KE is unchanged — this is the step people miss, because the usual falling-object story has GPE turning into KE.
  3. Energy is conserved, so the GPE lost must have gone somewhere else. The only thing acting is air resistance, so it has been transferred to the thermal store of the air (and slightly to her suit).

Why terminal velocity is the clean case: during the earlier part of the fall she was speeding up, so the GPE lost was split — some into KE, some dissipated. Once at terminal velocity the split stops and every joule of GPE lost goes to the air.

Note this is an energy question. The companion question about how she reaches terminal velocity is a forces question, and answers to one score nothing on the other.

U8

In the equation for gravitational potential energy turning into kinetic energy, the mass cancels from both sides. What does that tell you physically, and why does the real world not seem to agree?

Show answer

Setting GPE lost equal to KE gained gives m × g × h = ½ × m × v². The mass appears on both sides and cancels, leaving g × h = ½v².

Physically: the speed an object reaches after falling a given height does not depend on its mass at all. A cannonball and a marble dropped from the same window would land at the same instant, at the same speed.

Why the world seems to disagree: because of air resistance, which the equation ignores. A feather has a large surface area for its tiny weight, so air resistance balances its weight almost immediately and it reaches a very low terminal velocity. A cannonball's weight is enormous compared with the drag on it, so it barely notices.

The difference we observe is therefore not about mass affecting gravity — it is about how quickly air resistance becomes significant compared with the object's weight.

The confirming experiment: the same objects dropped in a vacuum tube, or on the Moon, land together. Apollo 15's commander did exactly that with a hammer and a feather.

Describe the energy transfers involved in generating electricity using (a) a coal-fired power station and (b) a wind turbine. (5)

Show answer

(a) The chemical store in the coal decreases as it is burned, transferring energy to the thermal store 1

This heats water to make steam, which turns a turbine — a transfer to the kinetic store 1

The turbine drives a generator, transferring energy electrically 1

(b) The kinetic store of the moving air is transferred to the kinetic store of the turbine blades 1

which drive a generator, transferring energy electrically 1

Most methods share the same middle: something spins a turbine, which drives a generator. Only solar cells skip it, going from radiation straight to electrical.

D14

Give two advantages and two disadvantages of generating electricity from wind rather than from fossil fuels. (4)

Show answer

Advantages, any two 11: wind is renewable and will not run out · no carbon dioxide is produced while generating, so it does not contribute to climate change · no sulfur dioxide, so no acid rain · no fuel cost

Disadvantages, any two 11: unreliable, since it only generates when the wind blows · high initial set-up cost · visual impact on the landscape and noise · a large number of turbines is needed to match one fossil-fuel station

The distinction to carry into any question of this type: renewables will not run out but are unreliable; non-renewables are reliable but will run out and cause pollution.

D15

A student carries a 12 kg suitcase 60 m along a level corridor at a constant speed, then lifts it 1.5 m onto a rack. Take g = 10 N/kg.

(a) Calculate the work done on the suitcase while carrying it along the corridor. (2)
(b) Calculate the work done lifting it onto the rack. (3)

Show answer

(a) Zero 1

Work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force. The supporting force is vertical while the motion is horizontal, so the distance in the direction of the force is zero 1

(b) The force needed equals the weight: W = m × g = 12 × 10 = 120 N 1

Note that (b) is the same calculation as GPE = mgh: 12 × 10 × 1.5 = 180 J. Work done against gravity and gain in gravitational potential energy are the same quantity.
